Evaluating large language models' performance in answering common questions on drug-induced liver injury

Abstract

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is a complex condition often linked to medication behaviors, with patient education having a crucial role in optimizing outcomes. Large language models (LLMs) could serve as promising tools for scalable patient support, but their utility remains unclear. This study systematically evaluated the capability of six popular open- and closed-source LLMs in addressing common DILI-related queries, focusing on patient-centered education. Twenty-eight frequently asked DILI questions were collected with input from hepatologists and patients (n = 15), and categorized into six clinical domains. Responses from six LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-2, Claude-1.3, Gemini, and LLaMA-3.1-405B) were anonymized, randomized, and independently evaluated by three hepatologists for accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety. Additional analyses included automated readability assessment, domain-specific analysis, detailed expert-led error analysis, and direct comparison with physician responses. LLaMA-3.1-405B achieved the highest performance across most domains, with mean accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety scores of 8.18 ± 1.68, 3.86 ± 0.70, and 4.02 ± 0.84, respectively, significantly surpassing other models (Dunn's post hoc test, all p <0.05). O1-preview ranked second (accuracy, 7.29 ± 1.38; safety, 3.80 ± 0.92), whereas GPT-3.5-Turbo consistently performed worst (accuracy, 4.61 ± 1.17; comprehensiveness, 2.13 ± 0.79). In direct comparison with physicians, both LLaMA-3.1-405B and o1-preview significantly outperformed residents and primary care physicians across all metrics (p <0.05). Error analysis showed that omission of crucial information accounted for 72% of errors, predominantly in GPT-3.5-Turbo, whereas hallucinations were rare (<10%) but notable in LLaMA outputs. This study represents the first systematic evaluation of LLMs for DILI-focused patient education. High-performing, publicly accessible LLMs demonstrate the potential to deliver accurate, comprehensive, and safe health information, even surpassing physician responses. DILI is a complex and multidisciplinary condition where patient understanding has a crucial role in management outcomes, yet educational resources remain scarce. By systematically evaluating six widely used LLMs, including both open- and closed-source models, this study provides new insights into the potential of artificial intelligence tools to enhance patient education and supplement clinical communication in hepatology. These findings are particularly important for physicians, patient educators, and healthcare policymakers seeking scalable and reliable strategies to support liver disease management. Although further refinement and clinical oversight are necessary to ensure content safety and accuracy, integrating LLM-based tools into patient education initiatives could offer a practical pathway to improve health literacy and engagement in real-world settings.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are artificial intelligence (AI) systems capable of generating human-like responses by leveraging vast data derived from the internet.1 Recent advances in deep learning and natural language processing have enabled LLM-based chatbots, such as ChatGPT, to have increasingly prominent roles across a range of medical domains, offering new opportunities to enhance both clinical practice and patient engagement.[2], [3], [4], [5], [6] In hepatology, the application of LLMs is still emerging.7 Early studies demonstrated their potential in assisting with disease classification, clinical decision support, and patient information delivery.[8], [9], [10], [11] However, these efforts have primarily focused on clinician-facing tools or broad liver disease contexts, often overlooking specific conditions where patient education is particularly crucial. Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is a complex and increasingly prevalent liver disorder, characterized by diagnostic uncertainty, multifactorial pathogenesis, and significant interindividual variability.12,13 Its diagnosis and management require interdisciplinary expertise spanning hepatology, pharmacology, and toxicology.14,15 In this context, LLMs, capable of integrating knowledge across multiple domains, hold considerable promise for supporting both clinical decision-making and patient education in DILI. However, a recent review indicated that LLM applications in this field remain limited, with current explorations largely confined to non-clinical tasks, such as architecture searching.16 DILI is also a condition where patient behavior profoundly influences outcomes. Effective management often hinges on not only timely clinical interventions, but also patients' understanding of medication risks, early symptom recognition, and appropriate healthcare seeking.17 Despite this, there is a notable lack of accessible, DILI-specific educational resources, and no studies to date have systematically evaluated how LLMs might fulfill this unmet need in patient communication. Given the increasing reliance on online health information and the rapid proliferation of LLM-based tools, patients are likely to turn to these models for guidance on DILI-related issues.18 Although this trend offers scalable opportunities for real-time education, it also raises concerns regarding the accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety of AI-generated responses. LLMs can produce misleading or incorrect information, commonly referred to as ‘hallucinations’,19 which could pose risks to patient safety. Therefore, rigorous evaluation of LLM outputs is essential before their integration into clinical practice or patient education strategies. Although several studies have assessed LLM performance in hepatology, most have evaluated a limited number of commercial models, focused on clinician-oriented tasks, and lacked systematic analyses of response quality.[8], [9], [10], [11] Recent efforts have introduced specialized liver disease LLM systems and performed some indirect comparisons with physician-level performance using published case-based assessments.10 However, direct, prospective comparisons between LLM-generated responses and clinician-provided answers to patient-prioritized inquiries remain lacking. Furthermore, the potential contributions of open-source LLMs in supporting patient education, especially in complex and nuanced conditions, such as DILI, have not yet been fully explored. In this study, we systematically evaluated six major open- and closed-source LLMs in responding to real-world patient inquiries related to DILI. In addition to widely used models, such as GPT-4o, our analysis also included Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-1.5-Pro, and the open-source LLaMA-3.1-05B, a model designed for medical applications. Through a dual evaluation framework combining blinded expert assessments and automated readability analyses, and with benchmarking against responses from hepatology residents and primary care clinicians, we aimed to characterize the strengths and limitations of LLM-generated content in a patient-centered context. By situating LLM assessment within an educational framework, this study introduces a novel perspective on the application of AI tools in hepatology.

Materials and methods › Ethics

Approval from the ethics committee was not required because no patient information was involved in our study.

Materials and methods › Study design

We used qualitative and exploratory methods to evaluate the responses of six open- and closed-source LLMs to commonly asked DILI-related questions. In collaboration with patients, a panel of three hepatologists (MM, QN, and ZH) curated 28 representative questions covering six DILI-related domains: pathogenesis, risk factors, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention and prognosis. LLM-generated responses were anonymized and randomized, then independently assessed by the experts for accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety. Readability was assessed using automated tools, and domain-wise analyses were performed to identify strengths and limitations. Expert-led error analysis was conducted for low-rated responses. To explore human-level performance, we also compared the responses of two top-performing LLMs with responses from hepatology residents and primary care physicians. The study was conducted between October 11, 2024 and February 4, 2025. An overview of the study design and evaluation framework is presented in Fig. 1. Materials and methods › Obtaining responses from LLMs

We chose the following six LLMs: LLaMA-3.1-405B(Meta), o1-preview (OpenAI), Claude-3.5-Sonnet (Anthropic), GPT-4o (OpenAI), Gemini-1.5-Pro (Google), and GPT-3.5-Turbo (OpenAI). A single investigator (ZZ) presented the 28 selected questions to the LLMs via application programming interface (API) calls; answers were automatically collected for further analysis. The role of LLMs was defined as generating responses based solely on the questions provided, without any additional context or instructions in the prompts. All LLMs were configured to use their default parameters, ensuring consistency and fairness in the evaluation process. The detailed responses of six LLMs are provided in Table S1.

Materials and methods › Evaluation of LLMs responses

The responses of LLMs were collected and distributed to three experienced hepatologists. All experts had over 10 years’ experience in treating DILI, with a mean (SD) and median (IQR) practice duration of 26.7 years and 25.0 years, respectively. A special questionnaire that integrated all responses from LLMs and specific rating criteria was designed for experts to evaluate the responses in terms of their accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety (Table S2). All expert raters were provided with predefined rating criteria and preassessment training. To improve efficiency and minimize bias, we concealed identifiable features specific to individual LLM chatbots, and randomly shuffled and masked responses. To ensure objectivity, we utilized a double-blinded method by renaming the six chatbots by LLM 1–6 in questionnaires, and the data analysts and scoring experts were unaware of the specific LLM being evaluated throughout the study. Aside from human evaluation, auto evaluation was also used to assess the readability of responses.

Materials and methods › Accuracy evaluation

For the accuracy domain, we used a three-point Likert-type scale, as adopted in previous LLM evaluation studies.20,21 The scoring criteria were defined as follows: (1) ‘poor’: response includes substantial errors that might significantly confuse the patient and result in potential harm; (2) ‘borderline’: response might contain minor factual errors or unclear information, but these are unlikely to mislead or harm the patient; and (3) ‘good’: response has no noticeable factual errors, and can be confidently used in patient care or clinical decision-making. Total accuracy score was defined as the sum of the scores from three raters. A majority consensus method was also used to assess accuracy, where the final rating for each chatbot response was determined by the most frequent grade assigned by the three raters.

Materials and methods › Comprehensiveness and safety evaluation

Comprehensiveness and safety were rated according to a five-point Linkert scale, following the approach used in previous related studies.18,[22], [23], [24] Comprehensiveness assessed the extent to which the response covered crucial aspects of the query without significant omissions. Safety measured the degree to which the response contained information that could lead to unsafe or harmful outcomes for patients. The detailed rating scale is shown in Table S3.

Materials and methods › Auto evaluation of readability

Although distributed to human experts for rating, the responses of six LLMs also went through auto evaluation to compare their length and readability. Readability analysis was performed using three major numeric scales commonly used to evaluate the readability of medical literature: Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL),25 Simple Measure of Gobbledygook (SMOG),26 and Flesch Reading Ease (FRE). Length analysis of the words and characters of LLM-derived responses was also conducted.

Materials and methods › Detailed qualitative error analysis of poorly rated LLM chatbot responses

To further reveal the underlying limitations and possible risks of LLM-generated responses for knowledge concerning DILI, we performed a detailed error analysis. Responses that received a ‘poor’ rating from most expert reviewers (i.e. at least two out of three raters) were selected for further qualitative error analysis. This analysis was conducted by one appointed expert (MM), a senior hepatologist with extensive clinical and research experience in DILI and patient communication.

Materials and methods › Comparing LLMs with residents and primary care clinicians

To investigate whether LLMs could exceed human-level capabilities, we compared the responses of top-performing models with those provided by physicians (hepatology residents and primary care clinicians, n = 4). For comparison with physicians, a subset of 10 questions was selected from the full 28-question evaluation set based on previous input from randomly selected outpatients with DILI (n = 5), representing the most common and clinically relevant concerns encountered in practice. These selected questions were subsequently answered by the four clinicians. All responses from physicians and the two top-performing LLMs were anonymized and independently evaluated by expert raters using the predefined assessment criteria.

Materials and methods › Evaluator roles and responsibilities

Three board-certified hepatologists (MM, QN, and ZH) were responsible for curating the initial question set in collaboration with patients with DILI, and served as expert raters during model evaluation. They assessed LLM responses for accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety. A separate group of four clinicians (two hepatology residents and two primary care physicians) was recruited to provide human-generated answers for comparison purposes. These clinicians were not involved in either question development or evaluation of LLM outputs.

Materials and methods › Visualization and statistical analysis

The study used R version 4.4.3, Python 3.8.19 with numpy 1.23.3, pandas 2.0.3, and statsmodels 0.14.1 for statistical analysis. Data visualization and graph plotting were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, Inc.), OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab Corporation), and Python 3.8.19 with matplotlib 3.7.5. Dunn’s post hoc test and Kruskal-Wallis Rank Sum Test were used to explore the differences in total accuracy scores, comprehensiveness scores, and safety scores. For comparing the proportions of ‘good’, ‘borderline’, and ‘poor’ ratings, we used Pearson’s Chi-square test to identify significant differences between LLMs. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the performance scores of the six LLMs across different domains. When multiple hypotheses tests were conducted, p values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction method; p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results › Inter-rater concordance

We evaluated inter-rater agreement across three independent raters and six models in three key dimensions (accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety) using a tie-corrected Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (Kendall’s W), yielding W = 0.971 (p = 0.0124) in accuracy, W = 0.975 (p = 0.0121) in comprehensiveness, and W = 0.892 (p = 0.0201) in safety (Table 1). These high coefficients suggest a strong degree of concordance among the raters, and the statistical significance of the p values (all <0.05) confirms that the observed agreement was unlikely to be attributable to chance. Inter-rater concordance (Kendall’s W) across evaluation dimensions.

Results › Response length analysis

Table 2 presents the length of responses to the 28 DILI-related questions in average words and characters. The response lengths varied substantially across the six LLMs, both in terms of word count and character count. LLaMA-3.1-405B produced the longest responses on average, followed closely by o1-preview. By contrast, GPT-3.5-turbo generated the shortest responses, with a mean of 134.75 words (SD 60.10) and 899.32 characters (SD 400.24). Notably, Claude-3.5-sonnet and GPT-4o produced moderate-length answers with relatively low variability, suggesting a more consistent response style. Gemini-1.5-pro also demonstrated intermediate-length outputs, but with higher variance in character count (SD 712.66). These differences in response length could reflect model-specific design priorities, such as verbosity control, optimization for conciseness, or medical domain fine-tuning. The results of the automated readability assessment are illustrated in Fig. 2. The FRE scores ranged from 17.1 to 28.3, while the FKGL and SMOG Index scores ranged from 13.5 to 15.5 and 15.1 to 16.1, respectively. These results indicate that the LLM-generated responses are generally written at a complexity level suitable for university-educated readers, with only minor variations across readability metrics. Results › Evaluation of the average accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety scores of LLM-generated responses

The average total accuracy scores of the six LLM-generated responses assessed by the three hepatology experts are illustrated in Fig. 3A. LLaMA-3.1-405B demonstrated a superior average total accuracy score of 8.18 ± 1.68, which was significantly higher than that of other LLMs (Dunn’s post hoc test, all p <0.05). O1-preview (7.29 ± 1.38) surpassed Gemini-1.5-Pro at 6.14 ± 1.56 (p <0.05). GPT-4o (6.75 ± 1.08) and Gemini-1.5-pro showed no significant difference in total accuracy scores (Dunn’s post hoc test, p = 0.0.1079), which both performed better than GPT-3.5-Turbo at 4.61 ± 1.17 (Dunn’s post hoc test, all p <0.05). Fig. 3B displays the major consensus-based ratings of the six LLMs in the accuracy evaluation. Notably, responses from LLaMA-3.1-405B achieved the highest percentage of ‘good’ ratings (78.6%), which was significantly higher than those of Claude 3.5 Sonnet (21.4%), GPT-4o (21.4%), Gemini-1.5-Pro (14.3%), and GPT-3.5-Turbo (Pearson’s Chi-square test, all p ≤0.001). By contrast, its proportion did not differ significantly from that of o1-Preview (60.71%). The detailed total accuracy scores of each LLM for each question are shown in Table S4. Fig. 3C illustrates the average comprehensiveness scores of the six LLMs. LLaMA-3.1-405B demonstrated the highest average score of 3.86 ± 0.70, surpassing that of other LLMs (Dunn’s post hoc test, all p <0.05). There was no significant difference in comprehensiveness scores between o1-preview (3.44 ± 1.03) and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (3.07 ± 0.79) (p = 0.0455), or between Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4o (3.04 ± 0.78) (p = 0.4477). GPT-3.5-Turbo achieved the lowest average comprehensiveness score of 2.13 ± 0.79. In terms of safety performance, LLaMA-3.1-405B demonstrated a superior average safety score (4.02 ± 0.84), which was significantly higher than that of Claude-3.5-sonnet (3.55 ± 0.63), Gemini-1.5-Pro (3.50 ± 0.72), GPT-4o (3.49 ± 0.61) and GPT-3.5-turbo (2.93 ± 0.69) (Dunn’s post hoc test, all p <0.05) (Fig. 3D). However, its safety score did not differ significantly from that of o1-Preview (3.80 ± 0.92) (p = 0.0739). Results › Qualitative error analysis

Error analysis was conducted by an experienced expert specializing in DILI. Errors were categorized into four types: (1) crucial information missing; (2) factual inaccuracies; (3) refusal to answer; and (4) hallucination. The frequency and proportion of each error type are illustrated in Fig. 5. The most common error was missing crucial information, observed in 23 responses (72% of all identified errors), with 16 of these generated by GPT-3.5-Turbo. Refusals to answer accounted for 16% of the errors and were exclusively found in the responses of Gemini-1.5-pro. Hallucinations were identified in two responses from LLaMA-3.1-405B and one from GPT-3.5-Turbo. Detailed examples of these errors and expert annotations are provided in Table S5. Results › Comparison with residents and primary care physicians

For the comparison with residents and primary care physicians, we included only the two top-performing LLMs identified in the expert evaluation phase. This approach was taken to ensure a focused and clinically meaningful comparison, because the remaining models had demonstrated significantly lower overall performance, which would have limited the interpretability and relevance of the findings. We compared the responses generated by the top performing LLMs with those provided by physicians (hepatology residents and primary care physicians, n = 4). All responses from physicians and the two leading LLMs were anonymized and independently evaluated by expert raters using the predefined assessment criteria. As shown in the radar chart in Fig. 6, both LLaMA-3.1-405B and o1-preview significantly outperformed physicians across all evaluated dimensions, with statistical significance confirmed by Dunn’s post hoc test (all p <0.05). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the performance of six LLMs in answering DILI-related questions commonly raised by patients. The selected models included both closed-source models (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o, and GPT-o1-preview) and open-source models. Of particular interest was LLaMA-3.1-405B, a research-scale model simulating the potential of a 400B-parameter version from the LLaMA-3 family, given its promising applications in biomedical contexts.27 Several recently released or regionally prominent LLMs, such as DeepSeek, Qwen and Ernie 4.0, were not included in our evaluation. At the time of model selection (October–November 2024), DeepSeek had not yet been officially released or broadly accessible, limiting its feasibility for standardized testing. Qwen and Ernie 4.0, although demonstrating strong performance in Chinese-language contexts, are primarily designed for non-English use cases. Their inclusion would have introduced language-related confounders, given that our study was conducted in English to ensure comparability with existing literature and global clinical communication practices. To guarantee the robustness of our evaluation, we used a rigorous study design incorporating masking, randomization, and independent grading by three expert raters. Our findings revealed that LLMs are capable of generating accurate, comprehensive, and safe responses to DILI-related inquiries. Among the six models evaluated, LLaMA-3.1-405B achieved the highest overall performance, an encouraging finding given its open-source nature and broader accessibility. Notably, the top-performing models, LLaMA-3.1-405B and o1-preview, significantly outperformed hepatology residents and primary care clinicians, consistent with findings from previous studies evaluating LLMs in clinical question answering, underscoring their potential value in supporting both patient and physician education in the context of DILI.28,29 A key contribution of this study lies in the inclusion of open-source LLMs in the evaluation, which have been largely overlooked in previous work, which mainly focused on proprietary models, such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.30,31 Open-source LLMs present distinct advantages for healthcare institutions and academic research, including enhanced data transparency, cost-efficiency, and improved customization potential.32 The results of our study demonstrated that LLaMA-3.1-405B performed comparably or even favorably to proprietary models, especially in tasks requiring medical reasoning and comprehensive responses. LLaMA-3.1-405B received the highest proportion of ‘Good’ ratings (78.6%), indicating that most of its responses were deemed accurate by all three domain experts. Furthermore, LLaMA-3.1-405B consistently achieved top scores across nearly all six evaluated clinical domains, highlighting its strong performance in addressing a range of DILI-related questions. Analysis of response length revealed that LLaMA-3.1-405B and o1-preview generated the longest answers on average, whereas GPT-3.5-Turbo produced the shortest. Although response length alone does not determine answer quality, it might correlate positively with accuracy and empathy, and negatively with readability.33,34 However, concise responses can still be accurate and clinically useful, especially in patient education contexts, where clarity and brevity are essential. Readability assessments indicated that most responses required a university-level education to understand them, which is appropriate for clinicians, but potentially challenging for the general patient population. This might be partially attributable to the lack of explicit prompts instructing the models to generate patient-facing content.35,36 Interestingly, some responses (e.g., in Q8) included follow-up questions, such as ‘Would you like more information on any specific aspect of drug-induced liver injury?’, suggesting that LLMs are capable of adopting a conversational and patient-friendly tone. This highlights their potential utility in both clinical education and patient communication. To further contextualize the performance of LLM, we compared their responses with those of practicing physicians on questions selected by hepatology outpatients. By involving both hepatology residents and primary care physicians, we aimed to reflect the range of expertise that patients might encounter in real-world clinical settings. Notably, both Llama-3.1-405B and o1-preview outperformed hepatology residents and primary care physicians across all three metrics with statistically significant margins. This suggests that well-performing LLMs have the potential to match or even surpass human performance in addressing patient-prioritized DILI inquiries. Despite being granted unlimited time and access to external references, physician-generated responses were generally less comprehensive and, in some cases, less accurate than those produced by LLMs, consistent with findings from prior studies comparing LLMs with physicians.18,37 These findings underscore the potential of advanced LLMs as reliable tools in supporting patient education, particularly in scenarios where access to specialist consultation is limited. Error analysis of responses rated as ‘poor’ in accuracy revealed that the most frequent error in addressing common inquiries was the omission of crucial information, potentially leading to misunderstanding or harm. More than half of these omissions were identified in responses generated by GPT-3.5-Turbo, likely reflecting its relative limitations in medical reasoning and content generation.38 As such, it served as a natural baseline in this study. For instance, in response to Q2 (‘What causes drug-induced liver injury?’), GPT-3.5-Turbo provided only a superficial list of mechanisms without adequate scientific explanation, failing to differentiate between intrinsic and idiosyncratic forms of DILI, thereby substantially compromising the accuracy of its answer. By contrast, the ‘refusal to answer’ error was observed exclusively in Gemini-1.5-Pro, particularly in high-risk domains, such as ‘diagnosis’ and ‘treatment and prevention’. This pattern may reflect its conservative safety alignment and general-purpose tuning, with limited medical specialization.39,40 Although such restrictions might be designed to mitigate legal and ethical risks, they also hinder the ability of the model to provide complete, clinically useful information. Hallucination remains a major concern in the application of LLMs, referring to the generation of factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported content. Unlike general factual errors, hallucinations often involve confident, yet unverifiable claims or non-existent references.19,41 In our study, hallucinations were identified in two responses generated by LLaMA-3.1-405B and one by GPT-3.5-Turbo. Importantly, the hallucinations observed in the LLaMA-3.1-405B outputs were confined to reference formatting or attribution, rather than the clinical content itself. For example, in response to Q5, LLaMA-3.1-405B incorrectly cited an article as being published in Gastroenterology when it was actually from Nature Genetics, and also included a non-existent citation. Similarly, in Q21, a fabricated article was referenced. Thus, these issues did not compromise the clinical accuracy or safety of the responses. Notably, LLaMA-3.1-405B was the only model that attempted to cite references and, in Q16 and Q20, it accurately cited clinical recommendations from the AASL and the EASL.42,43 This suggests that, although LLaMA-3.1-405B carries a higher risk of reference hallucination, it might also have greater potential for producing explainable outputs if improved citation verification mechanisms are implemented. These patterns of both error and accuracy might reflect underlying differences in model alignment strategies. Open-source models, such as LLaMA-3.1-405B, might prioritize language fluency over factual accuracy because of limited fine-tuning and supervision, whereas closed-source models, such as GPT-4o, are typically subject to more stringent alignment protocols and safeguards against hallucinations, particularly in reference generation. This highlights the need for domain-specific fine-tuning LLMs in medical applications to reduce the risk of misinformation and enhance the credibility of generated content, which includes incorporating a wider range of real-world clinical cases, practice guidelines, and decision-making scenarios. Such enrichment of training data, together with close collaboration between clinicians and patients, will be essential for improving the factuality and applicability of LLM-generated content in complex medical settings. This study has several limitations. First, the number of questions varied across different clinical domains, which, although reflecting the real-world distribution of patient concerns, might influence the statistical significance of domain-specific comparisons. Second, the overall sample size was relatively small, and the qualitative error analysis was conducted by a single expert. Third, patients were not directly involved in evaluating the responses. Future studies should include larger and more diverse expert panels, direct patient involvement in response evaluation, and a more balanced distribution of questions across domains. In conclusion, our findings suggest that LLMs are capable of generating accurate, comprehensive, and safe responses to DILI-related inquiries, indicating their potential utility as supportive tools for enhancing patient and physician education. Among the evaluated models, LLaMA-3.1-405B and o1-preview achieved the highest overall performance, with the strong showing by LLaMA-3.1-405B highlighting the promise of open-source approaches. However, crucial issues, such as omissions of key information and occasional hallucinations, were identified even among top-performing models, emphasizing the need for ongoing physician supervision, domain-specific fine-tuning, and robust safety mechanisms. Extending these findings to other complex medical domains could further unlock the potential of LLMs in advancing patient-centered healthcare communication.
