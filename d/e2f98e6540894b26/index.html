Abstract

ABSTRACTModel‐informed drug development (MIDD) has grown from a constellation of methodological advances to a profound reorientation of how evidence is generated, evaluated, integrated, and leveraged in both drug development and regulatory decision‐making. Over decades, pharmaco‐statistical modeling and simulation (M&S) have moved from supplemental analyses toward shared frameworks for quantitative and mechanistic reasoning on questions as diverse as clinical trial planning, effectiveness and drug safety risk assessment, and therapeutic optimization. This perspective traces that evolution and argues that MIDD's translational promise lies not only in ever‐increasing technical sophistication, but in its capacity to reshape how we talk about how medicines are developed, evaluated, and optimized for patients.

MIDD: An Abbreviated History

In contemporary drug development, we take for granted that pharmacometric and other empirical or mechanistically‐anchored M&S approaches are routinely used to inform critical decisions. This routine application, however, has not always been the case. In 1991, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), American Association of Pharmaceutical Scientists, and American Society for Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics held a workshop whose specific aims are humbling to read now: (1) To identify the roles and interrelationships between PK, PD, and toxicokinetics in drug development; (2) To develop and grow strategies for incorporation of these principles in various phases of drug development; and (3) To lay the groundwork for the development of future regulatory guidelines to the pharmaceutical industry [1]. The importance of this conference and resultant report in what would become the trajectory of MIDD cannot be overstated. While it is true that PK science as a discipline and the conceptual origins of PK/PD modeling in clinical pharmacology can be traced back to decades earlier [2, 3, 4, 5], the early 90s marks an inflection point from sporadic, explanatory application of PK principles in drug development to the articulation of PK/PD as an integrative, strategic framework. This shift was aided by several converging factors including, but not limited to, pronouncement of PK/PD as a unified inferential framework by luminaries like Peck, Sheiner, Beal, and others, the increasing visibility and adoption of population approaches (e.g., NONMEM), the formation of pharmacometrics groups within the pharmaceutical industry, and early regulatory openness. Conceptual scaffolding that made clear the application space and potential impact of MIDD would be required for its promise to be understood, let alone for widespread adoption. Perhaps the most canonical of these is Sheiner's reformulation of George Box's “Learn‐Confirm” paradigm in 1997 [6]. In that formative paper, Sheiner contemplates clinical drug development as an iterative series of exercises through which we (1) get an early sense for a drug's activity at a tolerable dose; and (2) confirm a drug's benefit/risk profile in a representative patient population. Importantly, Sheiner foregrounds a “reorientation” of both thinking and process and audaciously centers the clinical pharmacologist as the bridge between what he calls the “science” of learning (i.e., clinical pharmacology principles, including quantitative M&S) and the “empiricism” of confirmation. Sheiner's Learn‐Confirm paradigm emerged against a backdrop in which the now well‐known woes of pharmaceutical R&D were coming into sharp focus: development timelines were increasing, attrition rates were high, and late‐stage failures were becoming more visible and costly. At the same time, clinical development was increasing in scale and complexity, and regulatory expectations for evidentiary rigor continued to develop. Within this environment, the traditional linear, compartmentalized approach to drug development and decision‐making was becoming increasingly untenable. The Learn‐Confirm paradigm reshaped how drug development and regulatory evaluation of investigational drugs conceptualized evidence generation, uncertainty, and the very notion of progression. This approach gave a new kind of language to an increasingly recognized reality: drug development is iterative, probabilistic, and (should be) continuously adaptive, requiring all manner of reasoning (including quantitative) about what is to be learned, what is actually learned, and what residual uncertainties remain that could all then inform drug development, regulatory, and public health decisions. The stage was set for MIDD. What would be needed next is continued evolution of the relevant methodologies along with a vision that specifically articulated the integration of M&S systematically across the drug development lifecycle as an applied science. The latter came in 2007 when Rick Lalonde and colleagues made popular the term “Model‐Based Drug Development” (MBDD) and proposed MBDD as a powerful mode for integrating multisource data and prior knowledge to prospectively inform decisions about dosing, trial design, risk management, and commercialization [7]. Importantly, Lalonde et al. not only provided a coherent umbrella term; they situated MBDD in the context of decision science using language that was accessible to decision‐makers irrespective of technical expertise, while providing examples of impact. By emphasizing learning, prediction, and most importantly decision impact, the paper positioned M&S as a strategic asset as opposed to a technical adjunct to drug development. In retrospect, the paper functioned as a kind of bridge document, translating years of pharmacometrics practice into an enterprise‐level vision important to what would later be termed “MIDD.” There continues to be a substantial and growing literature on the evolution, application, and impact of MIDD including: high‐profile reviews and impact assessments detailing the role of MIDD across development and regulatory decision‐making, including integrative lifecycle analyses and case synopses; literature that attempts to quantify MIDD's value (e.g., by estimating time and cost savings attributable to routine application); field syntheses and status updates that explicitly discuss MIDD's role in areas ranging from biopharmaceutics to generics to use in specific populations (e.g., pediatric patients); and state‐of‐the‐art articles and reviews exploring strategic integration, emerging techniques (e.g., AI/ML), and future opportunities [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Taken together, these publications illustrate that MIDD has progressed from niche methodological discussions to a truly transdisciplinary, forward‐looking applied science with demonstrable impacts.

The Role of the U.S. FDA in Advancing MIDD

Incorporation of new scientific approaches into routine drug development and regulatory practices has typically followed the classic hope‐hype cycle [15]. There are some key factors from the regulatory standpoint that go a long way in determining whether a new approach is likely to go beyond hype and be integrated into regulatory policy (e.g., guidances) or decision‐making (e.g., in the evaluation of INDs, NDAs, BLAs, ANDAs, etc.). Some of these include: (1) a strong signal from leadership that such approaches hold promise; (2) accumulation and dissemination of impact cases; (3) internal champions comprised of both technical experts and nontechnical experts, including those with decision‐making authority; (4) organizational investments in staff capacity and education; and (5) enhanced pathways for engagement between pharmaceutical developers and regulatory scientists on product development approaches that center the innovative or new methodology. MIDD has long been viewed at the U.S. FDA as having enormous potential. In 2004, for example, the FDA launched the Critical Path Initiative with concurrent publication of a watershed report “Innovation or Stagnation: Challenge and Opportunity on the Critical Path to New Medical Products” [16]. The report diagnosed problems in drug development and positioned regulatory science as an important part of the solution. Part of the report's core argument was that the disconnect between tremendous advances in biomedical research and their translation into more efficient and predictable drug development had to do with the paucity of applied tools needed to evaluate the safety, efficacy, quality, and other aspects of medical products. Among its proposed solutions, the report explicitly called for better quantitative tools, predictive models, and integrative approaches, leading to legacy impacts including establishment of the Critical Path Institute (which has a robust quantitative medicine [QM] component), increased FDA‐industry‐academia collaborations in quantitative science, investment in regulatory science infrastructure (with significant emphasis on a variety of quantitative‐translational capabilities), and a cultural shift toward seeing innovation in quantitative drug development tools as essential to public health. The FDA's commitment to advancing quantitative and in silico approaches to drug development has since carried forward under every subsequent FDA Commissioner. In addition to this high‐level endorsement, FDA staff scientists have an extensive record of championing MIDD approaches over several decades as demonstrated by regulatory impact cases, community engagement, and a wide‐ranging, field‐shaping publication history (both in the scientific literature and as formal regulatory guidance) [17, 18]. In one of its most recent exciting institutional expressions of support for MIDD, FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) established the QM Center of Excellence (QM CoE) (Figure 1) [19]. The QM CoE's vision is to “transform public health through the intersection of medicine and data science” with a mission to “foster innovation and the integration of [QM] throughout drug development and regulatory decision‐making for the benefit of patients.” The idea is to coordinate, advance, and institutionalize quantitative approaches across CDER. Through its core values of Collaboration, Innovation, and Stewardship, the QM CoE also has hopes to “cultivate and sustain a thriving community which advances QM innovation and application.” The QM CoE's creation reflects the recognition that modern drug evaluation increasingly depends on supporting complex decisions in ways that exceed traditional, discipline‐bounded workflows. Importantly, the QM CoE serves four interrelated roles that are critical to the advancement of MIDD (Table 1). Historically, quantitative medicine at FDA advanced through individual champions, specialized groups, and domain‐specific successes (e.g., population PK, PBPK, etc.). The QM CoE represents a shift from reliance on distributed expertise to intentional, strategic institutionalization. It signals that quantitative methods are core components of how CDER evaluates evidence. In a sense, the QM CoE represents the structural maturation of earlier conceptual milestones in the history of MIDD at FDA. This reflects the growth of quantitative approaches from specialized tools to shared regulatory infrastructure, embedding model‐informed reasoning more deeply into the culture and practice of drug evaluation. Perhaps the biggest catalyst for adoption of MIDD from the regulatory perspective, at least in the United States, has been FDA's MIDD Paired Meeting Program [20]. This program emerged from a longer evolution in how FDA engaged with quantitative methods in regulatory decision‐making. By the mid‐2010s, pharmacometric M&S was already being used by drug developers (sponsors) and in regulatory assessment, but engagement between sponsors and FDA on these approaches was often fragmented, narrowly scoped, or late. Discussions about M&S frequently occurred well after development plans were fixed, limiting their influence on strategy. Recognizing both the growing sophistication of model‐based approaches and the need for earlier, more in‐depth dialogue, FDA launched the MIDD Paired Meeting Program in 2018 under the fifth reauthorization of the Prescription Drug User Fee Act (PDUFA VI). The program was designed as a pilot to create a dedicated, predictable forum for sponsors and FDA to discuss how quantitative methods could inform specific development or regulatory decisions within the context of a given development program. The defining features of the program were its paired structure and multidisciplinary nature with subject matter experts leading the multidisciplinary review teams (a cultural change from the common practice of physician‐run regulatory meetings). The paired meeting format reflected an implicit acknowledgement of the Learn‐Confirm ethos, and the dedicated meeting pathway allowed for sufficient time to gain sponsor‐regulator alignment on proposed strategies. Early experience with the program demonstrated several benefits. It encouraged sponsors to frame modeling proposals around explicit regulatory questions, improved the quality and clarity of submissions, and facilitated cross‐disciplinary engagement within FDA [21, 22]. Importantly, the program helped shift conversations away from whether MIDD was acceptable toward how it could be used to support such decisions as dose selection, trial design, efficacy and safety extrapolation, just to name a few. Based on positive uptake and experience, the program was continued and expanded under PDUFA VII, signaling that MIDD was integral to drug development more broadly. The impact of the MIDD Paired Meeting program has been remarkable. An industry‐led analysis of pilot participants reported improved alignment on development strategy, stronger cross‐functional coordination within sponsor organizations, and clearer mutual understanding between sponsors and regulators regarding how M&S could address specific scientific and regulatory questions [11]. Participants also described tangible efficacy gains, including greater confidence in dose selection, trial design, and decisions about the necessity of certain studies, with some programs projecting significant reductions in development timelines and costs. These observations suggest the program encouraged earlier, more intentional integration of model‐informed approaches into development planning and strengthened the role of MIDD as a decision‐relevant component of regulatory engagement. There has been an exceedingly high demand for direct engagement between drug developers and FDA teams on MIDD. From the outset of the program in 2018, we expected to grant about eight requests per year in CDER. There has been more demand than we projected. Except for 2023 when companies were not sure if the program was still available after its initial 5‐year pilot phase, we exceeded yearly projections in terms of both meeting requests received and granted, suggesting a real need for and benefit of direct engagement on MIDD issues (Figure 2a). Furthermore, these requests have spanned over a dozen therapeutic areas (Figure 2b). In addition, 62 unique small, medium, and large pharmaceutical and biotech companies have participated to date, with companies continuing to request repeat participation for different development programs (Figure 2c). Importantly, as reported by participating companies from the IQ Consortium, the MIDD Meeting Program was utilized for questions of application across all phases of drug development, from preclinical to postapproval settings, and across a range of questions resulting in significant reductions of clinical development time (on the order of months to years), fewer patients needed in clinical trials, fewer clinical trials needed, and more informed go/no go decisions. The cost savings per participating drug development program ranged from $1 million to $70 million per program (and more for programs for which decisions to terminate development were made). “The estimated savings,” the authors report “not only serve the pharmaceutical companies, they also provide significant value to patients and the medical community as successful medications will reach the market faster. Additionally, the savings on resources and cost of development can be applied to the advancement of additional promising candidates” [11]. Global Convergence

The U.S. FDA is by no means the only regulatory body to support MIDD. MIDD has gained prominence through initiatives and science policy advancement within individual regulatory agencies including, but not limited to, the European Medicines Agency (EU), Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (Japan), Therapeutic Goods Administration (Australia), and Health Canada. At the institutional level, multiple regulatory agencies have developed or expanded early scientific/regulatory advice and cross‐disciplinary engagement around MIDD. While the formality, scope, and maturity of these efforts vary, the intentions are similar: earlier dialogue, clearer articulation of decision contexts, and increased openness to model‐informed reasoning as part of regulatory evaluation. A major milestone in global convergence is the development of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) M15 guideline “General Principles for Model‐Informed Drug Development” [23]. This guideline articulates principles for planning, documenting, submitting, and evaluating MIDD evidence across global regulatory bodies in a standardized approach and reflects a broad, collaborative regulatory commitment to integrating MIDD [24]. There is a critical aspect of ICH M15 that should be mentioned. One of the major barriers in the acceptance of MIDD is not methodological or logistical; it has to do with communication. For MIDD approaches to be acceptable to decision‐makers within a pharmaceutical company or to regulatory assessors, there must be a clearly articulated problem to solve/decision to be informed and a description of how the MIDD approach will address that decision in the context of a totality of evidence. These issues go beyond technical rigor. Even the most well‐founded methodological approach is unlikely to be endorsed if it is not clear to decision‐makers (who may not be technical experts) what the question is. ICH M15 acknowledges this challenge. To that end, it articulates a harmonized assessment framework intended to facilitate dialogue and alignment between sponsors and regulators on MIDD strategies (Table 2). The core elements of this risk‐based framework, along with guidance on structuring a Model Analysis Plan and features such as harmonized terminology and reporting expectations, are expected to facilitate greater use and more consistent evaluation of MIDD evidence across submissions and regulatory jurisdictions. Future Considerations

It is tempting to think the future of MIDD lies in the increasing sophistication of computational methods and other technologies. Technologies will continue to advance, and the drug development and regulatory communities will need to stay abreast, if not catalyze, those advances. In many ways, however, the major historical barriers to MIDD have been more cultural than methodological. As MIDD continues to mature, its future impact will be shaped less by further expansion of technical methods than by how effectively quantitative reasoning is embedded into decision‐making across the drug discovery‐development‐regulatory continuum. A continued priority should be emphasis on decision context. M&S is most valuable when its purpose, intended influence, and limitations are explicitly addressed, allowing quantitative evidence to be weighed appropriately alongside other lines of evidence and judgment. Frameworks such as ICH M15 reflect growing recognition that structured, risk‐based evaluation grounded in the question of interest and the consequences of uncertainty are likely to be essential for wider adoption of MIDD across diverse settings. MIDD has been invaluable in data‐rich settings. There is growing interest, however, in MIDD's translational promise in areas where evidence generation has been most challenging including rare diseases, pediatric drug development, precision medicine, and settings where robustly designed clinical trials are infeasible or exceedingly burdensome. In these settings, novel, consolidative model‐informed approaches might be needed to help bridge gaps in evidence while maintaining scientific rigor and patient protection. Future progress will also depend on organizational and workforce readiness. As MIDD approaches become more critical to development strategy, there is a concurrent need to strengthen MIDD literacy among clinicians, drug development teams, and regulatory reviewers and to foster cross‐disciplinary collaboration. Finally, as MIDD gains broader acceptance, there will likely be a need to define what MIDD “success” looks like to justify its application against competing approaches. Previously, ultimate success was defined when MIDD obviated the need for a clinical trial. While that remains desirable where appropriate, recent publications as discussed above have expanded what is possible in terms of “return on investment.” Success can include earlier learning, enabling go/no go decisions, more confident dose selection, fewer late‐stage failures, better alignment between evidence and decisions taken, and so on. To the extent these are measurable, measurement should be encouraged. Ultimately, the long‐term success of MIDD should be judged by whether it enables more transparent, timely, and well‐reasoned decisions that improve the development and evaluation of medicines for patients worldwide.
