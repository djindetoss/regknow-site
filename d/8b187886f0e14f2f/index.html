Abstract

Precision medicine offers the opportunity to improve the benefit-risk profile of new therapies by prospectively identifying patients most likely to respond or least likely to experience harm; however, its systematic integration into drug development remains inconsistent outside oncology. Key barriers include timely generation of robust predictive biomarker hypotheses (i.e., hypotheses regarding treatment-by-biomarker interactions), appropriate validation strategies, and coordinated development of companion diagnostics within increasingly complex FDA, European Medicines Agency, and In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation regulatory frameworks. This review presents a pragmatic operational framework to guide the incorporation of precision medicine into drug development across therapeutic areas. We outline structured approaches for early biomarker hypothesis generation and prephase II evidence development; define five clinical development scenarios based on the strength of the biomarker signal-front-loaded enrichment, precision-medicine-enabled phase II, adaptive phase II, adaptive phase III, and back-loaded confirmatory enrichment; and summarize regulatory and lifecycle considerations for companion diagnostics. Examples from oncology and emerging applications in cardiovascular and metabolic diseases illustrate evolving regulatory expectations and common industry challenges. We also discuss opportunities to extend precision medicine beyond predictive biomarkers to diagnostic, prognostic, and safety biomarkers, as well as digitally derived signatures. Treating precision medicine as a core component of drug development-rather than an optional enhancement-can improve clinical trial efficiency, support commercial viability, and ensure that patients derive meaningful clinical benefit. Early, structured biomarker planning; integrated clinical-diagnostic strategies; and iterative collaboration among sponsors, regulators, HTA bodies, payers, and patients are critical for translating biomarker insights into de-risked pivotal trials and aligned regulatory and market-access decisions.

Precision medicine aims to improve clinical outcomes by aligning diagnosis and treatment with biological and clinical heterogeneity, rather than relying on population‐averaged effects from heterogeneous trial populations. In many development programs, this heterogeneity is acknowledged but rarely translated into explicit strategies for identifying responsive or high‐risk subgroups, leading to diluted effect sizes and suboptimal benefit–risk profiles. 1 While oncology has established precision medicine as a default paradigm in many indications, progress in prevalent, multifactorial diseases (cardiovascular, metabolic, autoimmune) remains uneven despite advances in multi‐omics, digital phenotyping, and AI/ML. Existing discussions often focus either on technical biomarker discovery or late‐stage regulatory issues, without connecting early hypothesis generation, trial design, companion diagnostic development, and market access into an operational framework. To date, the most convincing demonstrations of this approach have emerged in oncology. The development of therapies such as trastuzumab for HER2‐positive breast cancer, 2 vemurafenib for BRAF V600E–mutated melanoma, 3 and endocrine therapies for hormone receptor–positive tumors 4 established that genetically defined patient selection can dramatically improve both clinical outcomes and the commercial success of drug development. These successes reshaped regulatory expectations, normalized the co‐development of drugs and companion diagnostics, and made biomarker‐defined indications a default strategy in many oncology drug development programs. By contrast, progress in precision medicine has been far more limited in prevalent, multifactorial diseases such as cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, metabolic disorders, autoimmune conditions, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH). In these indications, disease biology is distributed across multiple interacting pathways, clinical phenotypes overlap, and treatment effects are often modest when averaged across unselected populations. Despite broad recognition of underlying heterogeneity, sponsors frequently face practical barriers to implementing explicit precision strategies, including difficulty defining endpoints that are both mechanistically meaningful and acceptable to regulators, uncertainty about payers' willingness to reward narrower but higher‐value indications, and a reluctance to front‐load development costs by running broader phase I–II programs followed by smaller, biomarker‐enriched phase III trials. This gap persists despite rapid growth in the technical capacity to support precision medicine outside oncology. High‐throughput multi‐omics platforms, advanced imaging, wearable sensors, longitudinal electronic health record data, and AI/ML methods can now generate rich, multidimensional data at scale. 5 , 6 These data enable the discovery of composite biomarker signatures, dynamic disease phenotypes, and treatment‐response patterns that extend beyond single analytes or static baseline measurements. Yet, in many development programs, such insights remain exploratory or retrospective, rather than serving as the foundation for prospective trial design and regulatory strategy. A central barrier is not the absence of potential biomarkers, but the difficulty of translating early signals into credible predictive claims that withstand regulatory scrutiny. Predictive biomarkers—particularly those essential for safe and effective use—must be supported by coherent, prospectively planned evidence spanning discovery, early clinical research, and confirmatory trials. This often necessitates the co‐development of an in vitro diagnostic or other validated test suitable for routine clinical practice. 7 , 8 Diagnostic development introduces additional timelines, evidentiary requirements, and regulatory dependencies, and misalignment between drug and diagnostic programs can materially delay approval or erode the value proposition of an otherwise effective therapy. These challenges are amplified when biomarker hypotheses are introduced late in development. Programs that enter phase II or phase III without an explicit precision medicine strategy face constrained options: retrospective subgroup analyses lack credibility, biosamples may be unavailable or unfit for purpose, and trials are rarely powered to detect differential effects across biologically meaningful strata. 9 , 10 By contrast, early articulation of a mechanistically grounded predictive biomarker hypothesis allows development teams to make deliberate design choices—such as prospective enrichment, stratified randomization, or adaptive population refinement—that directly shape the interpretability and regulatory viability of downstream results. 11 , 12 Several structural factors contribute to the slower adoption of precision medicine outside oncology. First, predictive biomarker effect sizes in common chronic diseases are often modest, resulting in smaller differences in treatment response between biomarker‐defined subgroups and making detection of treatment‐by‐biomarker interactions statistically challenging. Second, translational models that reliably recapitulate human disease heterogeneity are frequently lacking, limiting the ability to generate robust early biomarker hypotheses. Third, early clinical development programs in many therapeutic areas rely heavily on studies in healthy volunteers, which constrain the ability to assess disease‐relevant biomarker–treatment interactions prior to phase II. Finally, from a commercial perspective, the development of therapies targeting narrower biomarker‐defined populations may be less attractive when incremental clinical benefit is modest and reimbursement frameworks do not clearly reward stratified approaches. Regulatory agencies have increasingly signaled openness to such approaches. Guidance documents now explicitly address enrichment strategies, adaptive designs, and the co‐development of drugs and diagnostics, reflecting recognition that smaller, biomarker‐defined populations may offer a more efficient and ethical path to demonstrating benefit. 13 , 14 , 15 At the same time, important uncertainties remain, particularly for complex multi‐analyte signatures, AI‐/ML‐derived biomarkers, and digitally captured endpoints, where validation standards, interpretability, and generalizability are still evolving—especially outside oncology. 16 , 17 Sponsors must therefore balance scientific ambition with regulatory pragmatism across multiple jurisdictions. This article proposes a pragmatic framework for integrating predictive biomarkers and companion diagnostics into drug development, emphasizing decision‐making under uncertainty across the lifecycle. Rather than providing an exhaustive review, we focus on conceptual decision frameworks and operational choices recurrent across programs. The goal is to support development teams in formulating and executing precision‐medicine strategies where traditional broad‐population models are misaligned with biology and payer expectations. Throughout, the emphasis is on conceptual decision frameworks and regulatory science rather than specific proprietary programs. The objective is to provide actionable guidance for development teams navigating precision medicine strategies in indications where unmet need remains high and traditional broad‐population development models are increasingly misaligned with biological and clinical reality.

Biomarker terminology and evidentiary standards

Throughout this article, terminology is aligned with the FDA–NIH BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource, which provides standardized definitions for biomarkers and clinical endpoints in drug development. 18 This article distinguishes several biomarker roles and evidentiary concepts that are critical to precision‐medicine development strategies: A predictive biomarker identifies individuals who are more likely than similar individuals without the biomarker to experience a favorable or unfavorable effect from a specific therapeutic intervention. A prognostic biomarker provides information about the likely course of a disease in an untreated individual or under standard care, independent of the effect of a specific intervention. Importantly, prognostic value—higher baseline risk in biomarker‐positive patients—does not establish predictive utility, which requires demonstration of treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction within randomized studies. We also differentiate three levels of evidence: analytical validity refers to the ability of a test to reliably and accurately measure the analyte of interest; clinical validity refers to the strength and consistency of association between the biomarker and a clinical state or outcome; and clinical utility refers to evidence that using the biomarker to guide treatment decisions improves net clinical benefit compared with not using it. 19 For companion diagnostics essential to safe and effective drug use, all three levels must be established. These definitions guide appropriate interpretation of evidence strength across the framework sections that follow. We discuss pharmacodynamic (response) biomarkers that indicate that a biological response has occurred in an individual exposed to a medical product or environmental agent, important for early clinical decision making. Within the BEST framework, biomarkers are distinct from clinical endpoints. A clinical endpoint directly measures how a patient feels, functions, or survives, whereas biomarkers may be used as surrogate endpoints only when supported by robust evidence linking them to clinical outcomes. A surrogate endpoint is a biomarker intended to substitute for a clinical endpoint and is expected to predict clinical benefit (or harm) based on epidemiologic, therapeutic, pathophysiologic, or other scientific evidence.

PRECISION MEDICINE HYPOTHESIS GENERATION AND PREPHASE II EVIDENCE

Stronger prephase II evidence enables decisive phase II designs (prospective enrichment, biomarker‐powered endpoints, adaptive allocation) and de‐risks phase III by clarifying target population, effect size, and companion diagnostic readiness. Below, we synthesize principal evidence sources, ordered from strongest to exploratory.

PRECISION MEDICINE HYPOTHESIS GENERATION AND PREPHASE II EVIDENCE › Disease diagnosis based on mechanistic abnormality

Highest confidence exists when the disease is defined by a molecular abnormality directly targeted by the therapy (e.g., CFTR mutations in cystic fibrosis, factor IX deficiency in hemophilia B). 20 , 21 Here, diagnostic and predictive biomarkers converge; clinical programs enroll biomarker‐positive patients from phase I onward, and broader testing lacks regulatory or ethical justification.

PRECISION MEDICINE HYPOTHESIS GENERATION AND PREPHASE II EVIDENCE › Validated biomarkers from competitor or class‐effect trials

The second tier of evidence is published data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating drugs with a similar mechanism of action, in which a biomarker has been shown to predict differential treatment response. Published RCTs showing differential treatment response by biomarker provide actionable evidence. Examples include NT‐proBNP stratification in heart failure trials of natriuretic peptide modulators and HbA1c‐based response in GLP‐1 agonist trials. 22 , 23 , 24 Competitor validation confirms tractable biology, available assays/cutoffs, and regulator/payer readiness. Failing to adopt proven enrichment can compromise trial success.

PRECISION MEDICINE HYPOTHESIS GENERATION AND PREPHASE II EVIDENCE › Mechanistic and early‐clinical evidence

Candidate pharmacodynamic or predictive biomarkers derived from mechanistic studies and measured in observational cohorts and patient‐inclusive phase I studies reveal heterogeneity in target expression, pathway activity, or downstream effects across patients with the same clinical diagnosis. Cross‐sectional cohorts characterize biomarker variability; longitudinal cohorts assess prognostic value (whether biomarker‐positive patients experience faster progression or more events independent of treatment). 25 Phase Ib or proof‐of‐mechanism studies provide the first opportunity to relate baseline biomarkers to drug‐specific pharmacodynamic effects or early clinical outcome measures, though small sample sizes (typically 20–60 patients) limit definitive conclusions. These sources build biological plausibility but cannot establish predictive utility, which requires demonstration of treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction in randomized trials. Analyses that assess only biomarker‐outcome associations without formal interaction testing risk conflating prognostic and predictive effects. Nonetheless, mechanistic and early‐clinical evidence remain the most actionable inputs for prephase II planning potential. 26 , 27

PRECISION MEDICINE HYPOTHESIS GENERATION AND PREPHASE II EVIDENCE › Synthesis and decision framework

Most programs triangulate multiple evidence sources. Stronger prephase II evidence supports aggressive phase II designs (prospective enrichment, biomarker‐powered testing); weaker evidence requires exploratory approaches preserving optionality. Document the precision‐medicine strategy in target product profiles and clinical plans before phase II, including biomarkers, evidence strength, phase II design, success criteria, and companion diagnostic timelines. This ensures cross‐functional alignment and enables early regulatory engagement via prephase II meetings. 44 , 45

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS

The design of phase II and phase III trials to evaluate precision medicine hypotheses depends critically on the strength of the biomarker evidence available at the start of phase II, as well as on the disease setting, endpoint characteristics, and competitive landscape. 46 No single approach is universally optimal; instead, sponsors must weigh trade‐offs among speed, cost, risk exposure, and the strength of evidence required for regulatory approval and market access. In this section, we describe five archetypal development scenarios. These scenarios are not exhaustive, and hybrid or customized designs may be appropriate in specific contexts (Table 1 ). Clinical development scenarios based on strength of predictive biomarker hypothesis

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 1: Frontloaded enrichment

In the frontloaded scenario, the decision to prospectively select a biomarker‐defined subgroup is made before initiating phase II, based on robust biological rationale and strong external or internal evidence. 47 This evidence may come from competitor trials that have demonstrated differential treatment effects in the biomarker‐positive subgroup, from mechanistic understanding so clear that broader testing is scientifically or ethically unjustifiable, or from rare disease settings where the biomarker defines the disease itself. Because the subgroup is selected prospectively, the phase II trial enrolls only biomarker‐positive patients, using either the competitor's assay and cutoff (if validated and accessible) or a closely aligned alternative. The trial evaluates dose–response, treatment effect size, and safety in the target population, with success criteria aligned to a precision medicine target product profile. Phase III then proceeds in the same enriched population, with the companion diagnostic validated prospectively as part of the pivotal program. 48 The primary advantage of frontloaded enrichment is efficiency: trials are smaller, faster, and more likely to demonstrate clinically meaningful effects. The primary risk is that the biomarker hypothesis may be wrong or the cutoff suboptimal, in which case the program forecloses the broader population without empirical testing. Frontloaded enrichment is therefore best suited to settings with very high confidence—rare diseases, validated competitor biomarkers, or biology so well understood that uncertainty is minimal. 49

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 2: Precision medicine–powered phase II

When prephase II evidence supports a plausible predictive biomarker hypothesis but does not yet justify prospective enrichment, a precision medicine–powered phase II design offers a middle path. 50 In this scenario, the phase II trial enrolls patients from the broad population, randomizes them to treatment or control, and is prospectively powered to detect a treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction consistent with a predictive biomarker effect. The biomarker may be binary (e.g., presence or absence of a genetic variant) or continuous (e.g., baseline expression level), and one or more cutoffs may be prespecified for subgroup analysis. Importantly, the trial is designed such that demonstrating superiority in the biomarker‐positive subgroup—even if the broad population shows a smaller or nonsignificant effect—is considered a successful outcome and sufficient to advance to phase III in the enriched population. 51 Statistical analysis plans typically include formal testing for interaction (treatment‐by‐biomarker) and may apply hierarchical or gatekeeping procedures to control type I error. 52 This design has several advantages. First, it provides empirical evidence for the predictive value of the biomarker within the investigational program, reducing reliance on external data. Second, it preserves optionality: if the biomarker hypothesis is not supported, the program can proceed in the broad population; if it is strongly supported, phase III can focus on the enriched subgroup. Third, it generates data on the companion diagnostic's clinical performance under trial conditions, informing analytical and clinical validation activities. 53 The trade‐off is that the phase II trial must be larger than a traditional dose‐finding study to achieve adequate power for subgroup analyses, and the timeline may be extended accordingly. Additionally, regulatory and commercial stakeholders must agree in advance on the evidentiary threshold for moving forward with enrichment, including the strength of the treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction and the clinical meaningfulness of the effect size in the subgroup. 54

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 2: Precision medicine–powered phase II › Feasibility considerations for detecting predictive effects

In many nononcology indications, effect sizes associated with predictive biomarkers are modest, and detecting a treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction requires substantially larger sample sizes than those needed to demonstrate an overall treatment effect. For example, assuming a moderate treatment effect in the overall population (e.g., relative risk reduction of 20–25%), detecting a clinically meaningful interaction—such as a doubling of effect in a biomarker‐positive subgroup—may require several hundred patients per arm, depending on biomarker prevalence and variability. When the biomarker is continuous and cut‐points are explored, multiplicity and loss of power further increase sample size requirements. As a result, many phase II studies are not powered to definitively establish predictive utility but instead provide directional evidence to support or refute enrichment strategies. In such settings, an absence of statistically significant interaction should not be interpreted as evidence of no predictive effect, particularly when confidence intervals remain wide. Instead, decisions should integrate effect size estimates, consistency across endpoints, biological plausibility, and external evidence when determining whether to proceed with enrichment or broader phase III designs.

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 3: Adaptive phase II

Adaptive trial designs embed prespecified decision rules that allow modification of the trial based on accumulating data while maintaining statistical validity and type I error control. 55 In the precision medicine context, adaptive phase II designs can be used to evaluate a biomarker hypothesis at one or more interim analyses and to adjust enrollment, randomization or the target population accordingly. 55 Several adaptive strategies are possible. One approach is to conduct an interim analysis of the treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction after a prespecified fraction of patients (e.g., 50%) have completed a pharmacodynamic or early efficacy assessment. If the interim data support an increased benefit–risk ratio in the biomarker‐positive subgroup, the trial can be modified to enrich the remaining enrollment toward that subgroup, either by increasing the randomization ratio or by restricting further enrollment to biomarker‐positive patients only. 56 Another approach uses an early biomarker (e.g., a pharmacodynamic readout) at interim to predict later clinical endpoints and gates the decision to continue in the broad population or enrich. 57 Adaptive designs offer flexibility and can improve efficiency if the biomarker hypothesis is correct. However, they are operationally complex, requiring real‐time data collection and analysis, decision‐making by an independent data monitoring committee, and logistical adjustments to enrollment and randomization. Trial duration is often longer than nonadaptive comparators because of the time required to implement changes, and regulatory agencies require detailed prespecification of all adaptation rules and statistical methodologies. 58 Adaptive phase II designs are therefore most suitable when uncertainty about the biomarker is moderate, the competitive environment demands speed, and the sponsor has the infrastructure to execute adaptive trials successfully. 58 Adaptive designs may partially mitigate power limitations by reallocating enrollment toward biomarker‐defined subgroups; however, the ability to detect treatment‐by‐biomarker interactions remains constrained by total sample size and interim timing, reinforcing the importance of realistic effect size assumptions at the design stage.

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 4: Adaptive phase III

In some cases, phase II results may suggest but not definitively establish a predictive biomarker hypothesis—for example, a post hoc subgroup analysis shows a trend toward greater benefit in biomarker‐positive patients, but the analysis was not prespecified or adequately powered. In such situations, an adaptive phase III design can prospectively test the hypothesis in the confirmatory setting. 59 One common approach is the adaptive enrichment design, in which the phase III trial initially enrolls patients from the broad population and includes a prespecified interim analysis to evaluate the treatment effect in both the full population and the biomarker‐defined subgroup. 60 If the interim data support a significantly greater treatment effect in the subgroup—based on predefined criteria and evaluated by an independent committee—the trial continues enrolling only biomarker‐positive patients, and the final analysis is conducted in the enriched population. If the interim analysis does not support enrichment, the trial continues in the broad population. 61 An alternative is to design a phase III trial with co‐primary objectives: demonstrating superiority in either the full population or a prespecified biomarker subgroup is considered a successful outcome, with appropriate control for multiplicity. 62 This approach has been used in cardiovascular outcome trials where a biomarker‐defined subgroup (e.g., elevated lipoprotein(a) levels) is hypothesized to have greater absolute risk and greater treatment benefit. 63 Adaptive phase III designs de‐risk the transition from phase II to phase III by allowing the program to pivot toward the most responsive population based on confirmatory‐phase data. However, they are subject to the same operational and regulatory complexities as adaptive phase II trials, and the interim analysis must occur early enough to allow meaningful enrichment but late enough to have sufficient data to inform the decision—a balance that is often difficult to achieve in outcome trials with long follow‐up. 64

CLINICAL DEVELOPMENT SCENARIOS › Scenario 5: Backloaded confirmatory enrichment

The backloaded scenario applies when a phase III program in the broad population has been completed and the results suggest a predictive biomarker—either through pre‐specified subgroup analyses or post hoc exploration—but the evidence is not sufficient for a regulatory claim. In this case, a confirmatory phase IIIb or phase IV trial can be conducted in the biomarker‐positive subgroup to validate the hypothesis and support an updated label. 65 This approach is most relevant when the broad‐population trial met its primary endpoint but the effect size was modest, payers or regulators request additional evidence before approving or reimbursing use in a subgroup, or when competitive pressure necessitates differentiation. The confirmatory trial is designed and powered conventionally, treating the biomarker‐positive subgroup as the target population and should use a prospectively validated companion diagnostic. 66 The backloaded scenario is the least efficient from a resource and timeline perspective, as it essentially doubles the phase III investment. It also carries the risk that the initial signal was a false positive or that the effect size in the subgroup is overestimated, leading to an underpowered confirmatory trial. Nonetheless, it may be the only option when precision medicine hypotheses emerge late in development or when early evidence was insufficient to justify prospective enrichment. 67

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT

Across all scenarios, the timing and design of clinical trials must be coordinated with the development of the companion diagnostic. Phase II trials provide the first opportunity to clinically validate the biomarker and to assess the analytical performance of candidate assay platforms under trial conditions. Phase III trials typically use a locked‐down, analytically validated assay that is on track for regulatory approval, ensuring that the pivotal efficacy and safety data are generated with the same diagnostic that will be used in clinical practice. 68 Delays or misalignment between drug and diagnostic timelines can have severe consequences. If the diagnostic is not ready when the phase III trial initiates, the program may need to use a research‐grade assay and later perform bridging studies to the commercial diagnostic—a strategy that adds time, cost, and regulatory risk. Conversely, if the drug approval is delayed or the indication changes, the diagnostic developer may have invested in a platform or claims that are no longer aligned with the drug label. 69 Early and sustained collaboration between the drug sponsor, diagnostic developer, and regulatory agencies is therefore essential, as discussed in the next section.

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Development and approval of companion diagnostics

Companion diagnostics are one class of diagnostic tools used to implement biomarker‐based patient selection and are required when a test is considered essential for the safe and effective use of a corresponding therapeutic product. However, not all biomarkers used in drug development or clinical practice require co‐development or regulatory approval as companion diagnostics. 70 They are distinct from complementary diagnostics, which provide useful information but are not essential, and from general‐purpose diagnostics used across multiple therapies or indications. In many cases, biomarkers may be implemented using standardized laboratory assays or clinical testing frameworks without formal designation as companion diagnostics, depending on regulatory requirements and clinical context. The regulatory designation of a test as a companion diagnostic carries specific requirements for co‐development, labeling, and postmarket obligations, and it directly affects the therapeutic's prescribing information and market access. 71

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Investigational use and early validation

During phase I and early phase II, biomarker assays are typically developed and optimized as laboratory‐developed tests or research‐use‐only reagents. These investigational assays are used to measure biomarkers in clinical trial samples, generate cutoff hypotheses and assess assay performance characteristics such as sensitivity, specificity, precision, linearity, and stability. 72 At this stage, the primary goals are feasibility, biological validation, and refinement of the predictive hypothesis, rather than full regulatory compliance. As the program advances toward pivotal trials, the assay must transition from investigational to a format suitable for regulatory approval and clinical deployment. This transition involves selecting a diagnostic platform (e.g., immunoassay, polymerase chain reaction, next‐generation sequencing, imaging algorithm), establishing a manufacturing process, locking down assay specifications, and conducting formal analytical validation studies to demonstrate that the assay performs consistently and accurately across the intended range of use. 73 Analytical validation typically addresses accuracy, precision, analytical sensitivity, analytical specificity, reportable range, reference intervals and potential interferents, following guidelines such as those from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). 74

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Clinical validation in pivotal trials

Clinical validation is the process of demonstrating that the diagnostic test accurately identifies patients who are more likely to benefit (or less likely to be harmed) by the therapeutic product. 75 For companion diagnostics, clinical validation is typically embedded within the pivotal phase III trials: the trial population is defined by the diagnostic test, and trial outcomes provide direct evidence that the biomarker‐positive population derives meaningful clinical benefit. However, when biomarker‐negative patients are excluded, such designs do not provide direct evidence regarding treatment effects in the biomarker‐negative population, which must instead be inferred from external data, earlier‐phase studies, or biological rationale. 76 Demonstration that biomarker‐negative patients derive less or no benefit requires either inclusion of these patients in randomized comparisons or robust external evidence supporting a differential treatment effect. The design of the clinical validation study depends on the biomarker type. For binary biomarkers (e.g., genetic mutations, protein overexpression), the trial enrolls test‐positive patients, and historical data, external controls or exploratory subgroup analyses in test‐negative patients provide the comparator. For continuous biomarkers with cutoffs, the trial may enroll patients across the biomarker range and include prespecified subgroup analyses to confirm the predictive threshold. 77 In either case, the clinical validation must demonstrate that the diagnostic's performance characteristics (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value) are adequate for clinical decision‐making. 78 Bridging studies may be required if the investigational assay used in early trials differs from the assay submitted for regulatory approval. Bridging typically involves testing banked clinical trial samples with both the investigational and commercial assays and demonstrating acceptable concordance, allowing the clinical validation data to support approval of the commercial assay. 79 In this context, the biomarker used to define the indicated population typically functions as a predictive biomarker and operationally serves as an enrichment factor in clinical trials.

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Regulatory pathways in the United States

In the United States, companion diagnostics are regulated by the FDA's Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) under the medical device framework, most commonly as Class III devices requiring premarket approval (PMA) or, for certain lower‐risk tests, de novo classification or 510(k) clearance. The FDA has issued guidance documents outlining expectations for co‐development of drugs and companion diagnostics, emphasizing early communication, parallel review processes, and synchronized approval timelines. The drug sponsor and diagnostic developer typically engage with FDA through joint meetings, starting as early as the end of phase I or beginning of phase II. These meetings allow alignment on the diagnostic's intended use, the clinical trial design, the analytical and clinical validation plans, and the labeling strategy. 80 FDA has established a collaborative review process in which the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) or the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) reviews the therapeutic product, CDRH reviews the companion diagnostic, and cross‐center consultation ensures that the drug and diagnostic labels are consistent and that approvals are coordinated. If the companion diagnostic is designated as essential for safe and effective use, the drug label will include specific language requiring or recommending the diagnostic test before prescribing, and the diagnostic label will reference the specific therapeutic product. The FDA aims to approve the drug and diagnostic simultaneously or in close temporal proximity, though in practice, diagnostic approval may lag by weeks to months if additional analytical data or manufacturing information is required. 81

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › European Union and IVDR

In the European Union, in vitro diagnostics are regulated under the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR), which came into full application in May 2022, replacing the earlier In Vitro Diagnostic Directive (IVDD). The IVDR introduced a risk‐based classification system (Classes A–D), with companion diagnostics typically classified as Class C or D, requiring conformity assessment by a notified body and more rigorous clinical evidence requirements. Under IVDR, a companion diagnostic must demonstrate analytical performance and clinical performance, including evidence that the diagnostic correctly identifies the patient population for whom the medicinal product is indicated. The clinical performance evidence may come from clinical trials of the medicinal product, literature, or other clinical data, and it must be sufficient to support the intended use statement. Unlike the FDA's centralized review, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) reviews the medicinal product, while the notified body assesses the diagnostic. However, EMA and notified bodies are expected to coordinate, and EMA guidelines encourage early dialogue between drug sponsors, diagnostic manufacturers, and regulators. 82 One key difference is that the EU system does not formally require simultaneous approval of the drug and diagnostic, and there is greater flexibility in the timing and nature of the diagnostic approval. Nonetheless, if the medicinal product's Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) includes a requirement for biomarker testing, the diagnostic must be available and CE‐marked for that indication at the time of drug launch.

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Global perspectives

Regulatory requirements for companion diagnostics vary across other major markets. In Japan, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) has established a consultation framework for simultaneous development and review of drugs and diagnostics, similar to the FDA model. In China, the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) has issued guidance encouraging co‐development and requires that companion diagnostics be approved before or concurrently with the associated drug. Canada, Australia, and other jurisdictions have their own regulatory pathways, though many align broadly with FDA or EMA frameworks and recognize approvals from those agencies as part of their evaluation. For global development programs, sponsors must navigate these regional differences in classification, evidentiary standards, labeling language, and postmarket requirements. Harmonization efforts—such as those by the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF)—have improved alignment in some areas, but significant heterogeneity remains. Practical strategies include designing pivotal trials that satisfy the most stringent regional requirements, engaging early with regulators in all major markets, and using modular or platform diagnostic technologies that can be adapted to regional preferences without compromising the core clinical validation. 83

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Labeling and claims

The labeling of both the therapeutic product and the companion diagnostic is a critical determinant of clinical uptake and commercial success. Drug labels may include language ranging from “required” (the diagnostic test must be performed, and only test‐positive patients should be treated) to “recommended” (the test provides useful information but is not mandatory) to “informative” (the test result is mentioned but does not restrict prescribing). The strength of the labeling language depends on the robustness of the predictive evidence, the magnitude of the treatment effect difference between biomarker strata, and safety considerations. 84 From a regulatory perspective, “required” language is supported by pivotal trials that prospectively enrolled only biomarker‐positive patients or demonstrated a strong treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction with minimal or no benefit in biomarker‐negative patients. “Recommended” language may be appropriate when benefit is seen across the population but is greater in the biomarker‐positive subgroup, or when safety concerns are more prominent in biomarker‐negative patients. 85 Diagnostic labels must mirror the therapeutic label and clearly specify the intended use, the patient population, the interpretation of results, and any limitations.

INTEGRATION WITH COMPANION DIAGNOSTIC DEVELOPMENT › Postmarket considerations

After approval, both the therapeutic and the diagnostic enter postmarket surveillance, with ongoing obligations to monitor safety, performance, and real‐world use. For diagnostics, this includes monitoring assay performance in clinical laboratories, reporting adverse events (e.g., false positives or false negatives with clinical consequences), and updating the diagnostic as needed to reflect new evidence or platform improvements. 86 Changes to the diagnostic that affect its performance characteristics or intended use typically require regulatory review and approval before implementation. From a lifecycle management perspective, sponsors and diagnostic developers must also consider the potential for label expansion—for example, extending the diagnostic's use to additional indications or therapeutic products or broadening the eligible population based on postapproval data. Such expansions require new clinical evidence and regulatory submissions, but they can significantly enhance the value of both the drug and the diagnostic. 87

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS

While much of the regulatory and development focus has been on predictive biomarkers of treatment efficacy, precision medicine encompasses a broader spectrum of applications that can enhance clinical development, inform treatment decisions, and improve patient outcomes. In this section, we discuss diagnostic, prognostic, and safety biomarkers, as well as the evolving role of digital and multi‐omic signatures, and consider their integration into drug development strategies.

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS › Diagnostic biomarkers

Diagnostic biomarkers are used to identify or confirm the presence of a disease or condition, often facilitating earlier detection or more accurate classification. 88 In therapeutic development, diagnostic biomarkers are particularly valuable in settings where the disease is underdiagnosed, misdiagnosed, or lacks a clear gold standard for diagnosis. Examples include blood‐based biomarkers for Alzheimer disease (e.g., amyloid‐beta, phosphorylated tau), 89 imaging biomarkers for MASH (e.g., MRI‐based fat fraction, elastography) 90 and circulating natriuretic peptides for heart failure. 91 When a therapeutic program targets a disease that is difficult to diagnose reliably, co‐developing a diagnostic biomarker alongside the drug can improve trial feasibility, reduce diagnostic heterogeneity, and ensure that patients enrolled in trials truly have the condition of interest. 92 Postapproval, the diagnostic biomarker can facilitate patient identification in clinical practice, potentially expanding the addressable market and ensuring that the therapy reaches those who need it most. From a regulatory standpoint, diagnostic biomarkers used for trial enrollment or as part of the indication definition must be well validated and reproducible, but they do not necessarily require formal approval as companion diagnostics unless the drug label mandates their use. 93 Nonetheless, sponsors should consider the practical availability of the diagnostic in clinical settings and work with diagnostic partners or laboratory networks to ensure access. 94

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS › Prognostic biomarkers

Prognostic biomarkers provide information about the natural course of disease independent of treatment, such as the likelihood of disease progression, recurrence, or adverse outcomes. 95 They are valuable for stratifying patients by risk, selecting appropriate endpoints for clinical trials, and supporting treatment decisions when multiple therapies with different risk–benefit profiles are available. 96 In drug development, prognostic biomarkers can improve trial efficiency by enriching the study population with patients at higher risk of events, thereby reducing sample size and trial duration. 97 For example, trials in chronic kidney disease often enrich for patients with higher baseline proteinuria or lower estimated glomerular filtration rate, as these patients are more likely to experience renal endpoints within the trial timeframe. 98 Similarly, cardiovascular outcome trials may enroll patients with elevated baseline biomarkers of inflammation or myocardial stress to increase event rates. 99 Prognostic biomarkers may also overlap with predictive biomarkers if the biological pathway that drives disease progression is the target of the therapeutic intervention. However, it is important to distinguish between the two: a prognostic biomarker that is not predictive identifies high‐risk patients but does not inform which treatment is most likely to benefit them. 100 Statistical methods such as testing for treatment‐by‐biomarker interaction are essential to establish predictive utility beyond prognostic value. 101 In many nononcology settings, prognostic enrichment—including approaches such as polygenic risk scores—may represent a more immediately actionable precision medicine strategy when predictive biomarker effects are modest, increasing absolute treatment benefit without requiring strong treatment‐by‐biomarker interactions. In many nononcology therapeutic areas, prognostic biomarkers may provide a more immediately actionable approach to precision medicine than predictive biomarkers. When treatment effects are broadly consistent across populations but absolute risk varies substantially, enriching trials or targeting treatment to higher‐risk patients can increase absolute risk reduction and improve the benefit–risk profile without requiring strong treatment‐by‐biomarker interactions. This approach is particularly relevant in cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, where therapies often produce modest relative risk reductions across heterogeneous populations. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) represent an emerging class of prognostic biomarkers that aggregate the effects of multiple genetic variants to estimate an individual's baseline disease risk. Recent studies have demonstrated that PRS can identify subgroups at elevated risk who may derive greater absolute benefit from established therapies, such as lipid‐lowering or antiplatelet treatments, even in the absence of clear predictive effects on relative treatment response. As such, PRS and similar composite risk models may support more efficient trial designs through prognostic enrichment and offer a scalable approach to precision medicine in common chronic diseases.

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS › Safety biomarkers and pharmacogenomics

Safety biomarkers are measured before or after exposure to a medical product or environmental agent to indicate the likelihood, presence, or extent of toxicity as an adverse effect. These biomarkers may support identification of individuals at increased risk, as well as detection, monitoring, or characterization of adverse effects following treatment, enabling either exclusion from treatment or enhanced monitoring and dose adjustment. 102 Pharmacogenomic markers are a prominent category, where genetic variants affecting drug metabolism, transport, or target sensitivity predict toxicity or intolerance. Examples include TPMT and NUDT15 genotyping before thiopurine therapy to reduce risk of myelosuppression, 103 HLA‐B5701* testing before abacavir to prevent hypersensitivity reactions, 104 and CYP2C19 genotyping to guide antiplatelet therapy. 105 A key challenge in the development of safety biomarkers is that many clinically important adverse drug reactions are rare, limiting the ability of pre‐market clinical trials to detect or robustly characterize such events. As a result, absence of a safety signal in early‐phase or even pivotal trials should not be interpreted as absence of risk. When prior evidence suggests potential genetic susceptibility—such as associations with thrombosis, arrhythmias, drug‐induced liver injury, or severe cutaneous reactions—developers should proactively evaluate underlying biological mechanisms and incorporate targeted pharmacogenomic assessments where feasible. Postmarketing surveillance and real‐world data are often critical to fully establish and validate safety biomarkers in these settings. The regulatory framework for safety biomarkers is similar to that for predictive biomarkers: if the biomarker is essential for safe use, the drug label will require or recommend testing, and a companion or complementary diagnostic may be needed. 106 However, the evidentiary standards differ in that the focus is on preventing harm rather than predicting benefit, and observational data, case series, or mechanistic evidence may be sufficient if the risk is serious and the prevalence of the at‐risk genotype or biomarker is substantial. 107 From a development perspective, safety biomarkers should be considered early as phase I, particularly if preclinical or mechanistic data suggests heterogeneity in tolerability. Incorporating safety biomarker testing into trials allows prospective evaluation of risk, supports informed consent, and may enable programs to continue in biomarker‐negative populations even if toxicity is observed in biomarker‐positive patients. 108

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS › Multi‐omics and composite signatures

Advances in high‐throughput technologies have enabled the integration of multiple data types—genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, imaging and clinical variables—into composite biomarker signatures. 109 These signatures can capture disease complexity and heterogeneity more comprehensively than single biomarkers and may have superior predictive or prognostic performance. 110 Examples include gene expression classifiers for cancer prognosis (e.g., Oncotype DX, MammaPrint), 111 multi‐protein panels for cardiovascular risk stratification, 112 and metabolomic signatures for diabetes progression. 113 The development and validation of multi‐omic signatures follow the same principles as single biomarkers, but with additional challenges related to data integration, feature selection, model complexity, and reproducibility across platforms and cohorts. 114 Regulatory agencies have emphasized the need for independent validation cohorts, transparency in model development, and demonstration that the signature adds clinical value beyond established risk factors or simpler biomarkers. 115 Composite signatures also raise practical challenges for diagnostic implementation. Unlike a single‐analyte test that can be performed on a standard clinical chemistry platform, multi‐omic signatures may require specialized sample handling, proprietary platforms, and centralized laboratory testing, all of which affect turnaround time, cost, and accessibility. 116 Sponsors must weigh these trade‐offs against the incremental predictive or prognostic value and consider whether simplified versions of the signature—using fewer biomarkers or more accessible assays—can achieve acceptable performance. 117

PRECISION MEDICINE BEYOND PREDICTIVE BIOMARKERS › Digital biomarkers and real‐world data

Digital biomarkers derived from wearable sensors, smartphone applications, electronic health records, and remote monitoring devices represent an emerging frontier in precision medicine. 118 These biomarkers can capture continuous or high‐frequency data on physical activity, heart rate, sleep, glucose levels, medication adherence, and symptom burden, offering insights into patient heterogeneity, treatment response, and disease progression in real time. 119 Digital biomarkers have been explored in trials across multiple therapeutic areas, including heart failure (remote hemodynamic monitoring, activity trackers), 120 diabetes (continuous glucose monitoring), 121 neurology (gait and tremor sensors in Parkinson disease), 122 and psychiatry (smartphone‐based mood and cognition assessments). 123 They offer the potential to identify subgroups with differential response based on baseline phenotypes, to detect early signals of efficacy or safety, and to support adaptive or decentralized trial designs. 124 However, the regulatory and evidentiary framework for digital biomarkers is less mature than for traditional biomarkers. Key challenges include validation of algorithms, management of missing data and signal quality, protection of patient privacy, and demonstration that digital endpoints are clinically meaningful and acceptable to regulators. 125 FDA and EMA have issued guidance on the use of digital health technologies in clinical trials and are developing frameworks for the qualification of digital biomarkers as drug development tools. 126 , 127 As these frameworks mature, digital biomarkers are likely to play an increasing role in precision medicine strategies, particularly for chronic diseases where remote monitoring and longitudinal phenotyping are feasible and valuable. 128

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

The integration of precision medicine into drug development represents a fundamental shift from population‐averaged treatment paradigms to individualized, biology‐driven therapeutic strategies. As this review has outlined, successful implementation requires a structured, evidence‐based approach that spans the entire development lifecycle—from early hypothesis generation and biomarker validation, through clinical trial design and companion diagnostic co‐development, to regulatory approval, labeling, and postmarket lifecycle management. In this section, we synthesize cross‐cutting themes, discuss barriers to implementation, and consider implications for patients, clinicians, payers, and regulators.

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Early planning and cross‐functional integration

A recurring theme is the critical importance of early planning. Precision medicine strategies are most effective when biomarker hypotheses are formulated during discovery or early clinical research, supported by mechanistic data, cohort studies, and translational models, and explicitly incorporated into the target product profile and clinical development plan before phase II. 129 Late identification of biomarkers—particularly after pivotal trials are complete—severely limits options and often requires costly and time‐consuming confirmatory studies. Early planning necessitates cross‐functional collaboration among discovery scientists, translational medicine, clinical development, regulatory affairs, biostatistics, diagnostic partnerships and commercial teams. Each stakeholder brings critical perspective: discovery teams identify candidate biomarkers and mechanistic rationale; translational medicine and biomarker scientists develop and validate assays; clinical teams design trials that balance scientific rigor with feasibility; regulatory affairs navigate agency expectations and labeling strategies; biostatistics ensure adequate power and control for multiplicity; diagnostic partners address platform selection, manufacturing, and market access; and commercial teams assess the value proposition for payers and clinicians. 130 Siloed decision‐making or delayed engagement of key stakeholders is a common source of failure in precision medicine programs. 131 Consistent use of BEST terminology across biomarker classification, evidentiary claims, and endpoint definitions can improve clarity and facilitate regulatory communication.

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Regulatory engagement and global coordination

Regulatory agencies have become increasingly supportive of precision medicine, recognizing its potential to improve development efficiency and patient outcomes. 132 However, expectations and requirements vary across regions, and navigating this heterogeneity is a significant challenge. Early and frequent engagement with regulators—through prephase II meetings, scientific advice procedures, parallel review processes, and ongoing dialogue during pivotal trials—is essential to align on biomarker validation standards, trial designs, statistical analysis plans, labeling language, and companion diagnostic requirements. 133 The FDA and EMA have established processes for co‐development of drugs and diagnostics, including joint meetings and coordinated reviews, and both agencies have issued guidance on enrichment strategies, adaptive designs, and biomarker qualification. 134 , 135 However, regulatory frameworks for novel biomarker modalities—such as AI/ML‐derived signatures, digital biomarkers, and multi‐omic panels—are still evolving, and sponsors should anticipate the need for iterative discussion and potentially post‐approval commitments to generate additional evidence. 136 Global coordination is also complicated by differences in diagnostic regulation, reimbursement pathways, and clinical practice. For example, the EU IVDR imposes more stringent clinical evidence requirements than the prior IVDD, and notified body capacity constraints have created bottlenecks in diagnostic approvals. 137 In Japan and China, regulatory pathways for companion diagnostics have matured rapidly, but language, cultural, and procedural differences require dedicated local expertise. Sponsors must design clinical programs and diagnostic strategies that satisfy the most stringent regional requirements while allowing flexibility for regional adaptation.

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Diagnostic development and market access

The success of a precision medicine strategy depends not only on regulatory approval but also on the practical availability, affordability, and adoption of the companion diagnostic in clinical practice. Diagnostic development is resource‐intensive, requiring investment in assay development, analytical validation, platform selection, manufacturing scale‐up, and quality systems. For sponsors without in‐house diagnostic capabilities, this necessitates partnerships with diagnostic companies, contract research organizations, or academic laboratories, and these partnerships must be established early to ensure alignment on timelines, intellectual property, commercial terms, and regulatory strategies. Market access for diagnostics is a distinct challenge. In many healthcare systems, reimbursement for diagnostics is determined separately from drug reimbursement, and the evidentiary standards, pricing structures, and approval timelines differ. 138 In the United States, coverage decisions for laboratory‐developed tests and FDA‐approved diagnostics are determined through a combination of national coverage determinations by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and local coverage determinations by Medicare Administrative Contractors. Evidentiary requirements for reimbursement, including demonstration of clinical utility, may vary depending on the test type, intended use, and coverage pathway. In Europe, health technology assessment bodies in individual countries evaluate diagnostics alongside or separately from drugs, and coverage may vary substantially across member states. 139 If the diagnostic is not reimbursed or is cost‐prohibitive, uptake of the therapeutic may be limited, undermining the precision medicine strategy. 140 Sponsors and diagnostic partners should therefore engage with payers early, present health economic data demonstrating the value of biomarker‐guided treatment, and consider pricing and contracting models that bundle drug and diagnostic costs or share risk. 141 Real‐world evidence on diagnostic use, treatment outcomes and cost‐effectiveness can further support reimbursement negotiations and postlaunch lifecycle management. 142

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Health technology assessment and payer expectations

Precision‐medicine development must anticipate not only regulatory requirements but also health technology assessment (HTA) and payer evidence needs, which often diverge from traditional trial‐centric hierarchies. Payers increasingly expect that biomarker‐guided strategies demonstrate added clinical value, cost‐effectiveness, and operational feasibility relative to nonstratified care. A biomarker identifying a smaller but highly responsive subgroup can improve cost‐effectiveness by concentrating treatment on high‐benefit patients—but only if the test is affordable, widely available, and accepted in clinical workflows. Test costs, false‐positive rates, and patient/clinician acceptance materially affect value propositions. Integrating HTA considerations into precision‐medicine strategies from the outset avoids misalignment between regulatory approval and market access, ensures that biomarker strategies deliver measurable value to health systems, and supports sustainable adoption in clinical practice.

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Patient and clinician perspectives

From a patient perspective, precision medicine offers the promise of more effective, safer, and personalized treatment, reducing trial‐and‐error prescribing and exposure to ineffective therapies. 143 However, it also introduces complexity and potential inequities. Access to biomarker testing may be limited by geographic, socioeconomic, or healthcare system factors, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries or underserved communities. 144 Patients and advocacy groups have emphasized the importance of equitable access to precision medicine, transparent communication about the benefits and limitations of biomarker testing, and inclusion of diverse populations in biomarker discovery and validation studies. 145 For clinicians, precision medicine requires understanding and integrating biomarker results into treatment decisions, which may necessitate education, decision support tools, and changes to clinical workflows. 146 Clinicians must also manage uncertainty when biomarker evidence is imperfect or when patients fall outside the validated biomarker range. Clear labeling language, clinical practice guidelines, and shared decision‐making tools can support appropriate use and avoid over‐ or under‐prescription. 147

DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS › Barriers and future directions

Despite progress, several barriers to precision medicine implementation remain. First, the cost and complexity of biomarker discovery, assay development, and clinical validation are high, and many programs—particularly those developed by small and medium‐sized biotechnology companies—lack the resources or expertise to execute precision medicine strategies effectively. Greater availability of shared infrastructure (e.g., biobanks, cohort studies, assay development platforms) and public–private partnerships could lower these barriers. 148 Second, the evidentiary standards for novel biomarker modalities—particularly AI/ML, digital biomarkers, and multi‐omic signatures—are still being defined, creating uncertainty for sponsors and slowing adoption. 149 Regulatory science initiatives to qualify biomarkers as drug development tools, establish performance benchmarks, and harmonize international standards are critical to accelerate progress. 150 Third, the precision medicine paradigm assumes that disease heterogeneity is biologically tractable and that subgroups can be reliably identified. In some settings, heterogeneity may be multifactorial, stochastic, or poorly understood, limiting the feasibility of biomarker‐based enrichment. 151 Continued investment in basic and translational research to elucidate disease mechanisms, identify therapeutic targets, and develop better experimental models is essential. 152 Finally, precision medicine must be integrated into a broader vision of personalized healthcare that includes lifestyle, environmental, social, and behavioral factors, as well as patient preferences and values. 153 Biomarkers are tools, not ends in themselves, and their use must be guided by a patient‐centered approach that balances scientific rigor, clinical utility, and ethical considerations. 154 A central challenge for precision medicine in prevalent chronic diseases is that biomarker‐defined subgroups often exhibit relatively small differences in treatment effect, requiring large sample sizes to demonstrate predictive utility and raising questions about clinical relevance. In contrast to oncology, where large effect sizes driven by dominant molecular drivers are more common, many chronic diseases are characterized by distributed and overlapping biological pathways, limiting the discriminative capacity of single biomarkers. In addition, the limited predictive value of nonclinical models and the reliance on healthy volunteer studies in early‐phase development delay the generation of clinically meaningful biomarker evidence. These scientific constraints are compounded by economic considerations: smaller, biomarker‐defined populations and modest incremental benefits can challenge conventional commercial models, particularly in healthcare systems where reimbursement for diagnostics and stratified therapies remains uncertain. Together, these factors necessitate more integrated scientific and economic strategies when operationalizing precision medicine outside oncology.

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Precision medicine represents a transformative opportunity to improve the efficiency of drug development, enhance benefit–risk ratios, and deliver more effective therapies to patients who need them most. However, realizing this opportunity requires deliberate planning, rigorous evidence generation, cross‐functional collaboration, and proactive engagement with regulators, payers, and patients. CONCLUDING REMARKS

Precision medicine is transitioning from niche strategy to core development paradigm. Success depends less on novel technologies than on disciplined planning, realistic evidentiary standards, and alignment across sponsors, regulators, payers, and patients. Treating biomarkers and companion diagnostics as integral components of the target product profile—not optional add‐ons—is essential to realize their clinical and economic value. Key stakeholder actions include: regulators refining adaptive‐design guidance and AI/ML standards; payers developing value‐based contracts tied to biomarker use; patients advocating for trial diversity and equitable diagnostic access; and sponsors treating precision medicine as a core strategic pillar rather than optional add‐on.
