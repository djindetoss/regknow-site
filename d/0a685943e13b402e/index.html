Artificial Intelligence in Mass Spectrometry-Based Metabolomics and Lipidomics for Drug Discovery and Development

Abstract

Artificial intelligence applied to mass spectrometry-based metabolomics and lipidomics has advanced through three technological generations: chemometric and classical machine learning, deep learning, and self-supervised foundation models, with large language models so far entering mainly at the edges. The metabolome and lipidome lie closest to drug action, yet they have been among the slowest omics layers to adopt modern artificial intelligence. This paradox arises from their downstream position, which leaves them without a closed molecular reference and without the standardized, readily modeled data available to the genome and transcriptome. This review examines what these methods contribute to drug discovery and development. We survey applications across the drug-development pipeline, from target identification and mechanism of action deconvolution, through the prediction of drug-induced toxicity, to pharmacometabolomics, in which pre-dose metabolic phenotypes inform patient stratification and dosing. Across these metabolomic and lipidomic applications, interpretable classical machine learning remains the dominant form of artificial intelligence. The newer deep-learning and foundation models have concentrated instead on compound annotation, the cross-cutting problem of assigning confident identities to spectra, yet successive generations have mitigated but not solved it. Beyond annotation confidence, batch effects, semi-quantification, and small-cohort overfitting also remain practical constraints. Overall, targeted, hypothesis-driven metabolomic and lipidomic studies can already support drug discovery when their inferences are restricted to confidently identified metabolites, whereas open, hypothesis-free discovery remains the frontier. Near-term progress will depend less on any single algorithm than on curated spectral resources, rigorous measurement, leakage-aware validation, and honest reporting of identification confidence.

Introduction

The productivity of pharmaceutical research has declined for decades. In an inversion of Moore's law known as "Eroom's law", the number of new drugs approved per billion dollars of inflation-adjusted research and development spending has fallen approximately exponentially since 1950 (Scannell et al., 2012), and contemporary estimates place the capitalized cost of a single approval in the billions of dollars over more than a decade of development (DiMasi et al., 2016; Wouters et al., 2020), a burden driven largely by candidates that fail in Phase II and III trials (Paul et al., 2010). Confronted with this attrition, drug discovery has sought to select better candidates and reveal their liabilities earlier (Peck, 2007), shifting from predominantly empirical screening toward data-driven design; artificial intelligence (AI), from classical machine learning through deep learning to foundation models and large language models, has become increasingly influential in target selection, lead optimization, safety assessment, and clinical development (Kolluri et al., 2022; Soni et al., 2026; Vamathevan et al., 2019). Yet these advances have not reached every molecular layer equally, and the reason lies partly in the structure of the data. Genomics and transcriptomics are anchored to a finite reference genome and to data that can be represented in relatively standardized discrete or quantitative formats such as variant calls and gene-by-sample expression matrices. These pose comparatively well-defined computational problems and have supported intensive algorithmic development (Soni et al., 2026). Mass spectrometry (MS)-based metabolomics and lipidomics, the measurement of small molecules and lipids, have no such closed reference and have been analytically harder to model, and they have been comparatively slower to receive the same depth of modern AI (Hong et al., 2025). This lag creates a central paradox in drug discovery. The molecular layer hardest for modern AI to model is also the one closest to drug action itself. That proximity follows from what these molecules represent. Where genomics and transcriptomics often help infer what a cell or a drug might do, the metabolome and lipidome record what has actually changed after a perturbation, serving as an integrative, downstream readout of physiological state and, for a drug, as a close molecular reflection of its pharmacodynamic response (Patti et al., 2012). But the same readout also reflects toxicity, compensatory physiology, diet, and the microbiome, so a change after dosing cannot be attributed to the drug alone (Bar et al., 2020). Even so, profiled after dosing, this molecular layer can help characterize mechanism of action (MoA) and toxicity; profiled before dosing, it can stratify patients by likely response, the founding premise of pharmacometabolomics (Clayton et al., 2006; Trevor et al., 2024). From a pharmacological standpoint, this dual role, characterizing drug action after dosing and predicting response before it, makes the metabolome and lipidome natural objects of study as readouts not only of disease but of drug action itself. The same properties that place this layer close to phenotype, however, carry an analytical burden that also helps explain the slower adoption of AI. Untargeted MS resolves thousands of features, yet many cannot be confidently matched to a known structure, the compound-annotation gap discussed below (Blazenovic et al., 2018; Schymanski et al., 2014; Sumner et al., 2007). Measurements are further shaped by batch effects and instrument signal drift, which demand dedicated quality-control designs and correction (Dunn et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2023; Martens et al., 2023), while untargeted intensities remain semi-quantitative (Gertsman and Barshop, 2018). Together with the small sample sizes and interpretability demands of clinical cohorts, these properties do more than slow AI adoption; they shape which methods succeed at which task. Within this constrained data landscape, the AI methods themselves have advanced through recognizable generations. Throughout this review, AI serves as an umbrella term for these data-driven approaches, with machine learning (ML) denoting the subset in which models learn patterns from data rather than follow explicit rules. Chemometric pattern recognition, classical ML, deep learning, and foundation models are therefore treated as successive generations within a single AI continuum. Chemometric pattern recognition (the multivariate statistical analysis of spectral data) and classical machine learning were joined by deep learning and, most recently, by self-supervised foundation models and large language models (Beveridge et al., 2025; Bushuiev et al., 2026; Duhrkop et al., 2015, 2021; Zhu et al., 2025b). As summarized in Table 1, these generations have not progressed uniformly across the pipeline. In the drug-development applications (target and mechanism, toxicity, and pharmacometabolomics), interpretable classical ML models such as random forests, partial least squares, and support vector machines remain the mainstay, with deep learning appearing largely as one modality within multi-omics integration. The newer generations are instead most established for compound annotation (the assignment of measured MS features to known chemical structures), a cross-cutting bottleneck that successive method families have mitigated but not solved (Hong et al., 2025). This review surveys AI across MS-based metabolomics and lipidomics, organized by the stages of the drug discovery and development pipeline. It covers target and mechanism, toxicity and safety, and pharmacometabolomics; the cross-cutting data, validation, and translational constraints that shape these applications; and the compound-annotation bottleneck and newest models, with the field's outlook. AI for target and mechanism

Target identification and validation sit at the front of the drug-discovery pipeline, and this is where metabolomics contributes in a way that distinguishes it from genomics. Rather than nominating candidate targets from sequence or expression alone, metabolomic profiling captures the metabolic phenotype that a target shapes, so that the measurement reflects the functional state of a pathway rather than its genomic potential (Pan et al., 2024). That orientation suits diseases defined by altered metabolism. In oncology, for example, metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, and the enzymes and pathways that drive it, together with the flux through them, become candidate targets that metabolic profiling and flux analysis can help reveal (Liang et al., 2021). The same reasoning extends to the lipidome. Many cancers depend on rewired lipid metabolism, and lipidomics combined with AI-based analysis has helped highlight dysregulated lipid enzymes and aberrations of therapeutic relevance (Irshad et al., 2023). A more specific problem, and one to which AI has begun to contribute directly, is target deconvolution (the identification of the intracellular target responsible for a compound's cellular effect). In one example, an interpretable logistic-regression model matched an antibiotic's metabolic-response profile to a mechanism-specific signature, and protein structural similarity then helped pinpoint its off-target (Chowdhury et al., 2023). The value of the metabolomic component is that it captures the functional consequence of target perturbation, not simply whether a compound binds a candidate protein. Metabolic signatures can therefore help distinguish among plausible targets that binding assays alone may not resolve, making the approach useful for target validation as well as hypothesis generation. Inferring a compound's MoA from the metabolic fingerprint it leaves is a closely related but broader problem than target deconvolution. The idea originated in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics, where chemometric pattern recognition could already classify antibiotics by their mechanism, establishing that a metabolic fingerprint carries mechanism-specific information (Hoerr et al., 2016). It then moved to MS, where an untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) approach recovered the modes of action of a blinded panel of eight antibacterial compounds, correctly inferring several of them from their metabolic responses alone (Vincent et al., 2016). At larger scale, high-throughput MS metabolomics profiled cellular responses to hundreds of antimicrobial compounds and predicted their modes of action by comparison with a reference set of known-mechanism drugs, with experimental validation (Zampieri et al., 2018). More recent AI-based work uses random forests and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), both interpretable machine-learning methods, to predict the MoA of a panel of anti-proliferative compounds from central-carbon and energy metabolism in prostate cancer cells (Saoud et al., 2024). Across all four studies, from early pattern recognition to more recent ML, the objective is not classification accuracy alone but identifying which metabolic changes define the mechanism, a requirement that recurs across the drug-development applications of this review. Beyond reading a compound's target and mechanism, MS-based metabolomics and lipidomics also help identify the compounds of drug discovery itself. Systems-biology-guided analysis of metabolic networks has been used to prioritize metabolite lead compounds and druggable targets directly, treating the metabolome not only as a pathway readout but as a source of chemical starting points (Palermo, 2023). Where that work analyzes the metabolome to identify new leads, natural-product discovery is the complementary case, since chemistry here is already abundant and the rate-limiting step is not finding candidates but recognizing which are already known. This process, called dereplication, is time-consuming and must be completed before genuinely new leads can be pursued. Tandem-MS molecular networking addresses this by organizing spectra by structural similarity so that unknown bioactive metabolites cluster with characterized families, and it has been proposed explicitly as a strategy for drug discovery, drug metabolism, and precision medicine (Quinn et al., 2017); coupled to community infrastructure such as the Global Natural Products Social molecular networking platform (GNPS) (Wang et al., 2016), it helps prioritize candidates and connect them to known chemistry before costly isolation and structure elucidation. Both approaches depend on confident compound annotation, placing this lead-discovery work at the edge of the same bottleneck treated more generally below, linking target and mechanism work to the field's central problem. Across target and mechanism work, the most prominent AI approaches are interpretable classical ML models such as pattern recognition, random forests, and regularized regression (Table 1, 2), consistent with the small, mechanism-focused datasets typical of this stage, where knowing which metabolic changes mark an effect matters as much as predictive accuracy (Table 3). The lead-discovery applications are different in kind. Molecular networking and community spectral libraries organize chemistry rather than train predictive models, and their reach is bounded less by algorithm choice than by the compound-annotation limits discussed below. Much of the concrete primary evidence to date is metabolomic rather than lipidomic, although lipidomics-informed target work is emerging (Irshad et al., 2023), and more complex AI approaches such as deep learning appear mainly where target inference is folded into broader multi-omics integration rather than into metabolomics or lipidomics alone. The same preference for interpretability recurs, with higher stakes, when AI is turned to drug toxicity and safety.

AI for toxicity and safety

Safety liabilities are a well-recognized cause of drug-candidate attrition (Waring et al., 2015), so detecting them early is a central goal of preclinical and translational research. MS-based metabolomics, together with lipidomics for lipid-related toxicities, can contribute biochemical signatures of injury that complement conventional safety markers and support the early identification of adverse effects (Sille and Hartung, 2024; Son et al., 2024). AI can add value in this setting by combining many individually modest metabolic changes into a multivariate predictive signature, as the multi-metabolite classifiers discussed below illustrate. Because these molecules respond to the downstream physiological consequences of exposure, they can, in some settings, detect biochemical injury before standard clinical chemistry becomes abnormal, which is what makes them attractive for early risk assessment rather than for post hoc confirmation alone. Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is the most developed application, both because it matters clinically and because the liver's central role in metabolism means that injury registers in blood and other readily sampled biofluids. Here AI-based analysis, mostly with classical machine learning, maps metabolic profiles onto injury status. Untargeted profiling has yielded candidate diagnostic biomarkers from saliva, pointing toward noninvasive tests (Yu et al., 2024), and from serum to separate intrinsic from idiosyncratic injury (Wei et al., 2025). In a pediatric cohort receiving high-dose methotrexate, pre- and post-treatment plasma metabolomics flagged moderate-to-severe hepatotoxicity, supporting early risk assessment. The underlying models combined LASSO-based feature screening with random forest, support vector machine, and Bayesian logistic regression (Guo et al., 2026). The maturity of the application is clearest at scale. A multicenter targeted-metabolomics study of 516 serum samples mapped the metabolic heterogeneity of DILI across antibiotic-, herb-, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-, and statin-induced cases and trained a compact ten-metabolite classifier to distinguish antibiotic-, herb-, and statin-induced DILI from one another and from controls, reporting accuracies near 0.91 on two held-out test sets; its discriminating features implicated glutathione, fatty-acid, and carnitine metabolism, providing clinicians with interpretable biological context rather than a score alone (Ding et al., 2026). The same approach extends upstream into preclinical screening. In the HepaRG hepatocyte model, untargeted LC-MS with partial least-squares discriminant analysis and random-forest selection characterized the metabolic mode of action of bosentan-induced cholestasis, implicating ceramide and phospholipid remodeling (Cuykx et al., 2019). Coverage of other organ toxicities is more uneven. Cardiotoxicity is beginning to attract MS-metabolomic study. In breast-cancer patients receiving doxorubicin, plasma metabolomic profiling with logistic-regression feature selection and random-forest modeling identified baseline metabolites associated with subsequent doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, although the cohort was small and the reported discrimination only moderate, making this an early proof of concept rather than a validated predictor (Singh et al., 2026). Evidence for nephrotoxicity remains comparatively limited. Conventional markers such as serum creatinine are late and nonspecific, motivating the search for better biomarkers (Sher et al., 2026). Although kidney disease more broadly has been extensively profiled, MS-omics AI/ML evidence specific to drug-induced renal injury remains sparse. Where organ-by-organ evidence remains uneven, a complementary way to organize safety applications is by mechanism, and toxicities driven by lipid disturbance are where lipidomics contributes most. When the injury mechanism is itself lipid-mediated, MS lipidomics can measure the mediators directly. In paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathy, targeted and untargeted LC-MS lipidomics with machine learning quantified hundreds of lipid species and highlighted sphingolipids among the candidate mediators, with biological testing of a leading candidate (Lotsch et al., 2024); here the measured lipids lie closer to the toxicity phenotype than in many other applications, so the readout is mechanistically as well as diagnostically informative. Drug-induced phospholipidosis is another clinically relevant lipid toxicity (Breiden and Sandhoff, 2019), but AI models built on measured MS lipidomics for this liability remain underdeveloped, making it an open opportunity. This gap partly reflects lipid-specific analytical hurdles, as many lipids are isobaric or isomeric species that differ only in fatty-acyl composition, acyl position, or double-bond location and yield similar fragment ions (Liebisch et al., 2020). Such species are therefore often reported only at the class or species level of structural resolution. Lipid annotation also still relies heavily on class- and adduct-dependent rules curated in resources such as MS-DIAL 4, rather than on the large, uniformly annotated datasets that learned models require (Tsugawa et al., 2020). As in target and mechanism work, the most prominent AI methods for toxicity prediction are classical, interpretable ML models (Table 1-3); deep learning is comparatively sparse in MS-omics toxicity studies. In safety work, two further forces strengthen this preference. One is regulatory expectations, which favor a transparent rationale for AI models when predictions may affect candidate continuation or clinical monitoring (Togo et al., 2024); the other is the modest cohort sizes of most toxicity studies, where many metabolite features and few events make overfitting a practical risk (Vabalas et al., 2019). Those constraints carry forward, but the question changes. The same metabolic profiles used to anticipate harm can also, especially when measured before dosing, help forecast who is likely to benefit, the premise of pharmacometabolomics.

AI in pharmacometabolomics

Pharmacometabolomics inverts the timing of the preceding sections. Rather than measuring the metabolome after exposure to characterize mechanism or toxicity, it profiles a subject's pre-dose metabolic state (the "metabotype") to anticipate how that subject will respond to a drug (Clayton et al., 2006). Conceptually, the metabotype is framed as a phenotype-proximal readout that integrates genetic, physiological, and environmental inputs, which makes it complementary to pharmacogenomics by also reflecting the dietary and microbiome influences that genotype does not capture. Because such metabotypes are high-dimensional and shaped by many interacting factors, pharmacometabolomics is a natural setting for AI-based prediction, although current practice remains largely classical, as discussed below. Recent reviews position this approach as a precision-medicine tool across drug discovery and development (Clayton et al., 2006; Stolaki et al., 2025; Trevor et al., 2024). Post-dose profiling can still contribute within this framework (Beger et al., 2020; Kaddurah-Daouk et al., 2014), mainly as a follow-up test of the prediction, checking whether patients a pre-dose model flags as responders show the expected response after exposure; in that role it connects the patient-selection end of the pipeline back to the mechanistic readouts discussed earlier. The clearest use is pre-dose forecasting of response. Baseline metabolic profiles have been associated with response to metformin in early type 2 diabetes (Park et al., 2018) and have distinguished drug-response phenotypes for antihypertensive therapy by gas chromatography-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF/MS) (Rotroff et al., 2015). Across these examples the metabolic profile serves as an integrative readout of the many factors that shape how a patient responds. The same pre-dose logic extends from predicting response to individualizing dose. In renal-transplant patients receiving tacrolimus, ultra-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF-MS) metabolic profiling with classical modeling linked the metabolic profile to pharmacodynamic response to help individualize dosage (He et al., 2022); and in healthy volunteers, pre-dose plasma gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiles predicted the pharmacokinetic parameters of atorvastatin through a partial least-squares model, a direct demonstration that a baseline metabotype can forecast drug exposure before administration (Huang et al., 2015). Lipidomics carries the same pre-dose, patient-stratification logic into settings where lipids are central to a drug's effect. In advanced non-small cell lung cancer, LC-MS lipidomic profiles of pre-treatment serum, analyzed with partial least-squares discriminant analysis, were associated with whether patients subsequently achieved disease control or progressed on chemo-immunotherapy, though in a single treated cohort (Jiang et al., 2024). The same sensitivity of the lipidome to drug action also holds in the opposite direction. Measured after dosing rather than before, a lipid profile captures not who will respond but what a patient has already been exposed to. Because statins so strongly reshape circulating lipids, statin exposure itself can be inferred from the lipidome (Yi et al., 2025), which is less a response prediction than an exposure signal and one worth controlling for as a confounder when lipidomic data are interpreted (discussed below). Pharmacometabolomic studies can be viewed as drug-response extensions of the metabolome-wide association framework (Holmes et al., 2008), but in practice they remain driven mainly by classical statistical modeling with multiple-testing correction, with machine learning, the AI component of these studies, used chiefly for high-dimensional feature selection rather than as the central inferential engine. The analytical methods here are again mostly classical, with the representative examples relying on chemometric or classical statistical modeling such as partial least squares and logistic or ridge regression, and more complex AI approaches such as deep learning not yet established in the pharmacometabolomic studies summarized in Table 1. This likely reflects both the modest, often longitudinal cohort designs typical of the field and the premium on interpretable metabolite signatures that can be measured and acted on in the clinic. Pharmacometabolomics closes the application arc, from finding targets, through anticipating harm, to selecting and dosing patients. These applications are united, and limited, by a shared dependence on trusted measurements, generalizable AI models, and confident identification of the underlying metabolites and lipids. The review turns first to these data and validation constraints and then to the compound-annotation bottleneck at their core.

Data, validation, and translational readiness of AI models

The applications discussed above share a set of constraints that depend less on which AI algorithm is chosen than on whether the underlying measurement can be trusted and whether a model built in one study will generalize to the next. These constraints help explain a pattern seen throughout this review, namely that interpretable classical models still lead the drug-development applications (Table 3). They also determine what any AI method must satisfy before a research finding can inform a decision that affects a drug or a patient (Fig. 4). The first constraint is the nature of the measurement. Untargeted MS is a discovery tool. It surveys thousands of features and reports them semi-quantitatively as relative intensities that are sensitive to matrix and acquisition conditions (Gertsman and Barshop, 2018; Zhu et al., 2025a), whereas targeted assays trade breadth for the absolute, validated quantification of a predefined panel. This distinction maps onto drug-development stages. A semi-quantitative untargeted signature is well suited to generating hypotheses by nominating a candidate toxicity marker or a response-associated metabotype, but a claim that affects dosing, patient selection, or a safety decision generally requires transition to targeted, quantitative assays that can be validated and audited. Matching the analytical mode to the question is a prerequisite for any AI model built on these data, not a secondary consideration. A second constraint is confounding. The metabolome and lipidome lie downstream of many influences, including diet, the gut microbiome, circadian rhythm, disease state, and concomitant medication as well as the drug under study. A change observed after dosing therefore cannot be attributed to the drug by default. The same proximity to physiology that makes these layers valuable as pharmacodynamic readouts also makes them noisy, and isolating a genuine drug effect calls for designs that constrain the alternatives, such as paired pre- and post-dose sampling, longitudinal profiling, and well-matched controls. For example, GC-TOF/MS profiling in major depressive disorder found that early biochemical changes during sertraline treatment partly overlapped those seen with placebo, illustrating why post-dose metabolic shifts require controls before they can be interpreted as drug-specific effects (Kaddurah-Daouk et al., 2013). Confounding of this kind also favors interpretable AI models, whose selected features can be checked against known biochemistry rather than accepted uncritically. A third constraint is technical variation. Signal intensities drift across an analytical run and differ between batches, and these batch effects can match or exceed the biological differences a study aims to detect; controlling them requires quality-control designs built in from the start, including pooled quality-control samples interspersed through the sequence, system-suitability monitoring, and drift correction, not repairs attempted afterward (Broeckling et al., 2023; Dunn et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2023; Martens et al., 2023). An AI model trained on uncorrected data can learn the batch structure instead of the biology and will fail to transfer to a new site or instrument. Computational correction, including AI-based approaches, offers a complementary route, in which methods ranging from statistical batch-effect models to deep adversarial autoencoders such as NormAE learn batch structure from the data and can address the nonlinear inter-batch variation that pooled-QC normalization may leave behind (Rong et al., 2020). Such correction does not replace careful design, however, because aggressive adjustment can attenuate real biology along with the batch signal, and unless it is fit within training folds and evaluated on held-out batches, it can leak information into downstream validation. Earlier still in the workflow, the raw-data processing that precedes any model, including feature detection, peak alignment, adduct and isotope grouping, and imputation of missing values, embeds analytical choices that propagate into every downstream result. A final constraint is the cohorts themselves. Clinical metabolomic and lipidomic studies are typically small and high-dimensional, with far more measured features than subjects. This setting invites overfitting and favors regularized, interpretable ML models (Whalen et al., 2022), and it makes validation design just as critical. If correlated samples, such as repeated measurements from the same subject, are split across training and test sets, information can leak between them and inflate reported accuracy. Validation must therefore be group-aware, keeping such correlated samples together, so that reported performance reflects genuine signal rather than leakage (Bernett et al., 2024). Interpretability is not merely a modeling preference in this setting (Table 3). A pharmacology-facing AI model is more useful, and more likely to withstand regulatory scrutiny, when it points to specific, mechanistically plausible metabolites or lipids rather than only reporting a high discrimination score from an opaque predictor. Reproducibility, in turn, depends on transparent reporting of data provenance, processing parameters, and identification confidence, without which a published signature cannot be independently rebuilt or trusted for a regulatory decision. These constraints frame why, for translational use, the central difficulty lies less in the choice of AI model than in the measurements and validation frameworks beneath it. Appropriate model choice can mitigate these limitations, but it cannot overcome poor measurement, confounding, drift, or uncertain feature identity. The most fundamental of these data problems is knowing what each measured feature is, the compound-annotation challenge to which the review now turns.

Current challenges and perspectives

Annotation, the task of turning raw spectra into confident molecular identities, is the field's cross-cutting bottleneck and the main focus of recent AI development. Untargeted MS resolves thousands of features per sample, yet only a fraction can be matched to known structures, and those assignments must be reported at explicit levels of identification confidence rather than treated as certain (Schymanski et al., 2014; Sumner et al., 2007). Two reporting frameworks, the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) levels and the Schymanski scheme, now structure how that confidence is reported, grading an assignment from a fully confirmed structure down to a molecular formula or an unresolved feature (Fig. 3, Table 4). Moreover, in any untargeted experiment a substantial share of features remains at the lowest, unidentified end of this scale. Unlike the genome, which is finite and fully sequenced, the metabolome offers no closed reference set against which every feature can be placed (Blazenovic et al., 2018). The three AI generations of this review are usefully seen as successive attempts to overcome this bottleneck (Fig. 2, Table 4). Chemometric and kernel methods came first. CSI:FingerID predicts molecular fingerprints (encoded structural features) from tandem mass spectra (MS/MS) and uses them to search chemical-structure databases (Duhrkop et al., 2015), while community infrastructure such as GNPS organizes spectra into molecular networks that group them by shared fragments, propagating annotations from characterized to unknown members of a spectral family (Wang et al., 2016). Deep learning followed and broadened the range of tasks it could address. CANOPUS predicts compound classes (not full structures) from fragmentation spectra using a deep neural network (Duhrkop et al., 2021); MSNovelist generates candidate structures de novo, that is, without a database match (Stravs et al., 2022); models such as MIST-CF infer the chemical formula that anchors any subsequent structural assignment (Goldman et al., 2024); and machine-learning prediction of MS/MS spectra now expands sparse reference libraries in silico (Nguyen et al., 2024), while deep networks predict complementary properties such as retention time to help narrow candidate identities (Xue et al., 2024). Lipid annotation has followed a somewhat separate path, relying on largely rule-based, curated resources such as the MS-DIAL 4 lipidome atlas, which extended annotation coverage across lipid classes (Tsugawa et al., 2020). Even so, lipid identities are reported through a shorthand notation that reflects the structural resolution achieved, often the lipid class or species rather than the exact fatty-acyl position on the glycerol backbone, the double-bond position, or the stereochemistry, which depend on the analytical method (Liebisch et al., 2020). The newest generation applies self-supervised and language models. DreaMS, a foundation model pretrained on millions of tandem mass spectra rather than a closed reference library, learns general-purpose representations that transfer to downstream annotation tasks (Bushuiev et al., 2026). Many current language-model applications still assist adjacent steps, including lipidomics workflow automation (Beveridge et al., 2025) and prediction of the collision cross section, an ion-mobility measure of molecular size and shape that narrows candidate lists (Zhu et al., 2025b). These methods also differ in how many orthogonal analytical dimensions they exploit. Some rely mainly on MS/MS spectra with precursor mass or formula (CSI:FingerID, CANOPUS, MSNovelist, DreaMS), others add predicted retention time or ion mobility and collision cross section, and each added dimension further narrows the candidate space. More recently, language models have been turned toward spectral interpretation itself, learning to translate tandem mass spectra directly into chemically meaningful molecular descriptions (Zhou et al., 2026). None of these newest approaches yet amounts to a solution. These models also carry practical costs, because pretraining on millions of spectra demands substantial computational resources (Bushuiev et al., 2026). Language-model-style architectures further require continuous m/z and intensity values to be discretized or otherwise encoded (Zhou et al., 2026), a step that can sacrifice mass accuracy. Generative outputs can be chemically plausible yet incorrect, and such hallucinations are difficult to detect without orthogonal evidence. The adjacent-step models are aids and auxiliary predictors, semantic interpretation yields descriptions rather than exact structures, and systematic, comparable benchmarks are only beginning to emerge. Whether foundation models will finally close the annotation gap or mainly relocate it is not yet clear, and their reported gains are best weighed against the confidence levels above, useful for early, low-stakes triage but not yet a substitute for confirmed identification. Across all three generations the gap has narrowed but not closed; the bottleneck has been mitigated, not solved. Rather than improve annotation, one AI strategy bypasses it altogether. End-to-end deep learning trained directly on raw spectra can predict outcome labels, such as disease status, without first annotating individual compounds (Deng et al., 2024). Such models can highlight the mass-to-charge and retention-time regions that drove a prediction and may even map the high-contribution signals to candidate metabolite-protein networks through database and MS/MS matching. Used this way, such predictions can aid diagnosis, cohort enrichment, and early triage, but their underlying assignments are not by themselves confirmed identities, and for deliverables that must support mechanism, safety, or a qualified biomarker they shift rather than remove the need to define the molecular signal. These difficulties in reaching confident identification are real, but they do not block every use of the metabolome in drug development. Confident annotation is essential when a feature is used mechanistically, clinically, or regulatorily, and the confidence required rises with the stakes of the decision. Many targeted or hypothesis-driven studies therefore manage the bottleneck by narrowing the decision-relevant signal to confirmed metabolites, rather than waiting for a general solution. For example, broad targeted profiling can be distilled into a compact metabolite classifier validated on held-out test sets (Ding et al., 2026), and a lipidomic machine-learning signature can highlight a leading sphingolipid candidate for subsequent biological testing (Lotsch et al., 2024). In such settings, unconfirmed features remain exploratory rather than decision-bearing. The metabolome also cannot resolve a drug's pharmacology in full. As a downstream readout it records the integrated consequences of drug action, not the upstream causal steps that produced them. The bottleneck is most acute in open, hypothesis-free discovery, where no metabolite is singled out in advance and the informative feature may itself be one that cannot yet be identified. That setting is likely to benefit the most from the newer generations. As annotation improves and datasets become more standardized and shareable, drug-development applications that still rely mainly on classical models may increasingly adopt newer AI approaches developed to address the annotation bottleneck. At present, however, most frontier examples remain closer to spectral annotation and disease profiling than to validated drug-discovery or drug-development workflows. A more speculative next step is post-annotation interpretation. Agentic large language model systems such as Co-Scientist (Gottweis et al., 2026) and Robin (Ghareeb et al., 2026) are not MS-omics platforms, but they show how literature- and knowledge-guided agents can generate and rank hypotheses, and in Robin's case identify candidate therapeutics. Connecting such systems to MS-based profiling is a plausible next direction, not an established practice. Even then, interpretation will be only as trustworthy as the molecular identities it builds on, so confident annotation is its foundation rather than a limit on it. For annotation specifically, near-term gains are likely to depend less on new architectures than on scale, especially foundation models pretrained on large, shared spectral resources, and those gains will be trustworthy only when identification confidence is reported honestly. By grounding AI models in confidently identified metabolites and lipids, whose signals lie close to drug action, progress in compound annotation would help move metabolomics and lipidomics from measuring what a drug has done toward more reliable, testable anticipation of what it may do.

Conclusions

In summary, among the molecular layers used in drug discovery, MS-based metabolomics and lipidomics have been among the slowest to adopt modern AI, yet they sit closest to drug action. The reason for both facts is the same. Because the metabolome and lipidome record what actually changes after a perturbation, they directly reflect a drug's pharmacodynamic response, but that same downstream position, with no closed reference of known compounds, semi-quantitative intensities, batch and drift artifacts, and small clinical cohorts, makes the data hard to model and easy to overfit. The field has responded not by adopting the largest available models indiscriminately but by fitting each method to the task it suits, and the result is an asymmetric landscape in which the newest methods have concentrated on one problem above all, compound annotation. Interpretable classical machine learning, an early and still widely applied form of AI in this field, has remained the mainstay of the metabolomic and lipidomic drug-development applications. In target and mechanism work it identifies which metabolic changes mark an effect; in safety assessment it flags injury early, where regulatory scrutiny rewards a transparent rationale; and in pharmacometabolomics it turns a pre-dose metabotype into a prediction of who will respond. What recurs across the three is a need for signatures that can be explained and checked against known biochemistry rather than opaque predictive scores, because the cohorts are small and high-dimensional, where data-intensive black-box models tend to overfit. The newer generations, deep learning and self-supervised foundation models, have concentrated mainly on the annotation bottleneck, learning to turn spectral features into more confident compound identities, while large language models have so far entered only at the edges of the workflow. Whether AI methods reach the clinic depends less on the algorithm than on the evidence beneath it. An untargeted signature can nominate a hypothesis, but a decision affecting dosing or safety generally requires transition to targeted, validated assays, controlled designs that separate a drug effect from diet, microbiome, and disease, and group-aware validation that keeps reported accuracy honest. The annotation bottleneck is a separate limit that need not be solved for the field to be useful now, because targeted, hypothesis-driven studies restrict their inferences to the metabolites they can confidently identify; it is most acute in open, hypothesis-free discovery, where the decisive feature may itself be unidentified. As spectral resources are standardized and shared, newer generations may extend more reliably into that setting. However capable these methods become, though, the honest reporting of identification confidence will remain as decisive as it is today, the discipline that keeps a metabolite signature interpretable and useful rather than overclaimed.
