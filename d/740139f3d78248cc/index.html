Abstract

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) exhibits persistently high incidence and mortality rates worldwide, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden. Early screening, early diagnosis, and early treatment can significantly improve patients’ survival rates while reducing mortality. However, there remains a lack of effective biomarkers to aid in early screening and diagnosis. As a branch of artificial intelligence, machine learning can automatically analyze large volumes of data, greatly saving human time and resources. The advancement of high-throughput sequencing technology has provided researchers with abundant gene expression data, offering rich data resources for the training and validation of machine learning models. With the development of artificial intelligence, integrating knowledge from bioinformatics, machine learning, molecular biology, and clinical medicine for analysis enables a more comprehensive understanding and exploration of the molecular biological mechanisms underlying CRC. In summary, this project aims to utilize machine learning techniques to screen five CRC signature genes (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) from GEO datasets, validate these signature genes using TCGA database, and perform RT-qPCR to detect the relative mRNA expression levels of these genes in CRC. Ultimately, this study seeks to provide novel biomolecular markers for the early diagnosis of CRC.

Introduction

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common gastrointestinal malignancies worldwide, exhibiting significant geographical and ethnic disparities in incidence and mortality. Particularly in developed nations like those in North America, CRC consistently remains a leading cause of cancer incidence. While historically lower in developing countries, CRC incidence rates are rising steadily in these regions due to economic development and lifestyle changes [1]. Since 2020, CRC has been established as one of the top five most prevalent cancers in China. The vast territory of China contributes to marked provincial and regional variations in CRC incidence, largely attributed to differences in dietary habits and lifestyles [2]. Established risk factors promoting CRC development include smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, reduced dietary fiber intake, and high-fat diets. Conversely, smoking cessation, moderation of alcohol intake, maintaining a healthy weight, and adopting a healthy lifestyle are effective preventive measures [3]. Current CRC treatment modalities encompass surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Surgical resection constitutes the primary approach, particularly offering curative potential for early-stage CRC. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy serve critical roles both as adjuvant therapy post-surgery to reduce recurrence risk and improve survival rates, and as neoadjuvant therapy prior to surgery to downstage tumors and enhance resection rates. Targeted therapies and immunotherapy play pivotal roles in improving the prognosis of advanced CRC, especially demonstrating efficacy in patients with microsatellite instability-high /mismatch repair deficient tumors [4]. Despite these diverse treatment options, a subset of patients still experiences suboptimal outcomes and low survival rates, contributing substantially to the socioeconomic burden. Undertaking research on the screening and validation of biomolecular markers for the early diagnosis of CRC is expected to significantly improve the early detection rate of CRC, provide critical evidence for early clinical intervention and treatment, and thereby effectively enhance patient prognosis while reducing disease mortality. Big data mining has emerged as a significant research focus in recent years. This approach involves the integration and analysis of data from various databases, leveraging large datasets and predictive modeling algorithms to yield meaningful results. Data mining is extensively applied in healthcare, particularly for identifying potential diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic targets in oncology using public databases and constructing relevant models [5]. Biomarkers with diagnostic and prognostic utility play vital roles in clinical practice, facilitating rapid and accurate disease diagnosis and enabling precise prognostic predictions. However, tumor heterogeneity poses a challenge for identifying molecular markers with high sensitivity and specificity. Consequently, various ML methodologies have been developed to identify patterns within big data using mathematical algorithms for specific predictions [6]. ML is now widely employed in oncology for applications such as early cancer detection, cancer type classification, feature extraction, prognosis prediction, and drug response assessment [7]. Moreover, the flexibility and capability of ML algorithms to learn underlying hidden structures make them well-suited for analyzing epigenomic data [8]. Collectively, ML plays a crucial role in the screening process for tumor biomarkers. In this study, we first identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of CRC by mining sequencing data of CRC and normal colorectal tissues from the GEO database. Subsequently, we screened out signature genes of CRC using two machine learning methods, namely LASSO regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE). These screened genes were further validated in the TCGA database. Simultaneously, we analyzed the correlations between these signature genes and tumor-infiltrating immune cells in CRC, as well as the associations between the expression of these signature genes and clinical characteristics. Finally, quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was employed to verify the expression of these signature genes at the mRNA level in CRC cell lines. Collectively, these analyses aim to fully demonstrate the value of these signature genes as diagnostic indicators for CRC.

Methods › Data acquisition and processing

Gene expression datasets GSE113513 and GSE89076 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE113513 includes 14 pairs of colorectal cancer patient tissue samples, and GSE89076 includes 40 pairs of colorectal cancer patient tissue samples. Perform quality control, standardization, and normalization of the gene expression data in the datasets to obtain gene expression matrices. Merge the data from GSE113513 and GSE89076, and then apply ComBat to correct the merged dataset, which will serve as the training set. DEGs between CRC tissues and adjacent normal colorectal tissues were identified using Perl scripts and the “Limma” package in R. Meanwhile, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs. Transcriptomic and clinical data of CRC were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as the validation set. A total of 701 CRC-related samples were available in the TCGA database, including 51 normal controls and 651 CRC samples (Table 1). Data information

Methods › Screening of CRC signature genes via LASSO and SVM-RFE

LASSO regression analysis was performed using the R package “glmnet”. The LASSO regression method is commonly used for feature selection or as a binary classifier, which achieves the compression and selection of feature coefficients by adding an L1 norm penalty term to the loss function. In this study, we utilized the feature selection capability of LASSO to identify CRC-related signature genes. A tenfold cross-validation was employed to train the model. SVM-RFE is a recursive feature elimination algorithm based on support vector machines (SVM), which constructs the optimal feature subset by iteratively removing the least important features. SVM-RFE analysis on the data was conducted using the “e1071”, “kernlab”, and “caret” packages in R.

Methods › Immune infiltrating cell correlation analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to quantify the relative infiltration proportions of 22 immune cell types between normal colorectal tissues and CRC tissues in the training set samples. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The infiltration proportion data of the 22 immune cell types were extracted, and their infiltration patterns in normal tissues versus CRC tissues were analyzed. Subsequently, correlation analysis between the expression of CRC signature genes and different tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets was performed using the ggpubr and ggExtra R packages.

Methods › External validation of signature genes using TCGA

Download transcriptome data and clinical data of colorectal cancer and normal colorectal tissues from the TCGA database. Use R language to organize the transcriptome data and clinical data, and analyze the expression of characteristic genes of colorectal cancer in the TCGA database and the prognosis of patients. Using the data from the TCGA database, ROC curves were plotted to determine the predictive accuracy of these characteristic genes. Furthermore, we used the TCGA database to conduct a correlation analysis between the expression levels of the screened disease characteristic genes and the clinical characteristics of the patients. The specific operation code has been uploaded (Supplementary 1).

Methods › Cell culture

Human CRC cell lines (RKO and HCT116) and a normal colorectal epithelial cell line (NCM460) were used in this study. The CRC cell lines (RKO and HCT116) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The normal colorectal epithelial cell line (NCM460) was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Methods › Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from human CRC cell lines (RKO and HCT116) and the normal colorectal epithelial cell line (NCM460) using TRIzol reagent. According to the manufacturer’s instructions of the reverse transcription kit, the reaction mixture was prepared on ice to reverse-transcribe the extracted RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). Specific primers were designed and synthesized based on the sequences of the target genes (Table 2). The RT-qPCR reaction mixture was prepared on ice and carefully added to a 96-well plate. The sealed plate was placed into a quantitative real-time PCR instrument, and the relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated. Primer sequences for RT-qPCR

Methods › Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Unpaired Student’s t test was employed for statistical testing, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was performed independently at least three times to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the results.

Results › Analysis of differentially expressed genes in CRC

Gene expression datasets GSE113513 and GSE89076 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Among them, GSE113513 contains 14 pairs of tissue samples from CRC patients, and GSE89076 comprises 40 pairs of CRC patient tissue samples. Quality control, standardization, and normalization were performed on the gene expression data of the datasets to obtain gene expression matrices. The data from the GSE113513 and GSE89076 datasets were merged and then corrected to serve as the training set. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CRC tissues and normal colorectal tissues were identified using Perl and the “Limma” package in R. It was found that compared with normal colorectal tissues, there were 406 DEGs in CRC tissues (Supplementary 2), including 250 downregulated DEGs and 156 upregulated DEGs (Fig. 1A). GO enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were mainly enriched in the cell apex, apical plasma membrane, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix (Fig. 1B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, viral protein interaction, and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 1C). DO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were mainly enriched in diseases including adenoma, intestinal disease, malignant ovarian surface epithelial-stromal tumor, ovarian cancer, gastric adenocarcinoma, and ovarian epithelial cancer (Fig. 1D). GSEA identified the active functions in the experimental group (Fig. 1E) and the control group (Fig. 1F). Analysis of DEGs in CRC. A Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes. Results › Immune cell infiltration in CRC

In the training cohort, the relative proportions of distinct immune cell populations varied across individual samples (Fig. 2A). Subsequent analysis revealed statistically significant differences in the infiltration levels of 14 immune cell subsets between normal colorectal tissues and colorectal carcinoma tissues in this cohort (Fig. 2B). Compared to normal colorectal tissues, carcinoma tissues exhibited significantly lower levels of B cells naive, B cells memory, plasma cells, CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ memory resting T cells, follicular helper T cells, activated NK cells, M2 macrophages, and resting mast cells. Conversely, colorectal carcinoma tissues demonstrated significantly higher levels of CD4⁺ naive T cells, CD4⁺ memory activated T cells, M0 macrophages, and activated mast cells relative to normal tissues. These findings indicate distinct compositional differences in immune cell infiltrates between colorectal carcinoma and normal colorectal tissues. Immune cell infiltration in CRC. Results › Identification of characteristic genes in CRC

LASSO regression analysis was performed on the 406 DEGs obtained above, and tenfold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal lambda value. Candidate genes were identified based on non-zero coefficients, and a total of 14 CRC signature genes, including SCGN, CLDN1, EPHX4, MMP7, ABCG2, TGFBI, USP2, LYVE1, CCL23, SLC22A18AS, GCG, KIF1A, TCN1, and FABP1 were identified (Fig. 3A). In the SVM-RFE,Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination, analysis, when the number of features was 40, the classifier error was minimal. The CRC signature genes included CLDN1, KRT80, EPHX4, SCGN, AJUBA, CDH3, CHP2, EPB41L3, HILPDA, RNF43, GUCA2B, LRRC8E, FOXQ1, PKIB, NFE2L3, GUCA2A, PLP1, ABCG2, PHLPP2, RUNDC3B, HPGD, AXIN2, AZGP1, FAM107A, KIAA1549, SLC17A4, TMEM171, GTF2IRD1, KRT20, NEBL, CA2, SCARA5, NAP1L2, DSCC1, CPNE7, USP2, TSPAN7, CRNDE, ABCA6, and TMIGD1 (Fig. 3B). By taking the intersection of the genes identified by the two machine learning methods, 5 CRC signature genes (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) were obtained (Fig. 3C). Results › External validation of the signature genes was performed using TCGA database

In the training set, the CRC signature genes ABCG2, SCGN, and USP2 were significantly downregulated in cancer tissues, while CLDN1 and EPHX4 were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4A). Transcriptomic and clinical data of CRC and normal colorectal tissues were downloaded from the TCGA database to analyze the expression patterns of the signature genes. Consistent with the results in the GEO training set, ABCG2, SCGN, and USP2 were significantly downregulated, and CLDN1 and EPHX4 were significantly upregulated in CRC tissues from the TCGA database (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, ROC curve analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) values exceeded 0.9 in both the training set (Fig. 4C) and the validation set (Fig. 4D). This demonstrates that the identified signature genes have high accuracy as diagnostic markers for CRC. Validation of Characteristic Genes in CRC. Results › Analysis of the correlation between characteristic genes and the clinical features and prognosis of CRC

Previous comprehensive analysis results indicated that the five CRC signature genes ABCG2, CLDN1, EPHX4, SCGN, and USP2 could serve as diagnostic markers for CRC and were also correlated with immune infiltrating cells in CRC. To further clarify whether these signature genes are associated with the clinical characteristics and prognosis of CRC, correlation analysis was performed using clinical data of CRC patients and expression data of the signature genes from the TCGA database. The results showed that ABCG2 expression was correlated with the N stage of patients, CLDN1 expression was associated with patient gender, and EPHX4 had no correlation with any clinical characteristics of patients. SCGN expression was correlated with patient age, T stage, and N stage, while USP2 expression was correlated with tumor T stage (Fig. 6). Survival analysis results revealed no significant correlation between the five CRC signature genes and patient prognosis (Supplementary File 3). These findings suggest that these signature genes may only have certain value in CRC diagnosis but no clear role in predicting patient prognosis. Results › RT-qPCR validation of signature genes expression in CRC cells

To validate the expression of the screened characteristic genes for CRC, we used RT-qPCR to detect the expression of five genes (ABCG2, CLDN1, EPHX4, SCGN, and USP2) in normal colorectal epithelial cells (NCM460) and CRC cell lines (RKO and HCT116). The results showed that, compared with normal colorectal epithelial cells (NCM460), the mRNA levels of ABCG2 and CLDN1 were lower in the CRC cell lines (RKO and HCT116), while the mRNA levels of EPHX4, SCGN, and USP2 were higher (Fig. 7). Among these, the expression patterns of ABCG2 and EPHX4 were consistent with the results obtained from our database analysis. However, the expression patterns of CLDN1, SCGN, and USP2 were opposite to our predictions based on the database. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that we used only one type of normal colorectal epithelial cell and two CRC cell lines for validation, and we did not perform detection at the tissue level. Discussion

The rapid economic development in China has significantly improved living standards while concurrently inducing substantial lifestyle changes, particularly the adoption of Westernized dietary patterns. These shifts have contributed to a progressive annual increase in cancer prevalence, positioning malignant tumors as a leading cause of mortality among chronic disease patients in China [9]. CRC, among the most prevalent gastrointestinal malignancies, is managed through multimodal approaches including neoadjuvant therapy, surgical resection, and adjuvant treatments. Neoadjuvant therapy (chemoradiation administered preoperatively) aims to reduce tumor bulk, downstage lesions, and enhance surgical resectability. However, not all patients exhibit therapeutic responsiveness to neoadjuvant regimens, with certain cases demonstrating limited tumor regression that may potentially delay optimal surgical intervention [10]. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for malignant neoplasms, ranging from endoscopic mucosal resection or endoscopic submucosal dissection for early-stage CRC to radical resections for advanced cases [11]. Postoperative adjuvant therapy improves survival in resectable CRC; studies by Zhong et al. demonstrate its critical role in enhancing long-term survival for patients undergoing simultaneous resection of CRC with liver metastases [12]. Despite therapeutic advancements, prognosis remains favorable only for early-stage CRC. Unfortunately, the absence of prominent symptoms in early disease stages frequently leads to late-stage diagnosis and consequently poor outcomes. Therefore, investigating specific molecular biomarkers for CRC remains imperative. Screening genes associated with disease diagnosis and prognosis based on bioinformatics, followed by validating the roles of these key molecules in cancer cells through cell biological function experiments, is a current focus of research. The GEO database is an open-access repository containing hundreds of thousands of microarray gene expression datasets [13]. In this study, two datasets (GSE113513 and GSE89076) were retrieved from the GEO database: GSE113513 includes 14 pairs of tissue samples from CRC patients, while GSE89076 comprises 40 pairs of CRC patient tissue samples. After quality control, standardization, and normalization of the gene expression data in the datasets, gene expression matrices were obtained. The combined data from GSE113513 and GSE89076 were analyzed to identify DEGs between normal colorectal tissues and CRC tissues, resulting in a total of 406 DEGs. These DEGs were subjected to two machine learning methods—LASSO regression and SVM-RFE (Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination)—to screen for signature DEGs closely associated with CRC. The intersection of the results yielded five signature genes: ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4. Subsequently, expression analysis, prognosis analysis, and ROC curve analysis of these five signature genes were performed using data from TCGA database. The results confirmed that the expression patterns of ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4 were consistent between the GEO training set and the TCGA validation set. ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the identified signature genes exhibited high accuracy as diagnostic markers for CRC, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.9 in both sets. In addition, the five genes (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) were found to be correlated with immune infiltrating cells in CRC. Correlation analysis between the signature genes and clinical characteristics of CRC revealed that ABCG2 expression was associated with patient N stage, CLDN1 expression was correlated with patient gender, and EPHX4 showed no correlation with any clinical characteristics. SCGN expression was associated with patient age, T stage, and N stage, while USP2 expression was correlated with tumor T stage. Survival analysis results indicated no significant correlation between the five CRC signature genes and patient prognosis. Upon further experimental validation of the expression of these five genes, we found that the expression patterns of ABCG2 and EPHX4 were consistent with our database analysis results. However, the expression patterns of CLDN1, SCGN, and USP2 were opposite to our predictions based on the database. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that we used only one type of normal colorectal epithelial cell line and two CRC cell lines for detection, and we did not perform validation at the tissue level. Therefore, our study suggests that these characteristic genes may have value only in the diagnosis of CRC, with no clear role in predicting patient prognosis. However, this conclusion is based solely on our analysis of database data, and their exact functions require further in-depth experimental verification. Studies have suggested that ABC transporters are associated with the development of multidrug resistance (MDR) in malignant tumors [14]. ABCG2, an ABC transporter located in the plasma membrane of cells, is essential for the ATP-dependent efflux of various endogenous and exogenous substances [15]. Current studies have confirmed that ABCG2 is significantly associated with MDR in breast cancer, and multiple studies have focused on ABCG2-targeted drugs or molecular markers to overcome this resistance [16]. In CRC, ABCG2 is also considered to be correlated with tolerance to antineoplastic drugs. Studies have verified that ABCG2 is downregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal colorectal tissues, but its expression is not associated with the prognosis of CRC patients [17]. Dysregulation of CLDN family proteins is closely linked to the occurrence of various malignant tumors [18]. Abnormal expression of CLDN family proteins leads to the loss of intercellular adhesion and aberrant changes in intercellular signaling pathways, which significantly promote the invasion, migration, and proliferation of cancer cells [19]. CLDN1 is a member of the tight junction protein family [20]. Existing studies have confirmed that CLDN1 is significantly upregulated in CRC liver metastasis [20], and relevant research indicates that CLDN1 can enhance CRC resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs [21]. Additionally, studies have reported that targeting CLDN1 may facilitate the invasion and metastasis of CRC cells [22]. The epoxide hydrolase (EHs) family comprises four members: EPHX1, EPHX2, EPHX3, and EPHX4. This family is closely associated with cellular oxidative stress levels, and its related proteins are implicated in tumorigenesis. Studies have shown that in laryngeal cancer, high EPHX4 expression correlates with poor patient prognosis, and it participates in NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, as well as promotes the proliferation, colony formation, and invasion of laryngeal cancer cells [23]. In lung adenocarcinoma, EPHX4 serves as a novel prognostic and diagnostic biomarker [24]. Research by Cao et al. has confirmed differential expression of EHs family members in CRC: EPHX1 and EPHX2 are downregulated, while EPHX3 and EPHX4 are upregulated. Moreover, the expression levels of EHs family members are associated with certain clinicopathological characteristics of CRC patients, and EPHX2 and EPHX4 are recognized as diagnostic biomarkers for CRC [25]. Secretagogin (SCGN) is a molecular marker for neuroendocrine tumors. Current studies have demonstrated its differential expression in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and its association with the prognosis of RCC patients [26]. In CRC, studies have reported that SCGN is downregulated in CRC tissues compared with normal colorectal tissues, and overexpression of SCGN increases the apoptosis of CRC cells [27]. USP2 is a member of the ubiquitin-specific protease (USP) family. It can remove ubiquitin moieties from substrate proteins, inducing their deubiquitination and thereby preventing ubiquitination-mediated degradation. Furthermore, USP2 is involved in the proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance of various tumor cells, and multiple studies have validated its potential as a therapeutic target for malignant tumors [28]. USP2 can also stabilize the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells, promoting tumor immune escape; thus, it may serve as a potential target for tumor immunotherapy [29]. Relevant studies have suggested that small-molecule inhibitors of USP2 can accelerate the degradation of cell cycle-related proteins, thereby regulating the cell cycle of CRC cells [30]. Based on existing research findings and our study results, the five genes (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) play crucial roles in tumorigenesis and progression. Meanwhile, relevant studies on these genes in CRC further confirm their potential value as diagnostic biomarkers for CRC. In summary, current relevant research and our findings suggest that the five molecules (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) may be characteristic molecules associated with CRC. These molecules may hold potential clinical application value as diagnostic biomarkers for CRC. However, it is important to note that this study was primarily based on database analysis and only preliminarily validated the expression of these five molecules in colorectal cancer cells using RT-qPCR, without further functional experiments at the cellular level. Therefore, this research has certain limitations. Based on these findings, we plan to conduct further experimental validation at the cellular level to elucidate the functional roles of ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4 in CRC, thereby providing deeper insights into the roles of these key molecules in CRC pathogenesis.

Conclusion

Through screening of GEO datasets, external validation using TCGA database, and experimental verification via RT-qPCR, this study identified five genes (ABCG2, SCGN, USP2, CLDN1, and EPHX4) that exhibit differential expression in CRC tissues and possess favorable diagnostic value. These molecules may serve as potential molecular markers for the early diagnosis of CRC, providing novel candidate markers for CRC diagnosis.
