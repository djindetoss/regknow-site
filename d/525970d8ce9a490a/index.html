Abstract

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the capability of a large language model (LLM) for performing each of the steps of clinical practice guideline development from framing a healthcare question to creating the evidence-to-decision framework. The LLM tool used for this study was OpenAI Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT)-4o. This evaluation of an LLM was conducted concomitantly with development of a clinical practice guideline on neuromuscular blockade in adults with acute respiratory distress syndrome for the Society of Critical Care Medicine. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) handbook provided the steps of the process that served as the outline for this evaluation of GPT-4o. Each request for information posed to the LLM was performed during or soon after the same period as the respective step of the process being conducted by the guideline panel. The results follow the major sections of the GRADE process: framing the healthcare question and selecting and rating the importance of outcomes, summarizing the evidence and quality of evidence, and going from evidence to recommendations. The LLM demonstrated the most usefulness for the initial step of the guideline development process that involved framing the healthcare question and selecting and rating outcomes. The limitations of the LLM became most apparent during the remaining steps of the development process.

Text

Authors of a comprehensive narrative review evaluating areas of healthcare most likely impacted by artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and clinical decision-making ultimately derived consensus on five general areas: risk stratification, patient outcome optimization, early warning of acute decompensation, potential bias in machine learning, and future medical training (1). Another evaluation of AI in clinical practice found uses to support the diagnoses and treatment of disease in virtually every medical specialty, albeit with recognized limitations such as the integrity of generated data and ethical concerns (2). A relatively unstudied opportunity for AI applications is in the development of clinical practice guidelines given the capability of AI to analyze large amounts of data in real-time (3). Clinical practice guidelines are used worldwide as exemplified by the Guidelines International Network library that has links to over 3000 guidelines with a registry of others being planned or in development (4). The AI literature evaluating the use of natural language processing (understanding language) and large language models (LLMs) for generating text in guideline development has largely focused on automating the reviewing process associated with systematic reviews: article classification and screening, data extraction, and quality assessment (5). In 2024, a scoping review of 8899 screened records conducted by Guidelines International Network found no articles pertaining to the use of AI in the guideline development process (6). Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the capability of an LLM for performing each of the steps of clinical practice guideline development from framing a healthcare question to creating the evidence-to-decision framework.

METHODS

For this evaluation, institutional review board was not necessary (and thus no number was assigned) because it did not fall under the board’s guidelines as human subjects research. This evaluation was conducted concomitantly with development of the clinical practice guideline titled, “Sustained Neuromuscular Blockade in the Adult Critically Ill Patient,” as approved by the Board of Regents of the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) (7). The overlap was important given the ongoing improvements to LLM tools that could change the findings if the evaluation was conducted distant to the guideline development process. Selection of the guideline co-chairs occurred in June 2023 shortly after SCCM had promulgated a new guideline development process, which included limiting the number of Patient, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) questions to five and shortening the development timeline to 18 months. The Board gave permission to perform this ancillary evaluation and approved submission of the resulting article for potential publication. The LLM tool used for this study was OpenAI Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT)-4o. GPT-4o is estimated to have 1 trillion parameters comprising its neural networks, which is approximately six times that of GPT-3 (8). The queries of the LLM were largely conducted following subgroup assignments and initial literature searches with most of the full panel evaluation processes beginning in earnest in the latter part of 2024 and ending with the submission of the guidelines for publication in September 2025. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) handbook provided the steps of the process that served as the outline for this evaluation of GPT-4o (9). Each request for information posed to GPT-4o was performed during or soon after the same period as the respective steps of the GRADE process being conducted by the guideline panel. The comparisons between the guideline development group and GPT-4o were largely qualitative or thematic in nature. The following results of this evaluation are listed in order of the major sections of the GRADE process beginning with framing the healthcare question and selecting and rating the importance of outcomes. This is followed by a section summarizing the evidence and quality of evidence. The final section is going from evidence to recommendations.

RESULTS › Framing the Healthcare Question and Selecting and Rating Outcomes

Framing of the healthcare question involved PICO formatting as recommended by GRADE. Guideline panel members decided to focus the five PICO questions on the acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) so that was the focus of the request to GPT-4o. Therefore, the first request of GPT-4o was to, “Develop a list of the 5 most important PICO questions when considering the use of neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) for patients with ARDS admitted to intensive care units.” eTable 1 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B623) lists the comparative responses by GPT-4o and the guideline panel. The PICO questions by GPT-4o had more qualifiers based on ARDS severity, timing of NMBA administration, and specific outcomes. The panel’s questions included more details on adjunctive analgesia/sedation monitoring and a question related to NMBA in conjunction with proning. There was substantial similarity between GPT-4o and the panel in the outcome measures chosen for each PICO question, although there were a few exceptions such as GPT-4o including the surrogate measure of oxygenation and an outcome specific to cost effectiveness (eTable 1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B623).

RESULTS › Summarizing the Evidence and Quality of Evidence

This step of the GRADE approach is based on the findings of a comprehensive literature review. eTable 2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B623) shows the response given by GPT-4o when queried about a search. The inability of the GPT-4o to perform a live literature search or access external databases directly is a current limitation. Instead, GPT-4o provided suggestions for where and how to perform such a search. eTable 3 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B623) lists the GPT-4o response to providing a GRADE summary of findings and assessing certainty/quality of evidence. Only the panel’s PICO question number 1 and related outcomes served as the basis for this request, which included studies found by the methodologists’ literature search. This approach allowed for more ready comparisons to the panel’s findings. The GPT-4o response acknowledges the possible lack of a complete body of evidence and refers to the need for a comprehensive meta-analysis to draw definitive conclusions. Additionally, GPT-4o provides only a simplified version of a GRADE table.

RESULTS › Going From Evidence to Recommendations

The inability of GPT-4o to appropriately summarize the evidence and quality of evidence precluded any assessment of strength of recommendations as strong or weak with attendant justifications.

RESULTS › Implementation Strategies

Development of implementation strategies is not a specific step in GRADE methodology. However, an implementation specialist provided by SCCM developed such strategies in conjunction with a subgroup of volunteer panel members. eTable 4 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B623) provides the GPT-4o response to the question, “Design an implementation strategy for the use of neuromuscular blocking agents in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome using the principles of implementation science.” The response does not contain the kind of detailed implementation strategy developed by the panel’s implementation group but rather lists general statements about the pursuit of implementation strategies.

DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY

As noted in the results, GPT-4o demonstrated the most usefulness for the initial step of the guideline development process involving framing the healthcare question and selecting and rating outcomes. However, even for this aspect of the process there were differences noted in the specific PICO formulations between GPT-4o and the guideline panel. This divergence is not unexpected since the panel members are deriving their choices from their practice settings that consider factors beyond published literature. The panel members are experienced clinicians whose decision-making reflects clinical judgment taking into account the nuances of contemporary patient care. The inability of GPT-4o to provide meaningful summaries of evidence, assessment of quality of evidence, and high-level implementation content are inherent structural limitations since these issues rely heavily on data interpretation informed by stakeholder-specific factors. For example, the GRADE approach allows guideline developers to take into account bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias that can reduce the assessment of quality. On the flip side, developers using the GRADE approach can also increase quality assessment if there is a large magnitude of effect, a well-recognized dose-response gradient (i.e., increased dose yields increased response), or rigorous studies that consider plausible confounders. Current LLM technologies have problems making such inferences and could yield hallucinations if prompted to try to do so. In a recent publication, a panel of the Guidelines International Network describes principles for use of AI from guideline planning to appraisal of recommendations, referred to as the “guideline enterprise” (6). The panel noted their lack of detailed guidance for using specific AI tools but identified eight general principles: transparency, preplanning, additionality, credibility, ethics, accountability, compliance, and evaluation. There was a summarization of each of these principles in conjunction with examples of actions for implementation. There was also reference to reporting checklists for trials involving AI and tools to aid in the performance of systematic reviews. The study cited as support for the latter use involved an AI tool intended to “streamline and accelerate literature reviews” (10). These are forms of hybrid architecture that can integrate an LLM such as combining GPT-4o and a specialized retrieval method like a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system (11). Such specialized tools have the potential to improve the literature search and evaluation process in guideline development but were not purchased for the purpose of this evaluation. As part of this evaluation, GPT-4o was queried to describe its capacity for developing clinical practice guidelines, the response was that it “helps ensure they are more accurate, comprehensive, and up-to-date.” There were six general areas listed as examples to support this response: analyzing large amounts of data, automating the extraction and synthesis of information for systematic reviews, predicting outcomes for different treatment options by analyzing historical patient data, personalizing information for subgroups of patients or even individuals, help with updating guidelines, and consensus-building process by summarizing expert opinions and facilitating communication among guideline development panels. In contrast to the sanguine response by GPT-4o, this evaluation of the LLM to perform the steps of clinical practice guideline development demonstrates a variety of shortcomings that limits its potential utility to specific steps of the development process. Such shortcomings appear to be more reflective of generative models’ tendency toward generalized claims of capability rather than a specific mismatch unique to the issues in this evaluation. An inherent limitation when using any LLM for guideline development is the subjective nature of selected components of the decision-making process. This subjectivity is exemplified by the variability in responses by guideline panel members compared with those of GPT-4o for the outcome measures for each PICO question. Another example is the detailed implementation strategy developed by the panel’s implementation group compared with the broad recommendations by GPT-4o. This is not surprising given the subjective nature of aspects of the implementation development process that reflect the viewpoints of panel members besides implementation design principles noted in published literature. However, as part of an effective implementation strategy, LLMs have demonstrated promise for providing plain language summaries of evidence-based reviews to patients (12). This evaluation of GPT-4o provides several directions for future research related to guideline development beginning with systematic assessments of different prompting strategies to obtain the desired information. Other investigations could include comparisons of different LLMs, evaluations of hybrid architecture such as combining LLMs with RAG systems, and evaluations of the usefulness of LLMs with or without hybrid architecture to assist in the updating of clinical practice guidelines. In conclusion, GPT-4o demonstrated the most usefulness for the initial step of the guideline development process that involved framing the healthcare question and selecting and rating outcomes. The limitations of the LLM became most apparent during the next step of the development process, summarizing the evidence and quality of evidence, given its inability to perform a live literature search or access external databases. This precluded a GRADE summary of findings or an assessment of the certainty and quality of evidence, which is one of the most labor-intensive aspects of guideline development for the methodologists and other panel members that leads to final recommendations. There is the possibility that a series of specific questions to GPT-4o might have provided more detailed information for this step in the GRADE process, but that remains a subject for future investigations. Additionally, it is likely that some of the limitations of the LLM noted in this study will be overcome with ongoing advancements in AI technologies.
