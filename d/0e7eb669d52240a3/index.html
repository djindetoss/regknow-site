Abstract

Model-informed drug development (MIDD) has traditionally been guided by hypothesis-driven modeling, in which models are constructed based on established biological mechanisms, physicochemical principles, and experimental hypotheses. More recently, the rise of artificial intelligence has enabled data-driven modeling approaches that often bypass explicit mechanistic assumptions. The potential synergy between these two paradigms is reshaping strategies for implementing MIDD. This study aims to compare deep-learning-driven and hypothesis-driven approaches, highlighting their respective strengths, limitations, and opportunities for integration. A case study is presented with the re-analysis of warfarin PK and PK/PD using both modeling paradigms. The comparative PK analysis indicated that the deep-learning model achieved predictive performance comparable to, and numerically slightly better than, hypothesis-driven modeling based on a one-compartment PK model. The hypothesis-based PK/PD analysis was conducted using three model structures: direct effect, effect compartment, and indirect response. The results of these analyses, including the estimated clinical dose, were compared with those obtained from the deep-learning approach. The deep-learning model demonstrated predictive performance similar to that of the effect compartment and indirect response models and yielded a comparable estimate of the effective dose across the two modeling approaches. A simulation-based sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the dose selection derived from the deep-learning-based modeling approach. The outcomes of the analysis suggest that effective implementation of the MIDD paradigm may be enhanced through the complementary use of deep-learning-based approaches alongside established hypothesis-driven, mechanistic models, thereby supporting evidence-based drug development and regulatory decision-making.

INTRODUCTION

Model‐informed drug development (MIDD) has emerged as a pivotal framework in contemporary pharmaceutical research, designed to enhance the efficiency and success rate of the drug development process. By leveraging quantitative modeling and simulation approaches, MIDD integrates diverse data sources, including preclinical studies, clinical trials, and real‐world evidence, to support informed decision‐making throughout a drug's entire life cycle. By leveraging mathematical and statistical models, MIDD facilitates a deeper understanding of a drug's pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, efficacy, and safety profiles. 1 , 2 This approach supports dose selection, trial design, and regulatory evaluation, ultimately contributing to more efficient and evidence‐based development strategies. The hypothesis‐driven (HD) and deep‐learning‐driven (DD) strategies represent two complementary approaches for implementing the MIDD paradigm, each providing unique strengths and opportunities that can enhance decision‐making in drug development. Hypothesis‐driven modeling is usually based on: compartmental pharmacokinetics models, mechanistic models connecting pharmacokinetics to clinical response, physiologically based pharmacokinetics, quantitative systems pharmacology, receptor‐occupancy models, indirect response models, etc. These models are built using known physiology, biochemical kinetics, anatomy, etc., often via ordinary differential equations. The key benefits of this modeling approach are the interpretability, the ability to extrapolate (species scaling, pediatric dosing, organ impairment, and disease state shifts) the possibility to integrate prior biological knowledge. Obviously, the implementation of these models requires: (a) good prior knowledge (mechanism, clearance pathways, tissue distribution, receptor kinetics, etc.) and (b) resource‐intensive with frequent issues in the identification (parameter estimation) frequently associated with the sparse data available. Furthermore, this modeling approach faces the risk of employing misspecified mechanisms, which may lead to misleading predictions, as well as potential oversimplification arising from incomplete or missing knowledge. Data‐driven modeling refers to artificial‐intelligence‐based methodologies that rely on raw data rather than on mechanistic assumptions. Methods range from logistic models, random forests, support vector machines, ensemble methods, to more advanced techniques such as deep learning. Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning that uses artificial neural networks (ANN) to automatically learn representations from data. Among the relevant features of deep learning, the ability to handle large and complex datasets, the possibility of identifying covariates or predictors not considered in mechanistic thinking, and the rapid hypothesis generation and screening of dose–response and exposure–response relationships. The deep‐learning models have been shown to outperform traditional machine learning algorithms without requiring explicit feature selection. This finding is supported by the universal approximation theorem stating that a feedforward deep‐learning model can approximate any continuous function with an arbitrary degree of accuracy by adjusting the weights of the connections within the network. 3 The objective of this paper was to compare the performances of a deep learning versus a hypothesis‐driven modeling approaches in the analysis of a clinical pharmacology study conducted to establish a PK/PD relationship and to assess a criteria for clinical dose selection using plasma warfarin concentrations and prothrombin complex activity (PCA) in 32 subjects treated with warfarin sodium at the dose of 1.5 mg/kg of body weight.

Methods

The nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling approach, as implemented in the NONMEM software (version 7.5.1, ICON Development Solutions), was used in the analysis of the hypothesis‐driven models. This approach characterizes typical model parameters at the population level while simultaneously quantifying inter‐individual variability and residual unexplained variability. In contrast, deep‐learning approach directly predicts observed data without modeling hierarchical effects. The methodology used in the analysis of the deep‐learning model was based on the ANN approach. The ANN is a subset of machine learning methodology that utilizes multilayered neural networks, called deep neural networks, to imitate the complex decision‐making power of human brains. It is based on an ANN architecture that employs layers of interconnected nodes known as neurons working together to process and learn from input data. The basic operational principle of ANN lies in selecting a set of the connection weights of each node in the neural network appropriate for optimizing the predictions. The two key processes associated with neural networks are feedforward and backpropagation. Feedforward consists of layers of neurons where information moves in only one direction, forward, from the input layer, through hidden layers, to the output layer. There are no cycles or loops, hence the term “feedforward.” The second process, usually referred to as backpropagation, is a learning algorithm used for training neural networks by optimizing the weights of the connections between neurons. Backpropagation works by minimizing the error between the actual output and the predicted output of the neural network by adjusting the connection weights of the network. Feedforward and backpropagation work simultaneously during the training phase of a neural network. The network initially makes predictions using the feedforward process. These predictions are then compared to the actual target values and the error is calculated. Backpropagation optimizes the predictions by minimizing this error using updated weights of the interconnected nodes. In the current analysis, the supervised learning methodology and the resilient backpropagation method were used to optimize the backpropagation algorithm to train neural networks. 4 A schematic of feedforward error–backpropagation neural network with n nodes in the input layer, m nodes in the hidden layer, and r nodes in the output layer is presented in Figure 1. ANN analysis was conducted using the “neuralnet” library available in the statistical software R (Version 4.4.1 for Windows R project, http://www.r‐project.org/). The choice of an ANN model was motivated by the universal approximation theorem, which states that a feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer and a sufficient number of neurons can approximate any continuous function to an arbitrary degree of accuracy through appropriate adjustment of its connection weights. 3 , 5 In the present analysis, the ANN model with a single hidden layer was used to approximate the underlying continuous relationships describing the warfarin plasma concentration–time profile and the associated pharmacodynamic response, characterized by longitudinal PCA. In addition, the same theoretical foundation supports the use of the ANN to model the continuous mapping between covariates and response, without requiring an explicit parametric specification of the functional form. Methods › Data

The PK/PD analysis dataset included plasma warfarin concentrations and PCA measurements evaluated in 32 subjects after a single oral dose of warfarin sodium, 1.5 mg/kg of body weight. Measurements were made each 12 or 24 h. The warfarin concentrations describing total racemic warfarin plasma concentrations (250 measurements) and PCA (232 values) in 32 subjects were obtained from publications by O'Reilly et al. 6 , 7 The analysis dataset included men (85%) and women (15%), with age ranging from 21 to 63 years, and body weight ranging from 40 to 102 kg. Warfarin is an oral anticoagulant commonly prescribed for the prevention and treatment of thrombosis and thromboembolism. It works by inhibiting the synthesis of vitamin K‐dependent clotting factors, thereby reducing the formation of blood clots within blood vessels. The rapid absorption and slow metabolism of warfarin sodium and the prolonged biological effect from a single dose suggested that drug levels adequate for therapeutic response could be achieved without a large loading dose. 8 A decrease in PCA to less than 35% of normal values within 48–96 h was deemed clinically relevant. 7 Model development focused on characterizing the relationship between warfarin pharmacokinetics and the time course of PCA. Using visualization tools and model performance comparisons, both HD and deep DD approaches were iteratively developed and refined from their initial frameworks. Comprehensive diagnostic analyses were conducted to ensure the accuracy, robustness, and reliability of the resulting PK/PD models.

Methods › Model selection

Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to assess the relative quality and discrimination between alternative models. All models evaluated in the analysis (HD and DD) were classified as non‐nested models. Therefore, model discrimination between alternative models was performed using the AIC and the BIC. 9 , 10 The AIC is aimed to estimate the relative quality of different models while penalizing for model complexity. The purpose of AIC is to find a model that maximizes the likelihood of the data while taking into account the number of parameters used. The AIC value was estimated as: AIC = −2 * log(L) + 2 * k, where L represents the likelihood of the model, which measures how well the model fits the data and k represents the number of parameters in the model. Similar to AIC, the BIC criterion is based on Bayesian principles and provides a more stronger penalty for model complexity compared to AIC. The BIC value was estimated as: BIC = −2 * log(L) + k * log(n), where L and k have the same meaning as in AIC. Additionally, the term log(n) represents the logarithm of the sample size (n). The log(n) term in BIC introduces a stronger penalty for model complexity compared to AIC, as the penalty term scales with the sample size. Lower AIC and BIC values indicate a better model. A difference of 2 or less is generally considered negligible, while differences larger than 10 provide strong evidence in favor of the model with the lower AIC/BIC values. The AIC and BIC criteria were computed using an approximated estimate of the log‐likelihood value in the DD analysis. 11 In the HD analysis, the AIC and BIC criteria were computed using the log‐likelihood values estimated using the objective function value provided by NONMEM. In addition to the AIC and the BIC values, goodness‐of‐fit plots were provided to assess the model performances.

Methods › Model Development

The HD and the DD models were developed using a sequential approach: the PK data were initially analyzed then a metrics of the individual PK exposure (i.e., the area under the individual model predicted PK concentrations curve), was estimated using a non‐compartmental approach. 12 Finally, the PK/PD analysis was conducted on the PCA data using the measure of the individual PK exposure as covariate. The HD modeling was conducted using the stochastic approximation expectation maximization with the interaction computational algorithm. The −2 log‐likelihood value at the final model parameter estimates was calculated using the importance sampling approach. Based on previously published analyses, 13 the PK model used in the HD analysis was a one‐compartment linear model with first‐order absorption, elimination, and a lag time for the absorption. According to the same reference, the PK/PD model in the HD analysis was conducted assuming three alternative assumptions: Direct‐response model where effect is driven by the plasma drug concentration in the central compartment Cp(t).Effect‐compartment model where effect is driven by the drug concentration in a hypothetical effect compartment. In this compartment the drug concentration Ce(t) equilibrates with the plasma concentration Cp(t) with a first‐order rate constant.Turnover model where drug effect arise through indirect mechanisms. According to this modeling approach, the drug can influence the factors that control either the input or production of the response variable (kin), leading to inhibition or stimulation, or it can alter the determinants of loss (kout) of the response variable, likewise through inhibition or stimulation. The best performing model was selected using the lowest values of the AIC and BIC criteria. The implementation of the deep‐learning approach using an ANN model required the specification of two hyperparameters defining the network topology: the number of hidden layers and the number of nodes in each hidden layer. Assuming that a single hidden layer was sufficient to characterize the model, a grid search was performed to identify the optimal number of nodes. The decision to use only one hidden layer was supported by the universal approximation theorem, which states that a feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer containing a finite number of neurons can approximate any continuous function, given appropriate activation functions and weights. 3 A grid search was initially conducted to evaluate the ability of alternative models based on different number of nodes to describe the data. The optimal number of nodes was determined using the lowest values of the AIC and BIC criteria. Methods › Comparison HD and DD Modeling Performances

The performance of the nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling approach, used to implement the hypothesis‐based strategy, and the DD‐based MIDD method, representing the data‐driven strategy, was evaluated and compared using three metrics: root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2). 14 RMSE is sensitive to large deviations and therefore emphasizes models that better minimize substantial prediction errors, whereas MAE quantifies the average magnitude of errors and is more robust to outliers. Both metrics are expressed in the same units as the observed data, facilitating intuitive interpretation. R2 is scale free and reflects the proportion of variance in the observations explained by the model; however, it does not directly quantify prediction error. In this PK prediction study, RMSE and MAE were used as the primary metrics for assessing predictive accuracy, with R2 reported to provide complementary context. Collectively, these metrics assess how well the models reproduce observed concentration–time data rather than their ability to recover true PK parameters or explicitly account for measurement noise. The performance of different neural network models were evaluated using the AIC, BIC, RMSE, MAE, and R2 criteria. For the DD models, AIC and BIC were calculated using an approximate negative log‐likelihood value based on the total number of trainable parameters (i.e., the weight associated with the interconnected nodes). 11 Given that the asymptotic assumptions of AIC and BIC may not be fully satisfied for DD models, unlike HD models, additional performance metrics (RMSE, MAE, and R2) were included to provide a more robust assessment of model performance.

Methods › Exposure–Response

The definition of the clinically effective dose was guided by a clinically relevant prothrombin complex activity (%PCA) reduction threshold, defined as %PCA remaining below 35% at 24 h post‐dose. 7 Accordingly, the objective of the PK/PD modeling was to identify an oral dose that yields systemic exposure sufficient to achieve this clinically meaningful pharmacodynamic response. The results of the PK/PD analysis were used to estimate the exposure necessary for achieving a clinically effective response (i.e., a reduction of %PCA >35%). At this purpose, the relationship between the individual model predicted warfarin AUCinf and the individual %PCA reduction was evaluated using the HD and the DD modeling.

Methods › Sensitivity Analysis

A simulation‐based sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the dose selection derived from the deep‐learning‐based modeling approach. This analysis was primarily aimed at assessing the robustness of the resulting decisions, not at further optimizing the model structure. Accordingly, model outcomes were simulated by perturbing the reference dose of 1.5 mg/kg by ±10%.

Results › PK/PD Modeling

The deep‐learning analysis was conducted using a two‐step strategy. Step 1: A reference model was developed without inclusion of any exposure‐related covariate. A grid search was conducted to determine the optimal number of nodes in the ANN model. The AIC and BIC criteria were used to compare the performances of alternative models based on different numbers of nodes. Specifically, the results were as follows: 6 nodes, AIC = 1863.1 and BIC = 1928.6; 7 nodes, AIC = 1610.1 and BIC = 1685.9; and 8 nodes, AIC = 1585.3 and BIC = 1671.4. Based on the optimality criterion of minimizing both AIC and BIC, the ANN model one‐layer model with 7 nodes was selected as the best performing model. As the individual‐predicted concentrations were shown to properly describe the PK time course of warfarin, in step 2, the, individual AUCinf values derived from the pharmacokinetic analysis were used as covariate to assess the potential impact of the systemic exposure on the clinical effective response (i.e., %PCA <35%). The results of the grid search conducted to define the ANN model structure were as follows: for 5 nodes, AIC = 2104.2 and BIC = 2176.6; for 6 nodes, AIC = 1146.2 and BIC = 1232.4; and for 7 nodes, AIC = 1703.1 and BIC = 1803.1. Based on the optimality criterion of minimizing both AIC and BIC, the ANN one‐layer model with 6 nodes was selected as the most performant final model. The comparison of the AIC and BIC values estimated in step 1 (AIC = 1610.1, BIC = 1685.9) and step 2 (AIC = 1146.2, BIC = 1232.4) indicated that final model accounting for the individual exposure provided a superior description of the PCA data. Discussion

The objective of the present analysis was to demonstrate the potential interest of a deep‐learning‐based methodology and to compare its performance with that of a conventional hypothesis‐driven modeling approach in MIDD. This work aims to contribute to the advancement of model‐informed strategies designed to enhance the efficiency of drug development. As a case study, the PK and the exposure–response relationship of warfarin was re‐analyzed employing both modeling approaches to illustrate their respective strengths, limitations, opportunities, and potential impact. The results of this comparative analysis indicate that the hypothesis‐driven models provide mechanistic transparency and regulatory acceptance but are constrained by biological knowledge gaps and limited scalability. Deep‐learning models deliver superior predictive performance with potential lack of interpretability and reproducibility. Among the advantages of a deep‐learning approach, we can mention the ability to: (a) capture nonlinear relationships without explicit feature engineering, (b) learn directly from raw data, (c) be scalable to large and high‐dimensional datasets, and (d) integrate multimodal data sources (such as text, images, and omics data). As the landscape of pharmaceutical research continues to evolve, the future of MIDD lies in the integration of advanced methodologies such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, and real‐world data, across the entire development continuum. 15 In this context, recent FDA draft guidance underscores the potential of AI and ML in regulatory decision‐making, reflecting an increasing openness from regulatory agencies to adopt and leverage these transformative technologies. 16 Recently, hybrid deep‐learning frameworks have emerged as a new generation of modeling paradigms that integrate data‐driven neural architectures with mechanistic‐, physical‐, or knowledge‐based models. 17 By combining the strengths of data‐driven inference with established scientific knowledge, these approaches achieve both high predictive accuracy and enhanced biological interpretability. While deep‐learning models excel at capturing complex nonlinear patterns, they often operate as “black boxes,” offering limited interpretability and uncertain extrapolation capabilities. In contrast, mechanistic models, such as PK/PD or systems biology models, explicitly encode established biological relationships but struggle to represent unknown or data‐driven effects. The key challenge in developing novel methodologies lies in designing hybrid deep‐learning frameworks that combine the expressiveness of deep networks with the interpretability and theoretical grounding of mechanistic models. Such integration is expected to yield models that are both scientifically consistent and empirically powerful. Among the known limitations of the deep‐learning approach we can mention the: (a)low interpretability with problems in extrapolation beyond the domain of observed data (e.g., different doses, populations, or disease states), (b) the risk of overfitting, (c) the dependence on data quantity, quality, and representativeness, and (d) the limited mechanistic insight for safety/risk predictions. However, the high potential of this approach has been highlighted in recent reviews indicating that it offers a promising alternative in pharmacometrics, particularly for uncovering complex dependencies and capturing the indirect influence of predictors. 18 Recently, a novel deep‐learning‐based methodology grounded in the neural ordinary differential equations (NODE) framework has been proposed. 19 , 20 Similar to the DD methodology, this approach is not intended to replace traditional mechanism‐based modeling, but rather to serve as a complementary tool for the analysis of pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data. Unlike conventional deep neural networks that rely on stacking many layers, a NODE learns a function that describes the rate of change of the system over time. The model then uses a differential equation solver to integrate this rate of change, effectively transforming the input into the output. Therefore, the implementation of NODE, requires an explicit mathematical formulation of the system in the form of differential equations. For example, the model used to describe the warfarin dataset required dedicated software (e.g., Monolix or NONMEM), the specification of a system of three differential equations, and the use of 96 parameters. 20 The principal limitations of the NODE approach include the high computational cost associated with numerically solving the differential equations, as well as the increased implementation complexity compared with the proposed DD methodology. Moreover, the NODE methodology differs fundamentally from the DD approach in that DD does not require specification of an underlying differential‐equation‐based structural model. Given that both DD and NODE demonstrated comparable goodness‐of‐fit to hypothesis‐driven, mechanism‐based modeling approaches, these methodologies may be viewed as complementary alternative frameworks for the analysis and characterization of PK and PK/PD data. 14 , 20 The outcomes of the present analysis suggest that effective implementation of MIDD paradigm may be enhanced through the complementary use of deep‐learning‐based approaches alongside established hypothesis‐driven, mechanistic models, thereby supporting evidence‐based drug development and regulatory decision‐making. Deep‐learning methods offer powerful capabilities for extracting complex, high‐dimensional patterns from large and heterogeneous datasets, enabling improved prediction, feature discovery, and data integration across preclinical and clinical domains. In contrast, hypothesis‐based models grounded in pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and systems biology principles provide mechanistic interpretability, causal reasoning, and regulatory transparency. By combining these complementary methodologies, hybrid models can leverage data‐driven learning to inform, parameterize, or augment mechanistic structures while preserving biological plausibility and interpretability. Such an integrated approach enhances predictive performance, supports decision‐making across the drug development life cycle, and aligns with regulatory expectations for robust, explainable, and reproducible MIDD applications.
