Abstract

Ovarian cancer (OC) remains therapeutic challenge due to its complex molecular heterogeneity and therapy-induced adaptive resistance. While non-apoptotic cell death and senescence pathways contribute to tumor evolution and immunosuppression, their integration into predictive models for multi-target drug design and immunotherapy optimization is underexplored. Machine learning was used to identify key genes that governing cell death and senescence (CDS). The resulting Cell Death and Senescence Learning Signature (CDSLS) was validated across multiple OC cohorts (n = 1858) and immunotherapy datasets. Multi-omics analyses, including single-cell RNA sequencing, were used to map the tumor microenvironment and identify conserved therapeutic targets. Functional validation of the hub gene RB1 included in vitro and in vivo experiments to assess its role in senescence, DNA damage, and T-cell activation. Patients with high scores predicting poor survival and immunosuppression. Knocking down RB1 promoted proliferation and suppressed senescence, while overexpression induced senescence, amplified DNA damage signaling, and enhanced CD8+ T cell activation. In vivo, RB1-overexpressing tumors showed restrained growth and elevated immune infiltration. Targeted affinity small molecule compounds (e.g., ZINC001175043471) were predicted using artificial intelligence tools to target RB1. Drug sensitivity analysis linked CDSLS to differential responses to brivanib, azacitidine, and other agents. Our framework supports the use of AI in identifying conserved binding sites, predicting mutational escape, and provide a basis for future analysis for OC.

Introduction

OC ranks as a primary contributor to deaths associated with gynecological cancers across the globe, with an estimated 239,000 new diagnoses and 152,000 fatalities each year1. High-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), classified as a type of epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), is the most common and aggressive variant of this disease, often originating in the fallopian tubes2. The lack of early symptoms typically leads to late-stage diagnosis, contributing to poor prognosis and limited survival rates. Despite advancements in surgical and chemotherapeutic treatments, including platinum-based chemotherapy and taxanes, many patients experience recurrence and develop resistance, complicating treatment outcomes3,4. Promising results have been observed with targeted treatments, including poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, particularly in individuals possessing BRCA mutations or exhibiting homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)5. However, relapse and resistance remain significant challenges. Moreover, immunotherapy has emerged as a potential treatment modality, although its effectiveness has been hindered by tumor heterogeneity and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). The tumor heterogeneity is not only evident in its genetic and phenotypic diversity but also in the complex regulation of various cell death modalities occurring within the tumor microenvironment6,7. Beyond apoptosis, emerging forms of regulated cell death—including necroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis—play critical roles in tumor biology, influencing progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response8,9. In ovarian cancer, apoptotic evasion is frequently mediated by the upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2, which counteract pro-apoptotic signals and promote cell survival. This evasion is primarily due to the upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins, which counteract pro-apoptotic signals10,11. Meanwhile, autophagy, regulated by key players like Beclin-1, can function as a double-edged sword, supporting cancer cell adaptation under metabolic stress while also triggering autophagic cell death under certain conditions. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death governed by redox regulators like GPX4, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target, particularly in tumors with high oxidative stress. Similarly, necroptosis, orchestrated by kinases such as RIPK1 and RIPK3, can induce immunogenic cell death but may also contribute to inflammatory tumor progression. Autophagy supports cancer cell survival under hypoxic and nutrient-scarce conditions, while ferroptosis, driven by lipid peroxidation, is emerging as a potential therapeutic target12,13. The roles of these cell death pathways in ovarian cancer are highly context-dependent, influenced by genetic background, tumor microenvironmental cues, and therapeutic interventions. Understanding the dynamic interplay and regulatory switches among these pathways is essential for developing strategies to overcome adaptive resistance and enhance treatment efficacy. Cellular senescence, an irreversible cell cycle arrest, is closely linked to death mechanisms and can be triggered by stressors such as DNA damage, oncogenic signaling, or therapeutic interventions14,15. Cellular senescence represents another critical and dynamic process in cancer biology, characterized by an irreversible cell cycle arrest. Its role is fundamentally dual: while serving as a potent tumor-suppressive mechanism that halts the proliferation of damaged or pre-malignant cells, it can also promote tumor progression through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)16. The SASP contributes to tumor progression by enhancing cancer cell survival, promoting metastasis, and facilitating immune evasion17. Senescent cells engage in complex interactions with the immune system. While they can activate innate immune responses, including the recruitment of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells, they can also influence adaptive immunity18,19. Recent research suggests that senescent tumor cells can effectively activate dendritic cells (DCs) and CD8+ T cells, both of which are essential for initiating robust anti-tumor immune responses20. The context of its induction is key. Oncogene-induced senescence (OIS) acts as an intrinsic anti-cancer barrier early in tumorigenesis, often evaded by aggressive cancers. In contrast, therapy-induced senescence (TIS) is a common outcome of genotoxic treatments like chemotherapy; while initially restraining tumor growth, the persistent SASP from TIS cells may subsequently facilitate tumor recurrence, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. Understanding this duality and the distinct implications of OIS versus TIS is crucial for developing senolytic or senomorphic strategies in ovarian cancer therapy. The tumor microenvironment in ovarian cancer significantly impacts the progression of tumors and their responses to treatment. This environment consists of a varied mix of immune and stromal cells, along with blood vessels, which interact in complex ways to either facilitate or hinder tumor advancement. Notably, the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), especially CD8+ T cells, has been associated with better clinical results21. However, ovarian cancers often exhibit an immunosuppressive TME, where factors such as PD-L1 expression, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) collaborate to dampen the antitumor immune response22. This immunosuppressive environment impedes the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and limits the success of immunotherapies. To overcome these challenges, combination therapies targeting immune checkpoints and modulating the TME are being explored to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in ovarian cancer. The interplay between cell death, senescence, and tumor immunity in ovarian cancer requires deeper mechanistic insight. The expression patterns of genes regulating these processes—collectively termed the cell death and senescence (CDS) signature—may hold critical information about tumor behavior and therapeutic susceptibility. Translating this multifaceted biology into clinically actionable knowledge, however, remains a challenge. Here, we address this gap by developing an interpretable, AI‑driven framework that integrates multi‑omics data—including genomics (CDS pathways), immunomics (TME composition), and clinical outcomes—to build a prognostic model23. This model not only stratifies patients but also pinpoints actionable biomarkers. By translating CDS biology into a robust predictor of survival and immunotherapy response, our work provides a unified computational foundation for designing multi‑target therapies to overcome adaptive resistance, ultimately advancing personalized treatment strategies for ovarian cancer.

Results › Identification of prognostic CDS genes in ovarian cancer

We explored how cell death and senescence-associated alterations affect the progression of ovarian cancer by performing a differential gene expression analysis on tumor samples compared to adjacent normal tissues, utilizing publicly accessible sequencing datasets. From this, we curated a cell death and senescence (CDS) gene set from the literature, identifying differential CDS genes between OC tissues and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Univariate Cox regression analysis was then performed to identify prognostically relevant CDS genes. Results › Construction of the CDSLS prognostic model

A prognostic model was developed using 101 machine learning algorithms and the 37 CDS-related genes. The optimal model, named CDSLS, was identified as CoxBoost + Enet [a = 0.1] (Fig. 2A). To validate the model, we collected 10 OC datasets, adjusting for batch effects. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) showed no significant differences across the datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Survival analysis using the CDSLS model revealed that high-risk patients consistently exhibited poorer survival outcomes across all datasets (Fig. 2B–K). The model was further validated using four immune therapy datasets, where high CDSLS scores correlated with poorer prognosis in patients receiving immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 1B–G). The box plot of CDSLS scores across four immunotherapy datasets demonstrated that higher CDSLS scores correlated with poorer prognosis and lower response rates among patients who did not respond to immunotherapy (Fig. 2L–P). The CDSLS score was also negatively correlated with Immunophenoscore (IPS), suggesting reduced responsiveness to immunotherapy in the high CDSLS group (Fig. 2Q–T).Fig. 2Construction and validation of the CDSLS model.A Heatmap of C-index values from 101 machine learning algorithms. Results › Performance evaluation and comparison with clinical parameters

We evaluated the clinical utility of the CDSLS model by comparing its performance against traditional clinical parameters such as age, stage, and grade across multiple OC datasets (Supplementary Fig. 2). The CDSLS model outperformed these clinical parameters in terms of the C-index (Fig. 3A). PCA analysis further demonstrated that patients could be distinctly clustered based on their CDSLS scores (Fig. 3B). Time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (timeROC) analysis revealed that the 5-year Area Under the Curve (AUC) for CDSLS was consistently above 0.6 across different datasets (Fig. 3C). Results › Chromosomal and mutational landscape of CDSLS-associated Genes

We performed CNV analysis for high and low CDSLS scoring groups, revealing significant differences in amplification and deletion mutations between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). Interestingly, the high CDSLS group exhibited lower tumor mutational burden (TMB) (Supplementary Fig. 3H, I). Our analysis, integrating TMB and CDSLS scores, indicated that patients in the low_CDSLS-low_TMB category exhibited a more favorable prognosis, while those in the high_CDSLS-high_TMB category had worse outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3J).

Results › Single-cell profiling reveals cell-type specific features of CDSLS

The single-cell RNA sequencing data from the CDSLS model was analyzed using traditional markers, leading to the identification of eight main cell types: epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, NK cells, myeloid cells, and plasma cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Certain CDS genes (e.g., TUBB6, RB1, CEBPB, CDKN1B) were predominantly expressed in epithelial cells, while others (e.g., DDIT4, FOS, UBB) were highly expressed in T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Notably, the high CDSLS scoring group had a higher proportion of fibroblast cells (Supplementary Fig. 4D) and exhibited more pronounced cell-cell communication (Supplementary Fig. 4E–G). Communication patterns between cells were notably stronger in the high CDSLS group, with significant differences in MIF and MIK signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4H, I). Both groups exhibited signaling primarily from epithelial and fibroblast cells, but the strength of signaling was higher in the CDSLS high-score group (Supplementary Fig. 4J).

Results › Association between CDSLS and drug sensitivity

To further explore the therapeutic potential of the CDSLS model, we analyzed its correlation with drug sensitivity. Protein-level analysis showed that CDSLS scores were positively correlated with several of the prognostic genes (Supplementary Fig. 6A–H) and negatively correlated with CERES (Supplementary Fig. 6I–P). Notably, we identified several drugs that exhibited a strong negative correlation with CDSLS scores, including brivanib, azacitidine, and BRD-K64610608/BMS-754807_2171 (Supplementary Fig. 6Q, S). Both brivanib and elocalcitol demonstrated significantly higher area under the curve (AUC) values in patients classified within the low-CDSLS group, which suggests that these particular medications may exhibit greater efficacy for individuals with low CDSLS scores. This observation is visually supported by the data presented in Supplementary Fig. 6R. Conversely, the drugs BMS-754807_2171 and Buparlisib_1873 revealed elevated half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values within the same low-CDSLS category. This finding indicates that these medications may have reduced effectiveness for patients exhibiting low CDSLS scores, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6T. For the flowchart of the entire study, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 7.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the interplay between cell death, senescence, and immune regulation in OC. By integrating a comprehensive set of 37 cell death and senescence-related genes into the CDSLS prognostic model, we highlight the significant role of these processes in tumor progression and the immune microenvironment. Our findings enhance the comprehension of ovarian cancer biology and provide a valuable instrument for patient categorization, potentially facilitating personalized treatment, especially for immunotherapy. Notably, the clinical utility of immunotherapy in ovarian cancer is currently limited by modest objective response rates, underscoring the urgent need for reliable biomarkers to identify patients who may benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and optimize immunotherapeutic strategies24. Cellular apoptosis is pivotal in cancer biology, affecting tumor advancement and immunological reactions. Apoptosis has conventionally been seen as a type of cell death that elicits no immunological response25. However, recent findings indicate that non-apoptotic regulated cell death (RCD) processes, including necroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, generate immune-stimulatory signals that enhance anti-tumor immunity26–28. As an example, dendritic cells (DCs) and other antigen-presenting cells (APCs) are activated by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released during necroptosis, which include HMGB1, ATP, and CRT29,30. This activation primes CD8⁺ T cells, thereby boosting tumor-specific immune responses. Consistent with this, our study identified genes such as TUBB6 and DDIT4, which regulate cell death processes and are upregulated in OV tumors, suggesting they may play a role in immune modulation. While RCD mechanisms like necroptosis and autophagy can enhance anti-tumor immunity, excessive inflammation due to overactive cell death can suppress immune responses. Excessive immune activation can attract myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), leading to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that may facilitate tumor metastasis31,32. Our findings underscore the importance of balancing immune stimulation with immune suppression to avoid detrimental outcomes, highlighting the need for more refined therapies that modulate these pathways effectively. Senescence is another key process in cancer biology with a dual role in tumorigenesis33. The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) functions as a defence mechanism by preventing injured cells from proliferating. However, it can also promote tumor progression by creating an inflammatory microenvironment through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and IL-1α34,35. In our study, CDKN1B (p27), a central regulator of cell cycle arrest36, was upregulated in OC and correlated with reduced immune cell infiltration. This suggests that senescent cells in OC may contribute to immune evasion. The SASP constitutes a complex and heterogeneous mixture of secreted factors, encompassing both immunostimulatory (e.g., CXCL10, CCL5, IL-15) and immunosuppressive (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, VEGF, TGF-β) cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. This duality enables SASP to play a context-dependent role in tumor-immune interactions. In early tumorigenesis or following therapy, the immunostimulatory components of SASP can facilitate the recruitment and activation of innate and adaptive immune cells (e.g., NK cells, CD8⁺ T cells), promoting the clearance of senescent cells—a process termed “senescence surveillance”16,17,20. However, in established tumors such as ovarian cancer, the sustained and chronic SASP often tilts the balance towards a net immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic effect. The persistent secretion of factors like IL-6 and IL-8 fosters a chronic inflammatory microenvironment, while the concomitant recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) actively dampens effective anti-tumor immunity, contributing to an immune-evasive or “immune-desert” phenotype37. Our findings, which link a high-CDSLS signature (enriched in senescence pathways) to excluded CD8⁺ T cells and inferior response to immunotherapy, align with this dominant immunosuppressive outcome in advanced OC. This underscores the therapeutic rationale for selectively targeting the deleterious, immunosuppressive arm of the SASP while preserving or enhancing its potential immunostimulatory effects. The role of RB1 in immune regulation is particularly noteworthy. As a well-established tumor suppressor, RB1 is crucial for controlling cellular senescence and maintaining cell cycle homeostasis38. Our finding that elevated RB1 expression correlates with an immunosuppressive phenotype and poorer prognosis in ovarian cancer appears, at first glance, to contradict its well-established role as a tumor suppressor. Classically, loss of RB1 function promotes cell cycle progression and tumorigenesis across many cancers. However, emerging evidence suggests that the role of RB1 is more nuanced and context-dependent. In our study, the prognostic signal captured by CDSLS likely reflects not merely RB1 expression level, but its functional state within the specific biological program of cellular senescence. Tumors lacking RB1 exhibit heightened immune cell infiltration and activation of immune response pathways, such as the cGAS-STING pathway, suggesting that RB1 loss may promote a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment39. Given its central role in regulating the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and our observed link between RB1-driven senescence and CD8+ T cell activation, RB1 functional status warrants future investigation as a potential modifier of the tumor-immune microenvironment and, consequently, of response to immunotherapies in OC. Thus, RB1 may serve as both a prognostic and therapeutic target in immunotherapy treatments. This study presents the CDSLS prognostic model, which integrates the expression profiles of 37 cell death- and senescence-related genes to predict survival in ovarian cancer (OC). Using machine learning algorithms, the model stratifies patients into high- and low-risk groups based on CDSLS scores, demonstrating superior predictive performance compared to traditional clinical parameters such as age and stage. Survival analyses across multiple OC datasets confirmed that high-risk patients consistently experience poorer outcomes. The study found that high-CDSLS tumors exhibit an “immune-excluded” phenotype, characterized by scarce CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and downregulated MHC molecules—a potential mechanism underlying resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). CDSLS also correlates with immune cell infiltration, immune response pathways, and Immunophenoscore (IPS), reflecting the status of the tumor immune microenvironment and supporting its utility in predicting immunotherapy response. The model’s robustness has been validated through cross-validation with multiple datasets. The novelty of the CDSLS model is twofold. First, by integrating key genes from multiple cell death and senescence pathways, it more comprehensively captures the complex biological crosstalk involved in tumor adaptation and resistance compared to signatures based on single pathways. Second, and more critically, it establishes a mechanistic link between intrinsic tumor cell fate decisions—such as RB1-mediated senescence—and the shaping of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, including CD8⁺ T-cell exclusion. This provides a causal hypothesis for immune-cold tumors and treatment resistance, positioning CDSLS not only as a prognostic tool but also as a translational framework for identifying actionable targets for rational combination therapies. Despite promising findings, this study has limitations. The CDSLS model was developed using retrospective data, requiring prospective validation in diverse cohorts for real-world applicability. The precise mechanistic roles of its key genes also need deeper experimental validation. To advance clinical translation, future efforts should focus on testing rational combination therapies guided by the model’s drug sensitivity associations. For example, therapies effective in low-CDSLS tumors could be combined with immunotherapy to overcome resistance in high-CDSLS settings, while senolytic agents might remodel the immune-cold microenvironment. Integrating CDSLS with established biomarkers could further refine risk stratification and guide targeted combination regimens.

Methods › Acquisition and processing of transcriptomic data

The RNA expression profiles along with clinical information related to ovarian cancer were sourced from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, specifically concentrating on the ovarian cancer cohort consisting of 378 subjects. To independently evaluate the developed model’s stability and generalizability, RNA sequencing data from the International Cancer Genome Consortium’s OV_AU dataset comprising 93 subjects were utilized. The RNA-seq data underwent normalization to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) and were subsequently log2-transformed for further analysis. In order to further confirm the validity of the model, a comprehensive array of gene expression datasets was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. This collection comprised several ovarian cancer microarray datasets, specifically GSE17260 with 110 samples, GSE26193 containing 107 samples, GSE26712 featuring 185 samples, GSE30161 with 58 samples, GSE49997 which included 194 samples, GSE63885 encompassing 75 samples, GSE9891 with a total of 278 samples, and finally, GSE140082 that comprised 380 samples. Together, these datasets represented a substantial total of 1858 samples, providing a broad and diverse basis for evaluation. For the purpose of external validation, an additional set of six immunotherapy datasets was employed. These included the GBM_PRJNA482620 dataset with 34 samples, Melanoma_GSE115821 consisting of 33 samples, Melanoma_GSE78220 including 28 samples, RCC_GSE67501 which had 11 samples, Melanoma_GSE91061 featuring 109 samples, Melanoma_GSE93157 containing 25 samples, and lastly, the IMvigor210 dataset that comprised 348 samples. The inclusion of these immunotherapy datasets enhances the robustness of the external validation process by ensuring a diverse range of contexts and patient populations is represented. A comprehensive differential gene expression analysis was conducted utilizing the GSE26712 dataset, which consists of 10 normal samples and 185 tumor samples, alongside the GTEx dataset that includes 88 normal samples. This analysis aimed to compare ovarian cancer tumors with adjacent normal tissues to identify significant differences in gene expression. To ensure the reliability of the results, the gene expression matrices were normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function available in the limma package. Furthermore, to address and minimize potential batch effects that may arise across the different datasets, the Combat function from the sva package was employed for effective batch effect correction. Prior to integration, genes with low or missing expression were filtered out. For microarray datasets, probes were retained if their expression was above the median intensity in at least 10% of samples within any dataset. For RNA-seq data, genes with TPM < 1 in more than 50% of samples were removed. Duplicate gene symbols were collapsed by retaining the probe/isoform with the highest median expression across all samples. Clinical metadata (overall survival time, vital status, tumor stage) were harmonized across all cohorts. Overall survival (OS) was uniformly defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. The baseline data of the dataset are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Methods › Collection and analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data

scRNA-seq data of ovarian cancer, encompassing tumor and peripheral blood samples from two treatment-naïve patients with high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, were sourced from the GEO database (accession GSE290141). While this dataset provides a high-resolution snapshot of the cellular landscape, the limited sample size is acknowledged as a constraint for capturing the full spectrum of inter-patient heterogeneity. All data processing and analysis were performed using R (version 4.1.3) and the Seurat package (version 4.0). For quality control, the following thresholds were established: mitochondrial content was set to be less than 25%, blood cell content was limited to under 3%, UMI counts were maintained between 200 and 50,000, and gene counts were restricted to a range of 200–7500. These thresholds were implemented to effectively eliminate low-quality cells and genes exhibiting minimal expression. Data normalization was conducted using Seurat’s NormalizeData function, followed by the identification of the 2000 most variable genes through the FindVariableFeatures function. In order to reduce the impact of cell cycle variations, scores from the G1/S and G2/M phases were removed through regression using the ScaleData function, with the parameter vars.to.regress designated as c(“S.Score”, “G2M.Score”). Furthermore, batch effects were mitigated by employing Harmony integration, which accounts for technical discrepancies while maintaining biological signals. Dimensionality reduction was performed using UMAP on the top 30 principal components. Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter set to 0.5. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function, with thresholds set as: adjusted p-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05, average log₂ fold change (avg_log2FC) > 0.25, and expression in at least 10% of cells within the cluster.

Methods › Cell type annotation

Cell type labeling was conducted through the use of well-known marker genes to pinpoint distinct cell populations within the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset. Epithelial cells were recognized via markers such as EPCAM, KRT18, KRT19, and CDH1, whereas fibroblasts were characterized by DCN, COL1A1, and COL1A2. The identification of endothelial cells was achieved through markers such as PECAM1, and CLDN5; T cells were characterized with the markers CD3D and CD3E. Natural killer (NK) cells were noted using markers NKG7, GNL and KLRD1; B cells were distinguished by CD79A, IGHG3 and IGHA2; plasma cells were marked by JCHAIN, and myeloid cells were identified based on LYZ, MARCO and FCGR3A. Additionally, mast cells were identified using KIT. After the annotation process, UMAP plots were employed to visualize the clustering of cells, while heatmaps and bar plots were produced to illustrate the distribution and expression of these markers among various cell types.

Methods › Identification of prognostic genes and CDSLS construction

The cell death and senescence-related gene set (CDS) was sourced from the literature40. Differential expression analysis between tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues was performed on the combined, batch-corrected GSE26712+GTEx dataset (as described above) using the limma package. Specifically, we applied a moderated t-test based on empirical Bayes moderation (via the eBayes function in limma) to identify genes significantly differentially expressed, with thresholds set at |log₂FC| > 0.5 and adjusted P-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05. Genes meeting these criteria and belonging to the CDS set were selected for subsequent prognostic analysis. Prognostic CDS-related genes were then identified using univariate Cox regression with a significance level of p < 0.05. A total of 37 prognostic genes were identified and selected for the construction of the CDSLS (Cell Death and Senescence Learning Signature) model.

Methods › Construction of risk scores for tumor prognosis

A prognostic model was developed utilizing 37 CDS-related genes and 101 machine learning algorithms. Each patient’s risk score was calculated by applying the optimal algorithm, selected through a series of model fitting steps. The surv_cutpoint function from the survminer R package was used to determine the cutoff for stratifying patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. The model’s predictive power was evaluated using the TCGA dataset and additional external datasets.

Methods › Machine learning-based risk feature generation

We used 10 machine learning algorithms and 101 algorithmic variants to create a robust prognostic model. Random Survival Forests (RSF), Lasso, Elastic Net (Enet), Ridge regression, CoxBoost, PLS-Cox, Stepwise Cox, SuperPC, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), and Survival Support Vector Machine were used. We first used univariate Cox regression to find predictive genes in the combined datasets, including TCGA. A total of 101 combinations of algorithms were leveraged to construct predictive models utilizing leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) on the TCGA-OV cohort. For validation purposes, independent datasets were applied, and the model exhibiting the highest average Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was selected as the best option.

Methods › Intercellular communication analysis

Cell-cell communication within the tumor microenvironment (TME) was meticulously examined through the utilization of the CellChat package. This analysis aimed to identify and assess the potential interactions that may occur among the diverse cell types present in the TME. To facilitate this evaluation, gene expression matrices underwent normalization before being imported into CellChat, allowing for the construction of a comprehensive communication network. Many functions were used to determine ligand-receptor interactions, including computeCommunProb, filterCommunication, and computeCommunProbPathway. Using the aggregateNet function, we visualized the communication network, which helped us understand the TME’s complex cell relationships.

Methods › Mutation analysis

Copy number variation (CNV) was assessed utilizing GISTIC 2.0, whereas tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated employing the maftools software. The analyses were conducted to assess the genetic changes linked to high and low CDSLS risk categories.

Methods › Correlation between prognostic model and tumor immunity

Immune infiltration levels for each TCGA patient were evaluated using the IOBR package, which integrates results from seven immune assessment methods. Immune cell infiltration was quantified, and a heatmap was generated to show the relative proportions of immune cells across risk groups. Additionally, immune-related gene expression was compared between high-risk and low-risk groups to explore the immune landscape associated with CDSLS.

Methods › Immune therapy response prediction

In order to forecast reactions to immunotherapy, the TIDE algorithm, which stands for Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion, was employed to examine mechanisms of immune evasion and anticipate responses to immune therapy utilizing the TCGA dataset. An analysis of the variations in CDSLS scores among immune responders compared to non-responders was conducted to evaluate the model’s capability in predicting outcomes of immunotherapy.

Methods › Cell culture

Shanghai Zeye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. provided the human ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR-3, and Shanghai Binsui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. provided the mice ovarian epithelial carcinoma cell line ID8. These cell lines were cultured and maintained in a humidified incubator set to a temperature of 37 °C and an atmosphere enriched with 5% carbon dioxide. The culture medium utilized for these cell lines was DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium), specifically from Servicebio, catalog number G4511. This medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin to support optimal cell growth and prevent bacterial contamination. To ensure the health and viability of the cells, the culture medium was refreshed twice a week. Once the cells achieved ~80% confluence, they were passaged at a ratio of 1:3 to facilitate continued growth and experimentation.

Methods › Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6 mice between the ages of six and eight weeks were used in this study. The participants received subcutaneous inoculations with ID8 cells that either overexpressed RB1 (RB1-OE) or knocked down RB1 (RB1-KD) after being randomly assigned into three different groups (n = 10 per group), and each mouse received subcutaneous inoculations with 5 × 105 ID8 cells that either overexpressed RB1 (RB1-OE) or knocked down RB1 (RB1-KD). Investigators were blinded to group allocation during tumor volume measurement and experimental endpoint analysis. Every five days, the tumor volumes were measured. The tumors were excised and weighed after the mice were mercifully put to death on the thirty-first day. Tumor tissue proteins were separated for Western blot examinations. Mice were subjected to euthanasia through intraperitoneal administration of a high dose of pentobarbital sodium (dosage: 120 mg/kg of body weight; purity: ≥98%, obtained from Sigma‑Aldrich). The volume of the injection was tailored based on the weight of each mouse (0.1 mL for every 10 g of body weight). The efficacy of euthanasia was verified by the absence of the corneal reflex, alongside prolonged respiratory and cardiac arrest lasting over 3 min. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Harbin Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval Number: 251111-01-GR). All experiments involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” (8th edition) published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), as well as relevant ethical guidelines and animal welfare regulations.

Methods › Generation of RB1 overexpression and knockdown OVCAR-3 cell lines

The plasmids used included the RB1 overexpression construct (pLVX-SPP1) and the empty vector (pLVX), both sourced from Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Beijing, China. Additionally, shRNAs that target RB1 along with a negative control (NC) were also procured from the same company. Following this, these plasmids were introduced into 293 T cells for the generation of lentiviral particles. Supernatants containing the virus were harvested 24 and 48 h after transfection and subsequently employed to transduce OVCAR-3 cells. After 48 h of transduction, cells were selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin (Macklin, Shanghai) to create stable OVCAR-3 cell lines with RB1 overexpression and RB1 knockdown.

Methods › β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining in cells and tissues associated with senescence

OCT substance was used to implant mouse tumor tissues, which were then cut into frozen slices. As directed by the manufacturer, SA-β-Gal activity was found using a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0602). Under a microscope, staining was seen and photographed.

Methods › Immunofluorescence staining of γ-H2AX in cultured cells

Forty percent paraformaldehyde solution was used to fix the cells for fifteen minutes. To make it easier for antibodies to reach intracellular structures, the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for ten minutes after this first step. To stop nonspecific antibody binding, the samples were blocked with a 5% BSA solution for 30 min following permeabilization. After applying the primary antibody that targets γ-H2AX, which was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Catalogue Number 9718), the samples were incubated at 4 °C for the entire night in order to guarantee the best possible binding conditions. After the primary antibody incubation, the samples were treated with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody, obtained from Abcam (Catalog Number ab6939), for 1 h at room temperature to allow for effective visualization. Finally, images of the samples were captured using a confocal microscope, enabling detailed examination of the cellular structures.

Methods › Immunofluorescence staining of γ-H2AX in tumor tissue paraffin sections

Tumor tissues were meticulously fixed using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution to preserve the cellular architecture. Following fixation, the tissues underwent a dehydration process, were cleared of any remaining solvents, and subsequently embedded in paraffin to facilitate sectioning. Thin sections, measuring 4 micrometers in thickness, were carefully mounted onto glass slides. Once mounted, the sections were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration to prepare them for immunohistochemical analysis. To prepare the tissue sections for antibody labeling, the endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited using a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, applying this treatment for a duration of 10 min to ensure that nonspecific staining was minimized. Following this, a blocking step was performed with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) to prevent nonspecific interactions between the primary antibody and the tissue. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-γ-H2AX primary antibody, which specifically targets the γ-H2AX marker associated with DNA damage. After the primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody for one hour at room temperature, enabling the detection of the primary antibody. Finally, the staining results were visualized using a fluorescence microscope, allowing for the observation of the specific binding of the antibodies within the tissue samples.

Methods › Western blot analysis

RIPA lysis buffer, specifically the Beyotime product with catalogue number P0013B, was used to recover total proteins from tissue and cellular samples. A BCA Protein Assay Kit, likewise from Beyotime and with the number P0010, was used to determine the concentration of these proteins. Protein separation was accomplished by loading 30 μg of protein per lane onto SDS-PAGE gels and then transferring the proteins onto PVDF membranes. Following the transfer of the proteins, the membranes were blocked for an hour with a 5% non-fat milk solution. Following this first step, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies that targeted p16INK4a, Bcl-2, and IL-6 for an entire night at 4 °C. These antibodies were obtained from Proteintech and had the corresponding catalogue numbers 10883-1-AP, 12789-1-AP, and 21865-1-AP. After the primary antibody was incubated, the membranes were incubated for an hour at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody from Abcam (catalogue number ab205718). A Millipore ECL chemiluminescence kit (catalogue number WBKLS0500) was used to detect the protein bands, and ImageJ software was used to quantitatively evaluate the bands that were produced.

Methods › Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was evaluated through the application of the CoraLite® Plus 488-Annexin V and PI Apoptosis Detection Kit, developed by Proteintech and cataloged as PF00005. This kit is designed to provide a reliable method for detecting apoptotic cells, utilizing fluorophore-conjugated Annexin V to identify early apoptosis, alongside propidium iodide (PI) to mark cells that are undergoing late-stage apoptosis or necrosis. By combining these two indicators, researchers can effectively analyze and quantify cellular apoptosis in various experimental contexts. Cell proliferation was evaluated with the TransDetect® EdU Imaging Kit-647 Fluorophore (TransGen, FU131-01). For macrophage surface marker staining, cells were labeled with CoraLite® Plus 555 anti-human CD44 (Proteintech, CL555-65063) and CoraLite® Plus 647 anti-human CD62L (Proteintech, CL647-65167). After incubation and washing with PBS, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine apoptosis rates, EdU-positive ratios, and the proportion of activated CD8+ T cells.

Methods › Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). For all hypothesis tests, two-tailed alternatives were used unless otherwise specified. Associations between two continuous variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Differences in categorical variables were evaluated by chi-square tests, while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparing continuous variables between two groups, especially for non-normally distributed data. The survminer package was employed to determine optimal cutoff values for continuous risk scores in survival analyses. Survival outcomes were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models and Kaplan–Meier survival curves, with comparisons made by the log-rank test. Where applicable, such as in differential gene expression analysis, P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Data are presented as mean values together with their standard deviations, providing a clear understanding of variability within the dataset. To compare means across groups, the statistical analyses comprised a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For post-hoc analyses, Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used, and for additional pairwise comparisons, the Student’s t test was employed. Statistically significant differences among the groups were indicated by asterisks, with thresholds established at *p < 0.05 indicating significance and **p < 0.01 denoting a higher level of significance.
