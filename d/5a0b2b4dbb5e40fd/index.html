Abstract

The electronic-structure informatics (ESI) descriptor set was applied to discover novel α-glucosidase inhibitors from a natural product (NP) database. The in silico screening was carried out through regression modelling for inhibitory activity (pIC50) using XGBoost with the ESI descriptor set. The optimized model achieved a test R2 of 0.85, demonstrating its high predictive accuracy. To explore potent NPs for α-glucosidase inhibition, in silico screening of 2623 NPs was performed. Already known NP-α-glucosidase inhibitors such as theasinensin A, chebulagic acid, and casuarictin were "re-identified" through the screening. It also revealed structurally novel NP compounds with moderate inhibitory activity and new scaffolds different from those of the known inhibitors. A series of docking simulations on the newly discovered compounds revealed that their binding scores are higher than a marketed drug, acarbose. These results demonstrate the applicability and uniqueness of the ESI descriptor set in "scaffold hopping" using NP databases.Scientific contributionThis study shows that the electronic-structure informatics (ESI) descriptor set supports effective scaffold hopping for discovering α-glucosidase inhibitors from natural product (NP) libraries beyond conventional structure-based searches. By combining quantum-chemistry-derived ESI descriptors with machine learning, we identify structurally novel NP inhibitor candidates, which exhibit competitive predicted activity despite low similarity to known chemotypes. This work demonstrates the value of electronic-structure information in in silico screening for identifying chemically diverse candidates.

Abstract

The electronic-structure informatics (ESI) descriptor set was applied to discover novel α-glucosidase inhibitors from a natural product (NP) database. The in silico screening was carried out through regression modelling for inhibitory activity (pIC50) using XGBoost with the ESI descriptor set. The optimized model achieved a test R2 of 0.85, demonstrating its high predictive accuracy. To explore potent NPs for α-glucosidase inhibition, in silico screening of 2623 NPs was performed. Already known NP-α-glucosidase inhibitors such as theasinensin A, chebulagic acid, and casuarictin were "re-identified" through the screening. It also revealed structurally novel NP compounds with moderate inhibitory activity and new scaffolds different from those of the known inhibitors. A series of docking simulations on the newly discovered compounds revealed that their binding scores are higher than a marketed drug, acarbose. These results demonstrate the applicability and uniqueness of the ESI descriptor set in "scaffold hopping" using NP databases. Scientific contribution This study shows that the electronic-structure informatics (ESI) descriptor set supports effective scaffold hopping for discovering α-glucosidase inhibitors from natural product (NP) libraries beyond conventional structure-based searches. By combining quantum-chemistry–derived ESI descriptors with machine learning, we identify structurally novel NP inhibitor candidates, which exhibit competitive predicted activity despite low similarity to known chemotypes. This work demonstrates the value of electronic-structure information in in silico screening for identifying chemically diverse candidates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-026-01192-8.

Introduction

According to the latest report in 2025 [1], about 589 million people in the world suffer from diabetes mellitus (DM), leading to over 3.4 million deaths in 2024, mainly in low- and middle-income countries. Furthermore, the prevalence of DM has steadily increased over the past several decades [1–3]. Among all diabetic patients, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), characterized by insulin resistance, impaired insulin secretion, and hyperglycemia, accounts for more than 90–95% [4]. For the survival of patients, access to affordable treatment, including insulin and α-glucosidase inhibitors, is essential. Until now, all available drug treatments for T2DM are associated with various side effects, including digestive problems, risk of heart failure, urinary tract, nerve, kidney, and eye infections [5]. There is an urgent global need to develop diabetes medicines that are highly effective, affordable, and have fewer side effects. α-Glucosidase inhibitors, which specifically affect the intestines, are recognized as important therapeutic agents for T2DM, although they may cause mild gastrointestinal side effects, such as flatulence, diarrhea, and abdominal pain [6]. Nevertheless, α-glucosidase remains an attractive target for developing potent and safe diabetic drugs due to its specificity for the intestine and its wide range of applications. In recent decades, interest in natural products (NPs) as potential treatments for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has increased, with active research focusing on NPs to identify α-glucosidase inhibitors [7–9]. The attraction of NPs will contribute to identifying new inhibitors and promising lead scaffolds of α-glucosidase inhibitors. NPs have long been a cornerstone in drug discovery. In particular, in the field of cancer medicines, NPs or NPs-derived compounds account for 64.9% (1220 compounds) of all approved cancer medicines [10]. Natural product drug discovery offers significant advantages, including diversity, safety, and accessibility. NPs offer a vast repository of compounds with unique and diverse structures, providing invaluable insights for drug discovery. However, the efficient discovery of new inhibitors from NP libraries remains challenging. Traditional in silico approaches, such as quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, have significantly contributed to relating molecular structures to biological activities. Nevertheless, most conventional descriptors primarily capture structural features and often overlook the electronic properties of molecules—features critical to molecular recognition, binding affinity, and electron/charge transfer between ligands and receptors. To address these challenges, we have been developing electronic-structure informatics (ESI) [11], which aims to analyze and predict the properties of molecules by focusing on their electronic structures. Based on quantum chemical calculations, numerical descriptors that characterize a substance, relating to the molecules' electronic structures and properties. Then, using statistical and machine-learning techniques, the ESI descriptors facilitate the analysis of compounds, aiding in the prediction of physical properties and bioactivity. This approach has been employed to predict bioactivity, to identify critical factors, and to discover new functional molecules [12–14]. Furthermore, the ESI method focuses on the electronic structures of molecules, which differ from approaches intended to search for structural similarity, such as 2D fingerprints. This feature can allow us to achieve 'scaffold hopping': the exploration of compounds or scaffolds beyond those already identified as medicines [15]. Building upon our brief preliminary research [16], the present study applies the ESI approach to predict the activity of α-glucosidase inhibitors and to mine NPs for novel inhibitors. We present potent and promising inhibitor candidates among NP compounds, and then we discuss the utility of the ESI approach in drug discovery and its capacity for scaffold hopping.

Computational methods › Electronic-structure calculations

All quantum mechanical (QM) calculations were conducted by Gaussian16 [39] based on the density functional theory (DFT), with M06-2X exchange–correlation functional [40] and 6-31G(d, p) basis set. The choice of the DFT functional is arbitrary. In the present work, the M06-2X functional was chosen following our prior studies [12–14]. This is a part of our “model chemistry” for machine learning, aiming at in-silico compound screening. Six primary QM calculations were conducted: geometry optimization, single-point energy evaluation, vibrational frequency analysis, solvation energy evaluation, molecular volume determination, and excitation energy evaluation. All types of ESI descriptors calculated in this study are listed in Table 1. Computational methods › Constructing regression models

To find the best training-test data split, firstly, 100,000 random 80/20 training-test data splits were generated. The differences in data distribution, the variance (∆V), and mean (∆M) of pIC50 between the training and test sets were evaluated in each split to ensure that the training and test sets have nearly equal data distributions. Among 100,000 splits, 1000 splits with the smallest values of the product (∆V × ∆M) were retained (as shown in Fig. S4). XGBoost [44] models were subsequently trained for each retained split with hyperparameters optimized via Bayesian optimization (Optuna [45]) using fivefold cross-validation within the training portion of each split. The model and split yielding the highest test R2 were selected for further analysis. The reported test R2 should therefore be interpreted as best-model performance rather than an unbiased estimate of generalization. Prior to model training, all features were standardized using the mean and standard deviation computed from the entire labeled dataset. We acknowledge that this procedure introduces a mild form of data leakage, since scaling parameters include information from the test set. However, because the primary regression model employed (XGBoost) is tree-based and insensitive to monotonic feature scaling, the practical impact of this standardization on model performance is expected to be limited. We intended to select the most stable and representative predictive model to apply in subsequent in silico screening. The above method is expected to construct more versatile models without using biased train-test random data splitting. The risk of overfitting will be reduced for specific data splits, enabling good performance in different or unknown datasets, such as subsequent NP screening.

Computational methods › In silico screening

Before the screening, the test set, which had been used to evaluate models, was served to the model as an additional training set, and then the model was retrained using the same hyperparameters. Further improvements in prediction accuracy and expansion of the range of adaptation were expected (although the effect cannot be verified). The retrained regression model was applied to predict the pIC50 values of 2623 natural products (NPs) collected from KampoDB. Several compounds, with higher predicted pIC50 values, were further evaluated and discussed as promising α-glucosidase inhibitor candidates.

Computational methods › Docking study

Docking studies were performed to evaluate binding modes and docking scores of the compounds, with the aim of prioritizing those obtained from the screening. Usually, α-glucosidase from the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae is used when measuring the inhibitory activity; however, since no crystal structure of S. cerevisiae α-glucosidase has been reported, previous docking studies [46–50] have utilized isomaltase from S. cerevisiae, which has 84% similarity to α-glucosidase of S. cerevisiae. Therefore, in this study, the crystal structure of isomaltase (PDB ID: 3A4A) was applied as a surrogate for the α-glucosidase structure. The deletion of the initially attached ligand and water, along with the assignment of partial charges to the crystal structure, was performed using UCSF Chimera [51]. Charges and hydrogen atoms were assigned in their neutral form, appropriate for pH 7, to the structure using the Open Drug Discovery Toolkit (ODDT) [52]. For ligands, those structures (several known α-glucosidase and NP compounds) were prepared as ground states. Cartesian coordinates were extracted from the Gaussian16 checkpoint files (of geometry optimization) using the utility "newzmat" and then converted to structure-data format (SDF) files using Open Babel [53]. Docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina [54, 55] to evaluate binding scores (in kcal/mol) and the resulting binding poses. Since antagonistic inhibition does not always occur for any compound, the size of the simulation box was set to cover the entire α-glucosidase structure, leaving a margin. During docking simulation, the protein was held rigid; only the ligand's position and conformation were allowed to vary. The docking exhaustiveness parameter was set to 32 to ensure a thorough sampling of binding poses. The docking poses were further analyzed to investigate hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with key amino acid residues at the active pocket, visualized with LigPlot + [56, 57]. The validity of the above docking protocol is discussed in detail in the SI (Figs. S8–S10 and S17).

Results and discussion › Computation of ESI descriptors

The accuracy of DFT can be verified by comparing several ESI descriptors with experimental values. However, directly comparing these properties for α-glucosidase inhibitors is challenging due to the lack of experimental data on their electronic properties. Therefore, we instead investigated the properties of several representative molecules (acetone, phenol, and hydrogen cyanide). The calculated and experimental dipole moments (μ) and vertical ionization potentials (IPv) of these molecules are summarized in Table 2. This comparison indicates that our DFT (M06-2X/6-31G(d, p)) method yields electronic properties that are in good agreement with experiments. The calculated μ values for these molecules are 2.84, 1.37, and 2.94 D, comparable with the experimental values of 2.88, 1.22, and 2.98 D. The calculated IPv are 9.65, 8.49, and 13.60 eV, comparable with the experimental values of 9.80, 8.75, and 13.61 eV. Thus, our DFT reproduced dipole moments and ionization potentials within the error of 0.7–12%, providing sufficient confidence in the reliability of the ESI descriptors. Results and discussion › XGBoost regression to predict pIC50

Regression models were constructed to predict the inhibitory activity of α-glucosidase inhibitors using the two types of ESI descriptor sets. The data were divided into the training and test sets for regression modeling, with 346 and 87 molecules, respectively. After repeating the data split 1000 times, we selected the split and model that achieved the highest R2 value. For the ESI-1 descriptor set, the R2 value was 0.73 (Fig. 2a), indicating a moderate correlation between the predicted and experimental pIC50 values. The model was further improved with an expanded ESI-2 descriptor set, achieving a test R2 of 0.85 (Fig. 2b). This improvement indicates that including excitation energy–related descriptors (the ESI-2 set) provides a more flexible representation of electronic structure. Given this predictive performance, subsequent in silico screening was conducted using the ESI-2 model To investigate which descriptors most influence the pIC50 predictions, we performed SHAP analysis. As shown in Fig. 2b, the top-five important features of the ESI-1 model were S(ρex), ∆Esolv, λA→S0, B3, and EAv. Similarly, in the ESI-2 model (Fig. 2d), the important features included 1/∆E1, EAv, ∆Esolv, λA→S0, and B3. The addition of 1/∆E1 clearly influenced the model's predictions and accuracy. In both the ESI-1 and ESI-2 models, solvation energy (∆Esolv), electron affinity (EAv), thinness of molecule (B3), and reorganization energy from anionized state to S0 state (λA→S0) emerged as influential descriptors. ∆Esolv can be interpreted as a descriptor related to molecules’ overall polarity and the ease of desolvation required when the molecule engages a binding site. A trend in weak ∆Esolv (i.e., less tendency to solvate) of the model indicates that less stabilization upon solvation in water would be desirable for inhibition. The observed trend, therefore, suggests that the model implicitly captured solvation-related characteristics shared among reported inhibitors, which may influence their interactions with the enzyme environment and inhibition potency. The importance of EAv may stem from the frequent incorporation of electronegative substituents (such as fluorine and chlorine) in the dataset compounds. These substituents lower the LUMO energy and increase electron affinity, which could enhance π–π interactions or ion binding with electron-rich residues in α-glucosidase binding sites. In this context, compounds with a high EAv (electron affinity) are expected to behave as better electron acceptors, stabilizing interactions with electron-donating (basic) amino acids in the pocket. Indeed, basic residues (e.g., ARG315, HIS280), commonly located in the competitive inhibition pocket, play a key role in polar or electrostatic interaction of ligands (Table S3). Furthermore, the emphasis on the thinness of the molecule (B3) likely reflects the geometric constraints of the binding site. In contrast, the highlight in λA→S0 indicates that the inhibitors may have moderate flexibility (rather than excessive), which help the ligands to remain in the binding pocket (i.e., stability of the binding state). Hypothetically, electron-donating amino acid residues in the binding pocket may promote electronic polarization or charge redistribution in the ligand. .Overall, these insights suggest that the predictive models have prioritized a balance between electronic complementarity and suitability as well as molecular shape compatibility, which are both critical for high-affinity and selective inhibition of α-glucosidase.

Results and discussion › In silico screening from natural products

We retrained the ESI-2 model on the complete set of 433 inhibitors (training data + former test data) to leverage all available data towards the NPs screening from KampoDB. As a result of in silico screening, we identified several compounds predicted to potentially inhibit α-glucosidase (Fig. 3). The ESI-2 model yielded many polyphenols rich in hydroxyl groups, including numerous gallate-containing compounds. Among these, flavonoids, a subclass of polyphenols characterized by a flavan skeleton, were identified as promising candidates. Results and discussion › Docking study for potential candidates

Docking studies were conducted to evaluate the binding scores of the compounds identified through in silico screening. All compounds described below, as shown in Figs. 5, 6 and S6, only bound to the active site (antagonistic inhibition pocket), allowing for a direct comparison of binding scores of them. The top-ranked α-glucosidase inhibitors in the dataset reached binding scores of –12.8 to –13.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 5). As summarized in Table S2, none of the screened NP compounds achieved docking scores better than those of the top-ranked α-glucosidase inhibitors; most NP hits generally had docking scores in the –8 to –10 kcal/mol range (indicative of weak to moderate scores). Among them, two NP compounds, CID:71448959 and procyanidin B-5 3,3’-di-O-gallate, exhibited relatively favorable (more negative) binding scores of –11.3 and –11.4 kcal/mol, which were more favorable than that of acarbose (–7.97 kcal/mol). Notably, residues ASP307, ARG315, PRO312, HIS280, and TYR158 were common interaction partners for both compounds and the top-ranked inhibitors. This consistent binding pattern indicates that the ESI approach expands structural diversity, while indirectly retaining the critical interactions required for strong α-glucosidase inhibition, achieved through descriptors related to electronic structures. Although our main focus is on the ESI descriptor framework, we also performed parallel modeling with conventional descriptors (ECFP4 and RDKit) to compare the screening outcomes. The detailed results are provided in the SI (Figs. S11–S17 and Tables S4–S5). Briefly, the top-ranked NP compounds selected by ESI, ECFP4, and RDKit differed, and chemical-space visualizations indicate that each descriptor set explores distinct regions of the NP-compound space (Figs. S13–S16). Under the same docking protocol, the ESI-selected NP set also showed a higher fraction (i.e., hit rate) of candidates with favorable docking scores (Fig. S17). Together, these comparisons provide one line of evidence that ESI-based screening can yield a distinct selection tendency compared with conventional descriptor-based approaches. We interpret these differences as arising from each type of encoded information (electronic structure in ESI vs. structure/physicochemical information in ECFP4/RDKit), while a systematic causal assessment of how descriptor choice influences screening outcomes remains an important topic for future work. While no NP compound was predicted to surpass the potency of the top-ranked inhibitors, several NP compounds exhibited comparable inhibition activity. Given their novel scaffolds and distinct chemotypes, these compounds represent promising starting points for further optimization. In this study, the ESI-based approach successfully identified structurally unique candidates with a competitive predicted inhibitory activity and docking score, highlighting the impact of ESI on lead discovery and scaffold hopping. Summary and conclusions

In this study, we applied the electronic-structure informatics (ESI) descriptor set to predict the activity of α-glucosidase inhibitors, a known drug category for diabetes treatment. Moreover, we conducted in silico screening for exploring new inhibitors and potent lead compounds from NPs in the KampoDB database. The ESI-2 model, enhanced with 'excitation energy' as an additional descriptor, showed reasonable prediction accuracy. Furthermore, by observing the ESI descriptors and their importance, valuable insights were gained not only into the properties of the ligands themselves but also into their responsiveness to α-glucosidase. Our in silico screening re-identified known α-glucosidase inhibitors, such as theasinensin A and chebulagic acid. Additionally, several flavonoids were pinpointed as potential candidates for α-glucosidase inhibitors. This result agrees with previous reports that the flavonoid family often has α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. These findings suggest that the ESI method can search for diverse compounds that are not restricted by known compounds and their scaffolds in the training data. The docking studies indicate that several promising NP compounds can favorably interact with active site of α-glucosidase, supporting their predicted inhibitory activity. CID:71448959 and procyanidin B-5 3,3'-di-O-gallate were identified as potential candidates with strong binding scores of –11.3 and –11.4 kcal/mol. The binding modes of those NP compounds and the top-ranked known inhibitors were essentially similar, but the NP candidates' scaffolds were surprisingly unique. This indicates that the ESI-based approach is applicable to identify novel scaffolds which show low similarity to known inhibitors and tend to have favorable docking scores.
