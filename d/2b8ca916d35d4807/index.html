Abstract

This study proposes a fuzzy machine learning framework for optimizing antiepileptic drug selection using Quantitative Structure-Property Relationship (QSPR) modeling under pharmacological uncertainty. Feature relevance was assessed using Random Forest-based importance and SelectKBest with mutual information, and a feedforward neural network was trained with 5-fold cross-validation. Fuzzy membership functions were incorporated to model variability in clinical and experimental data. Compared with Multiple Linear Regression and conventional QSPR models, the proposed approach achieved a 24 percent reduction in RMSE. Predicted pharmacological attributes were further integrated into a Multi-Criteria Decision-Making framework using TOPSIS to rank drug candidates based on efficacy, safety, and cost. The resulting rankings showed 95 percent Spearman correlation with clinician evaluations, demonstrating the framework reliability for uncertainty-aware antiepileptic drug prioritization and custom MCDM libraries for TOPSIS based prioritization.

Abstract

This study proposes a fuzzy machine learning framework for optimizing antiepileptic drug selection using Quantitative Structure-Property Relationship (QSPR) modeling under pharmacological uncertainty. Feature relevance was assessed using Random Forest-based importance and SelectKBest with mutual information, and a feedforward neural network was trained with 5-fold cross-validation. Fuzzy membership functions were incorporated to model variability in clinical and experimental data. Compared with Multiple Linear Regression and conventional QSPR models, the proposed approach achieved a 24 percent reduction in RMSE. Predicted pharmacological attributes were further integrated into a Multi-Criteria Decision-Making framework using TOPSIS to rank drug candidates based on efficacy, safety, and cost. The resulting rankings showed 95 percent Spearman correlation with clinician evaluations, demonstrating the framework reliability for uncertainty-aware antiepileptic drug prioritization and custom MCDM libraries for TOPSIS based prioritization.

Introduction

Epilepsy consists of a wide range of seizures due to chaotic electrical signals in the brain thereby interfering with sensations, behavior, muscle function and awareness1. An approximate amount of 50 million people in the world have epilepsy, of which 5 million new cases are reported annually. In the United States country wide, the number of adult patients suffering from epilepsy is approximately 3 million, and the number of children living with the condition is nearly 470,0002. Epileptic seizures, characterized by abnormal neuronal hyperactivity, are classified by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) into focal, generalized and unknown onset types based on origin and awareness. A person with seizures experiences abnormal electrical brain activity and may have symptoms that include focal twitches, strange sensations or body rigidness and unconsciousness3. As well as shock and head movement, patients sometimes have absence seizures and confusion after seizures and these can be made worse by anxiety and memory issues as seen in Fig. 1. Seizure types are determined by clinical history, EEG recording and brain imaging prior to suggesting specific antiseizure treatments. AEDs, known as anticonvulsants, are medicines used to treat and control the frequency and strength of seizures in epilepsy4. Some common AEDs are carbamazepine, valproic acid, lamotrigine, levetiracetam and topiramate, each with its own special effects and ways the body processes it5. Even though these drugs improve outcomes, it is essential to carefully consider which AED is best for a person and which is safest. Because decisions are often complex, it is helpful to rely on multi-criteria approaches when choosing treatment. Current research efforts are still concentrating on finding and perfecting new AEDs that can effectively manage seizures and cause the least harm to the mind or body6. Machine Learning7,8 is a branch of Artificial Intelligence (AI) that allows systems to find patterns in data, improve their performance and make predictions without being programmed. Machine Learning algorithms such as Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) need precise, organized data to work on problems such as classification, regression and clustering. On the other hand, Fuzzy Machine Learning uses fuzzy logic concepts to address real world ambiguity and uncertainty10. With fuzzy set theory, Fuzzy Machine Learning makes it possible for a variable to be a member of many classes by a certain degree, instead of just one or the other9. This approach is particularly effective for handling noisy data, linguistic variables and scenarios with incomplete information11. Some applications include medical health settings depending on humans to assess symptoms and others require artificial intelligence to control systems like humans do. Fuzzy Artificial Neural Networks(FANNs) are hybrid systems that allow decisions to be taken with more precision, as the system balances probabilistic learning with interpretable fuzzy rules12. While machine learning works great for predictable tasks, fuzzy machine learning becomes important in difficult problem scenarios where some things are clear yet others are uncertain13. In discrete mathematics, graph theory is the core subject dedicated to graphs which represent connections between pairs of things14. A graph consists of vertices (nodes) connected by edges (links) and it is widely used for computer science, biology, chemistry, social networks and problems needing optimization. In traditional graph theory, vertices may be connected by an edge or not15,16. However, many real-world problems present unclear or uncertain information that cannot be well represented with crisp graphs. In order to overcome this difficulty, fuzzy graph theory was proposed as a specialized branch of classical graph theory. To show how vertices are connected in a fuzzy graph, their links are given degrees of membership that lie between 0 and 117. With this framework, complex systems can be represented more precisely which is useful in areas where having partial knowledge is common such as decision making, pattern recognition and data mining18,19. Molecular structures are described by topological indices, extracted from graph theory and employed frequently in mathematical chemistry and cheminformatics20. With these indices, we can measure the size, shape, branching and connection patterns of molecules without needing actual geometric or experimental data. A molecule is shown as a graph with each atom at a vertex and every bond between atoms presented by edges21. Well-known topological indices are Wiener index, Zagreb indices, Randic index and atom-bond connectivity index which identify different structural features. They play an important role in estimating the properties, activity and drug potential of compounds22. Topological indices are applied for fast virtual screening and structure activity relationship (SAR) investigations in drug discovery and the study of materials. By using machine learning, topological indices become important features in the construction of models for predictive computational chemistry23. MCDM relates to operations research where multiple possible options are assessed and measured when criteria are conflicting24. With MCDM, TOPSIS is one of the most preferred techniques due to its clear and logical way of working. In TOPSIS, the best choice should be the one with the shortest Euclidean distance to the ideal solution and the farthest one from the negative-ideal position. You normalize the matrix, calculate the weights for each factor and determine the distances from both the ideal and negative-ideal solutions25. It is particularly valuable when making important choices in medical diagnosis, supply management or when evaluating drugs because you have to weigh different factors at the same time26,27. The simplicity and efficiency of TOPSIS make it a valuable tool in supporting rational and transparent decision making28. Introduction › Motivation and significance

Epilepsy, with over 50 million cases worldwide, is still a problem for neurologists because seizures can be caused by many things, drugs may work differently for each patient and standard approaches may not help everyone. Using common diagnostic techniques, predicting seizures and choosing the right treatment remains difficult when there is lots of unclear data and patient-specific differences. We are motivated to find ways that make precision medicine frameworks robust and easy for doctors to interpret at the same time. Applying ANNs and Fuzzy ANNs allows us to face two issues: handling the hard-to-model data from neurophysiology and supporting the imprecise outcomes from EEG changes or patients subjective experiences. This technique is significant because ANNs are precise with set data and Fuzzy ANNs can be certain with incomplete or unreliable information, as clinicians do. By using TOPSIS, we can rank anti-epileptic drugs accurately, using criteria such as effect, safety and how well patients stick to taking them. This synergy of machine learning and decision science not only advances personalized epilepsy management but also establishes a scalable paradigm for other neurological disorders, bridging the gap between algorithmic innovation and actionable clinical insights.

Methodology

The methodology was systematically organized into four key phases to establish robust QSPR models for epilepsy drug property prediction.

Methodology › Step 1: data collection and preprocessing

Drug Selection: Twenty anti-epileptic drugs were selected, including Carbamazepine, Levetiracetam, Phenobarbital, Valproic acid, Ethosuximide, Gabapentin, Pregabalin, Felbamate, Lacosamide, Tiagabine, Oxcarbazepine, Clonazepam, Eslicarbazepine, phenytoin, Fosphenytoin, Lamotrigine, Lacosamide, Lorazepam and Diazepam.Property Extraction: Ten key physicochemical properties were retrieved from authoritative databases:Density, Molar Refractivity, H-bond Donors/Acceptors, ACD/LogDACD/BCF, Polarizability, Surface Tension, Molar Volume(Source: ChemSpider)Data Normalization: Features standardized using z-score: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z = \frac{x - \mu }{\sigma }$$\end{document}

Methodology › Step 2: molecular graph construction

Graph Representation: Molecular structures converted into graphs using RDKit in Python.Node/Edge Definition:Atoms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} Nodes (labeled by element/charge)Bonds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} Edges (labeled by bond order: 1=single, 2=double.)

Methodology › Step 3: topological descriptor calculation

Eccentricity-Based Indices: Computed using graph eccentricity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon (v)$$\end{document} (longest shortest path from vertex v): \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {Eccentric Connectivity Index} = \sum _{v \in V} \deg (v) \cdot \epsilon (v)$$\end{document} Other indices: Wiener, Harary and Distance-based Eccentricity Indices.Feature Engineering: Twelve molecular descriptors derived as seen in Table 1.

Methodology › Step 4: machine learning algorithms

Model Selection: Supervised learning models such as Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and Fuzzy Artificial Neural Network (FANN) were selected for Categorization of epilepsy-related drug properties.Training and Validation: The dataset was split into training and testing sets by using 5k fold cross-validation was applied to ensure robustness and avoid overfitting.Performance Metrics: Model evaluation was carried out using accuracy, precision and recall to assess predictive capability on epilepsy related molecular descriptors.

Methodology › Step 5: MCDM technique (TOPSIS)

Ranking Objective: The Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was employed to rank the epilepsy drugs based on multiple molecular descriptors.Normalization and Weighting: Descriptor values were normalized and weights were assigned based on their relevance to predictive performance.Ideal and Negative-Ideal Solutions: Each alternative (drug) was compared to the ideal and negative-ideal solutions. The closeness coefficient was calculated to rank alternatives accordingly.This paper relied on the relevance of the domain and empirical validation to make the methodological decisions in this study. The chosen topological indices are highly established in QSPR investigations and have been utilized due to their capacity to build significant structural attributes that affect the antiepileptic drug activity with redundancy reduced by a preliminary statistical screen. Fuzzy membership functions were utilized because of their interpretability, strength, and ability to describe uncertainty, and the parameters were specified using the distributions of the descriptors.The ANN/FANN structures were refined by systematic experimental trial and error and by cross-validated performance indicators (RMSE, MAE, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}) to be able to ensure the generalization and prevent overfitting, which also increases the reproducibility and scientific validity of the suggested architecture.

The goal is to minimize a predefined loss function (Mean Squared Error for regression or Cross-Entropy for classification) through iterative optimization algorithms like gradient descent. Common algorithms include Artificial Neural Networks, requiring careful tuning of hyperparameters (learning rate, tree depth or hidden layers) to balance bias and variance. Supervised learning techniques › ANN algorithm

ANNs are computational models inspired by biological neural networks, designed to approximate complex nonlinear relationships through interconnected layers of artificial neurons. They employ activation functions (e.g., sigmoid, ReLU) and gradient-based optimization (e.g., backpropagation) to learn patterns from numerical data, excelling in tasks like classification, regression, and pattern recognition. However, traditional ANNs struggle with uncertain, imprecise, or linguistic data. FANNs hybridize ANNs with fuzzy logic to address these limitations. By integrating fuzzy membership functions and rule-based systems into neural architectures, FANNs can process both crisp numerical values and fuzzy linguistic variables (”high,” ”medium,” ”low”). This enables robust handling of ambiguous real-world data through:Fuzzy input layers with membership functions (triangular, Gaussian)Adaptive fuzzy rules encoded in network weightsDefuzzification output layers for crisp predictionsFANNs are particularly effective in domains requiring uncertainty management, such as medical diagnosis, control systems, and materials science, where they outperform conventional ANNs in interpretability and noisy-data robustness. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the successful application of artificial neural networks and fuzzy artificial neural networks algorithms for the prediction and assessment of potential anti-epileptic drugs. Both models performed effectively across various datasets, however the fuzzy artificial neural networks approach consistently provided more refined and accurate predictions due to its ability to handle uncertainty and incorporate degrees of membership rather than binary classification. The results demonstrate that fuzzy-based approaches, especially FANNs, outperform most other algorithm as we see in Table 9 and have a higher predictive accuracy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(R^2>0.75)$$\end{document}. This nuanced treatment of input features enables fuzzy artificial neural networks to better capture the complex, non-linear relationships present in biomedical data, particularly in pharmacological contexts. To further refine the selection of the most suitable drugs, we employed a multi-criteria decision-making method specifically, the technique for order of preference by similarity to ideal solution. TOPSIS enabled a systematic and objective evaluation of the drug candidates by considering multiple quantitative and qualitative factors such as predicted efficacy, safety profiles and computational model scores. Based on this comprehensive ranking system, Oxcarbazepine and Eslicarbazepine emerged as the top-ranking drugs, demonstrating optimal performance across the majority of selected criteria. The integration of machine learning models with fuzzy logic and decision-making frameworks like TOPSIS represents a powerful and intelligent approach for drug discovery and repositioning. This hybrid methodology not only enhances prediction robustness but also ensures that the final drug recommendations are both data driven and clinically relevant. Therefore, the findings of this study not only support the reliability of artificial neural networks and fuzzy artificial neural networks models but also highlight the importance of combining predictive analytics with decision support tools in the context of epilepsy treatment.

Conclusion › Limitations and future work

The proposed computational framework provides a robust approach for comparative evaluation and ranking of antiepileptic drug candidates, but several limitations should be acknowledged. To predict continuous physicochemical properties the study uses supervised regression models, including ANN and FANN. Regression specific measures (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}, RMSE, MAE, MSE) were used to assess model performance, and no measures of classification were used since the study is not based on categorical results. The statistics power and external generalizability is limited by the relatively small size of the dataset of 20 drugs. In order to minimize overfitting and to improve the strength of the models, 5k fold cross-validation and regularization were implemented. Therefore, the findings can be seen as comparative and exploratory but not definitive. Additionally, Despite the proposed modeling framework effectively describing the structure-activity relationships, it does not include explicit receptor level pharmacodynamics, mechanistic targeting information and drug interactions. These pharmacological dimensions play a vital role in defining target selectivity, off-target effects, as well as the synergistic or antagonistic interactions, especially in epilepsy, where combination therapy has become the common clinical procedure. The current model utilizes molecular topological indices, which represent global structural and physicochemical data but do not directly reflect ligand-receptor binding kinetics, modulation of signaling pathways, or interaction-induced pharmacodynamic variability. The further development of this work will focus on incorporating receptor-level descriptors (binding affinities, target occupancy measures), mechanistic pathway annotations, and rug interactions attributes based on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interaction networks. It is anticipated that through such integration, either through multimodal or graph-based learning, model interpretability, model robustness, and model translational relevance to clinically informed antiepileptic drug prioritization will be enhanced.
