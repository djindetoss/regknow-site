Abstract

ABSTRACTDrug development in pediatric rare diseases is complicated by practical and ethical constraints on clinical trial design, stemming from small, highly heterogeneous, and vulnerable patient populations. Virtual patients (VPs) created with machine‐learning (ML), mechanistically driven computational approaches, or hybrids thereof, have the potential to expedite and maximize the impact of trials. We discuss the potential of VPs to transform the efficiency and impact of clinical trials in pediatric rare diseases, based on adult and pediatric examples.

Virtual Patients and Their Applications

Advances in machine‐learning (ML) and Quantitative Systems Pharmacology (QSP) modeling are transforming disease understanding and drug development through the generation of Virtual patients (VPs), integrating historical patient data and/or mechanistic insights. With ML, VPs are implemented by non‐parametric predictive models trained on large data sets of longitudinal individual‐level patient data that may include demographic, clinical, genetic, biomarker, and disease progression features. ML models can then predict personalized disease trajectories with high levels of precision, enabling applications such as patient stratification, optimized analysis of treatment responses, as well as augmenting or replacing placebo patients [1]. Regulatory agencies are increasingly providing guidance to leverage these opportunities, focusing on ensuring that the models are trained and extensively validated, based on their context of use, on high quality, representative data (https://www.fda.gov/regulatory‐information/search‐fda‐guidance‐documents/considerations‐use‐artificial‐intelligence‐support‐regulatory‐decision‐making‐drug‐and‐biological). On what is perhaps the other end of the spectrum, mechanistic QSP models are driven by the integration of various data sources and thus are less dependent on large data sets. These models are increasingly applied in regulatory submissions [2] and acknowledged by regulatory agencies such as the FDA and EMA as valuable tools for investigating disease mechanisms and evaluating therapeutic strategies. QSP models enable the generation of virtual populations, allowing for the simulation of VPs, informing optimal dosing strategies and treatment schedules. Due to their mechanistic nature, QSP models require limited data to be calibrated and are generally thought to extrapolate well across different data sets. Conversely, extrapolation remains a weak point of ML models, which are excellent interpolators. Such QSP‐based VPs are thus particularly valuable in settings where patient recruitment is limited, such as in rare or pediatric diseases. By accounting for inter‐individual variability and integrating physiological processes, QSP‐based VPs can represent hard‐to‐enroll subpopulations, thereby expanding the scope of translational insights derived from computational studies.

Digital Twins of Disease Progression: Lessons From ALS

To exemplify the use of cutting‐edge VPs in an ongoing use case, we consider the rare disease amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) Phase 2 study (SEALS: NCT 06365216) of NX210c in the adult population. NX210c is a cyclic peptide with promising properties for neurodegenerative diseases due to its potential to repair the blood–brain barrier. In this study, approved by the French National Agency for the Safety of Medicines, patients will be randomized into three groups to receive either placebo (n = 20), 5 mg (n = 30), or 10 mg (n = 30) of NX210c. To enhance statistical power and allow more patients to receive active treatment, the control group will be supplemented with 60 VPs, implemented as predictions from ML models trained and validated on historical patient registry data from the PRO‐ACT database. The VPs will link baseline patient characteristics (such as baseline disease status, age, weight etc.) to longitudinal disease progression as measured by changes in for example, the revised ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS‐R), and to survival, similar to Beaulieu et al. [1]. VP outcomes will be compared to observed trial patient data and will subsequently serve to enhance the statistical analysis. The approach of VPs versus directly using historical controls was taken because of the following advantages it provides: a VP generated by a ML model more accurately reflects the treated individual by incorporating their exact baseline characteristics, minimizing selection bias compared to a matched historical control. From Adult Applications to Pediatric Rare Diseases

A representative application of QSP‐based VPs is introduced for Gaucher's disease type 1 (GD1), which simulates patient responses to both enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and substrate reduction therapy (SRT) with eliglustat in adult populations [3]. The model captures a range of disease severities and has been successfully used to predict therapeutic outcomes across diverse genotype–phenotype presentations. Building upon this result, the same GD1 model was employed by Simoni et al. [4] to develop a model‐based phenotype clustering approach. This methodology combined mechanistic knowledge with sparse experimental data to identify clinically relevant patient subgroups emerging during treatment—distinctions not readily discernible from the data alone. This case highlights the potential of mechanistic models to support precision medicine, especially when data availability is limited, as it may happen in the case of rare diseases. A similar strategy was recently applied in another rare lysosomal storage disorder, the Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency (ASMD). In this application, a QSP model supported pediatric extrapolation from adult data for olipudase alfa treatment. By demonstrating mechanistic similarity across age groups, the model enabled regulatory acceptance for pediatric use despite limited clinical data [5], paving the way for similar efforts in the future. In the pediatric context, QSP models may either be developed de novo or adapted from adult models. The latter requires structural modifications and parameter adjustments to capture age‐dependent physiological features. Long‐term simulations must accommodate developmental changes such as organ growth and maturation, which may push the model beyond its original assumptions. An exemplary pediatric case is the model described by Paris et al. [6], which captures the dynamics of phosphorylated neurofilament heavy subunits (pNfH) in children with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). Derived from an earlier adult model [7], it incorporates SMA‐specific pathophysiology and pediatric treatment with the antisense oligonucleotide nusinersen. Calibrated using clinical trial data, the model effectively predicts pNfH trajectories in treated and untreated patients, enabling in silico evaluation of disease progression and treatment efficacy in pediatric SMA.

Methodology for Success

The development of both successful ML‐ and QSP‐based VPs relies on the integration of heterogeneous data sources, spanning multiple sources (clinical records, genetic information, and biomarker profiles). High‐quality, multi‐scale datasets are essential for capturing the complexity of these diseases. Strategies such as data harmonization and standardization are critical to ensure consistency across studies and cohorts, while ethical considerations remain central when working with sensitive pediatric data. For ML‐based VPs, a common concern is limited data availability, particularly because ML is notoriously data hungry. Strict privacy regulations around pediatric data limit inter‐institutional sharing and exacerbate the problem, resulting in small, isolated cohorts, which are often insufficient for comprehensive ML model training and validation. A key aspect for success is thus a collective effort to gather pediatric rare disease data into research‐ready databases, as done, for example, by PRO‐ACT for adult ALS patients. Moreover, knowledge transfer from larger or related datasets can compensate for pediatric data scarcity. For example, a 2023 study [8] showed that self‐supervised models trained on adult inpatient data improved pediatric clinical predictions. In QSP, model development must account for the unique characteristics of pediatric physiology from the beginning. This includes integrating time‐dependent changes in anatomical and physiological parameters, such as organ growth and enzyme maturation, which influence drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Scalability is also crucial—models must be adaptable across age groups and disease subtypes, often requiring extensions of adult models to address pediatric‐specific pathophysiological mechanisms. Calibration and validation are foundational pillars of the modeling process. Rigorous testing against clinical trial data, such as those employed in the SMA and lysosomal storage disorders QSP applications presented in the previous section [3, 4, 5, 6], ensures reliability and predictive power. Iterative refinement, informed by emerging clinical and experimental insights, enables models to evolve and maintain relevance. This dynamic, data‐driven, and patient‐centric approach is fundamental in pediatric and rare diseases, where traditional trial designs are often constrained, and QSP modeling can serve as a bridge to informed and individualized treatment planning.

Future Directions

Integration of VP methodology into drug development pipelines and acceptance by regulatory agencies will require a step‐wise and context‐of‐use dependent approach, in which the tight control of the risk for patients is a key requirement [9]. Pre‐requisites for the development of models and methodologies are the sharing of high‐quality datasets by the community and the implementation of rigorous, step‐wise model testing by computational scientists. Notably, key successful applications have followed open science initiatives—such as those for ALS or Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy—demonstrating the potential value of such data sharing for young patients around the globe. Furthermore, it must be noted that although ML and QSP reside on opposite ends of the modeling spectrum (heavily data‐driven vs. heavily biological insight‐driven), this also means that they are highly complementary, and the strengths of each approach have the potential to strengthen the weaknesses of the other. Integrated QSP + ML approaches effectively harness the strengths of both methodologies [10], with mechanistic assumptions of QSP railing in the plasticity of ML, improving generalization and lowering the quantity of data needed. In applications where data is sparse—such as rare pediatric diseases—hybrid approaches can help bridge the gap to making these methodologies applicable.

Conclusion

The implementation of QSP and ML‐based VPs in rare pediatric diseases has the potential to optimize and accelerate trials, support biomarker selection as a key outcome, increase reliability and statistical power of the trial, resulting in enhanced probability of trial success. However, broad development and application of such technologies relies on the collective commitment to make data accessible, allowing the scientific and pharmaceutical communities to build methodologies, VPs, and trust. Establishing high‐quality data sharing as the standard in pediatric rare diseases would ensure sustainable and effective use of data, maximizing its impact on drug development and, ultimately, patient care.
