Abstract

Despite attempts to control the spread of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) through the use of anti-HIV medications, the absence of an effective vaccine continues to present a significant obstacle. In addition, the development of drug resistance by HIV underscores the necessity for computational drug discovery methods to identify novel therapies. This investigation specifically focused on employing a long short-term memory (LSTM) variational autoencoder deep-learning architecture for computational drug discovery in relation to HIV. Our data set comprised simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES)-encoded compounds, which were used to train the LSTM autoencoder. Remarkably, our model achieved a training accuracy of 91%, with a data set containing 1377 compounds. Leveraging the generative model derived from the training phase, we generated potential new drugs for combating HIV and assessed their interaction with the virus using a previously developed artificial intelligence model. Lastly, we verified the drug likeliness of our computationally generated compounds in accordance with Lipinski's rule of five. Overall, our study presents a promising approach to computational drug discovery in the ongoing battle against HIV.

INTRODUCTION

The global health landscape has been significantly impacted by the emergence of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), an infectious pandemic that originated in Africa and subsequently spread across the world. According to the World Health Organization's published data, approximately 1.5 million new infections occurred in 2021, resulting in a staggering global estimate of 38.4 million individuals living with HIV. In the same report, it is projected that around 650,000 people lost their lives as a result of HIV‐related illnesses. 1 Research conducted from 1990 to the early 2000s reveals a notable upward trend in the HIV epidemic, followed by a decline from 2004 to 2019. However, despite a decrease in HIV‐related deaths during the past two decades, the prevalence of the virus has continued to rise, accumulating an alarming number of cases. 2 This study emphasizes the significance and achievements of drug discovery endeavors targeting HIV as well as the effectiveness of antiviral medications in suppressing the virus. HIV is a group of viruses that belong to the family of retroviruses. Retroviruses are viruses that encode their own DNA polymerase and use the host cell's DNA polymerase to replicate its own DNA. Retroviruses have single‐stranded, positive‐sense RNA genomes that are transcribed into double‐stranded, proviral DNA after the reverse transcription stage. HIV specifically targets cluster of differantiation CD4+ T lymphocytes and destroys them, which ultimately leads to the immune system's collapse. HIV is able to escape the immune system by using several mechanisms, including the following: (1) the virus expresses several proteins that act to block the effects of the immune system, (2) the virus expresses proteins that block the effect of antiviral drugs, and (3) the virus mutates rapidly. 3 Several drugs have been developed that are able to suppress HIV. However, the virus is able to mutate rapidly and develops resistance to these drugs; therefore, drug discovery studies are constantly being conducted to identify new drugs that can suppress HIV. 4 Drug discovery is a pharmacological process where time, cost, and accuracy are crucial, and this is confirmed given one successful drug discovery takes about 10–15 years and costs $1.1 billion, and 90% of candidate drugs fail in clinical testing. 5 , 6 , 7 At present, with the development of technology, computer‐aided drug discovery (CAAD) methods are actively used to shorten the research cycle and reduce the cost and risk of clinical failure in the drug discovery process. The drug discovery process consists of several stages and a series of actions as shown in Figure 1. Typically, the process can be divided into the following four main stages: early drug discovery, preclinical phase, clinical phases, and regulatory approval. CAAD techniques help this process in the early stages, where researchers collaborate to identify and optimize potential candidate drugs. 8 There are numerous methods used for drug discovery in the literature, such as in silico, in vitro, de novo, and so on. 9 In this research, we studied de novo drug discovery with a deep‐learning approach for HIV. De novo drug discovery is basically based on the discovery of compounds with desired properties. However, the methods used to predict the reactivity and interaction of the novel‐discovered compounds are as important as their discovery process. 10 , 11 In a previous study conducted with another research team for a different purpose, in order to test the interactions of potential compounds that have been obtained against HIV, 12 a geometric deep‐learning approach was proposed to predict drug resistance to HIV, and for the virus–drug interaction, the authors used a different artificial intelligence (AI) model. In the previous study, the main purpose of the AI model developed with a different research team was not to produce a new drug, but was designed to measure the efficacy of an existing drug and to predict drug resistance to HIV and virus–drug interaction. In the current research, to prove the drug likeliness and oral administration of our computationally generated drugs, we tested those drugs with the Lipinski rule of five (LRoF). 13 Oral administration is a desirable but not mandatory method during the drug treatment of diseases. 14 The LRoF is conceived to aid the development of orally bioavailable small‐molecule drugs. LRoF was proposed to detect drugs with high feasibility and potential among the high number of candidate compounds. Reviewing the study published by Zhang et al. in 2007, 15 we observe that 51% of Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved drugs comply with LRoF, and thus we can say that LRoF can be accepted as an auxiliary standard during drug discovery. Another factor we had to consider in the drug discovery process was toxicity. Toxicity is a critical consideration as it can limit the safety and efficacy of a drug candidate. 16 Therefore, toxicity testing is an integral part of the drug development process, and it is typically performed at various stages of the drug discovery process, including preclinical and clinical trials. In this study, we used the ProTox‐II toxicity prediction tool, 17 which employs computational models to predict the toxicity of chemicals to aquatic organisms. The selection of aquatic organisms as a focus for toxicity prediction is based on their use as a preliminary indicator in environmental toxicity studies, providing insights that can be extrapolated to potential toxicity in humans. All candidate drugs proposed in this study have passed all 17 toxicity tests in ProTox‐II, and none had a lethal dose 50 (LD50) 18 value less than 2000 mg/kg, indicating low acute toxicity. In recent years, the application of deep‐learning models in the field of drug discovery has garnered significant attention, offering the possibility of tailoring drug candidates for specific biological targets. 19 One promising area in this domain is the use of molecular generative models. These models, by leveraging the power of deep learning, can potentially identify molecules with desired properties. Notably, a challenge that has emerged is the generation of syntactically invalid molecules by these deep‐learning models, which poses a bottleneck for their application in drug discovery processes. 20 To address this challenge, 21 a novel approach is introduced that hinges on the use of a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) for generating drug candidates. Instead of relying on diverse physical properties for training, the method uniquely employs molecular fingerprints coupled with the results of the growth inhibition of 50% (a metric used to assess the concentration of a compound required to inhibit 50% of cell growth), pertaining to the inhibition of growth by 50% for breast cancer cell lines. Their findings demonstrate that the CVAE can generate fingerprints, which, even if absent from the training data set, encapsulate the desired molecular properties. Furthermore, the versatility of their approach extends beyond just molecular generation; it holds potential for query expansion, enabling the search of databases using generated fingerprints as expanded queries. The advancement of deep‐generative models tailored for graph‐structured data has ushered in novel perspectives on chemical synthesis. 22 A salient feature of these models is their potential to maneuver directly through the expansive and discrete landscape of chemical structures, therefore eliminating the need for resource‐intensive search processes. An innovative model in this category, introduced as molecular generative adversarial network (MolGAN) by De Cao and Kipf, 23 brings to light an implicit, likelihood‐free generative approach specifically designed for small‐molecular graphs. Contrary to prior likelihood‐based techniques that necessitated costly graph‐matching processes or specific node‐ordering heuristics, MolGAN ingeniously repurposes generative adversarial networks to interact directly with graph‐structured data. To enhance its generative prowess, 23 integrating MolGAN with a reinforcement learning objective, thereby it was fostered the creation of molecules enriched with particular chemical attributes. Empirical evaluations conducted on the renowned QM9 chemical database underscored MolGAN's competence, showcasing its ability to produce nearly 100% valid chemical compounds. This makes MolGAN particularly commendable, especially when juxtaposed against recent methodologies that are rooted in string‐based (simplified molecular input line entry system [SMILES]) representations or those that directly generate graphs through likelihood‐based approaches. Another groundbreaking development in the field of chemical synthesis has been the introduction of methods to seamlessly transition between discrete and continuous representations of molecules. 24 A pioneering study by Gómez‐Bombarelli et al. 25 delineates an innovative methodology that facilitates not only the conversion of discrete molecular representations into a multidimensional continuous form but also the reverse transformation. Such a continuous framework has a myriad of applications. Not only does it enable the automatic generation of novel chemical entities through elementary operations in the latent space, such as decoding random vectors, slight perturbations of known structures, or even interpolations between distinct molecules, but also it ushers in the potential for gradient‐based optimization. This optimization harnesses the power of continuous representations, providing a more directed and efficient pathway to discover optimized functional compounds. As a testament to its versatility, Gómez‐Bombarelli et al. 25 showcased its efficacy in the realm of drug‐like molecules and further among molecules characterized by fewer than nine heavy atoms. The domain of de novo molecular design has witnessed the inception of various sophisticated models aimed at generating chemical entities with specified properties. One such promising approach was proposed by Lim et al., 26 wherein they used a molecular generative model grounded on the CVAE framework. A distinct hallmark of their methodology is its specialization in modulating multiple molecular attributes concurrently. This is achieved by enforcing these properties directly upon the latent space of the model, ensuring a harmonized generation process. Validating the prowess and versatility of their approach, Lim et al. 26 conducted a proof‐of‐concept study in which their model adeptly generated drug‐like molecules, targeting an impressive array of five distinct properties. In this study, we used a long‐short term memory (LSTM) variational autoencoder (VAE) architecture as the computational method for drug discovery. We would like to explain the concept of LSTM VAE as LSTM and VAE separately. First, LSTM 27 is a deep‐learning architecture that has been proposed as a remedy for the long‐term dependence issue of recurrent neural networks (RNNs). Unlike standard feedforward neural networks such as dense neural networks, 28 convolutional neural networks (CNNs), 29 and graph neural networks (GNNs), 30 LSTM has feedback connections as well. As a result of the feedback connections in LSTM, it is capable of learning sequence features from data, and thus LSTM is widely used for natural language processing (NLP) problems because those kinds of problems require learning grammatical rules and past‐linked information and understanding time series. As our data set is in the SMILES 31 format, which is developed for computers to understand and has its own grammatical rules and conventions, we thought an LSTM architecture would be suitable for the main task. Second, VAE 32 is a generative deep‐learning technique that can learn the probabilistic distribution underlying the data set and describes a latent space (also called embedding space) for the source data set. VAEs are a type of machine‐learning model that belong to the family of generative models. They are designed to learn the underlying probability distribution of a data set, enabling them to generate new data points that resemble the training data. VAEs achieve this by encoding the input data into a lower dimensional latent space and then reconstructing the data from this space. The “variational” aspect of VAEs comes from the use of probabilistic graphical models and the application of Bayesian inference, making them capable of handling a wide variety of data types. In the context of drug discovery, VAEs can be particularly powerful, as they enable the generation of novel molecular structures that maintain the properties of known compounds, therefore facilitating the exploration of chemical space for potential drug candidates. 32 The VAE technique can be used with different deep‐learning architectures, such as LSTM, CNN, and GNN. In this study, because we have a mainly generative task and a sequential data set, we combined the generative abilities of VAE and the learning abilities of LSTM from sequential data. This article makes significant contributions to the field of drug discovery for HIV, aiming to accelerate and optimize the identification of potential therapeutic compounds. By leveraging innovative computational techniques, particularly the LSTM VAE architecture, we present a novel approach that holds great promise for combating HIV. METHODS › Data

In this study, we obtained data from the MoleculeNet database 33 for the training phase of the proposed generative deep‐learning model. We used text data in SMILES format to train our LSTM network. SMILES is a notation to describe the structure of chemical entities using American Standard Code for Information Interchange characters. There are 17 different data sets in the MoleculeNet database for different tasks, such as quantum mechanics, biophysics, and physiochemistry. The data set we used in our study consists of experimentally measured HIV replication inhibiting compounds. METHODS › Data › Vectorization (Preprocessing)

In NLP applications, extracting meaningful information from the given text requires the text encoded into vectors such that the texts can share a similar context closely in a vector space; this technique is called vectorization. In this study, we used one‐hot encoding technique as the vectorization technique. To perform one‐hot vectorization in classic NLP tasks, each word in a sentence is represented as a one‐hot vector, and thus a vectorized sentence is represented as a matrix. As our data set is in SMILES format, we considered each character in SMILES as a one‐hot vector; therefore, each SMILES is represented as an array of one‐hot vectors or an M × N matrix (M is the embedding size, and N is the vocabulary size). As an example, Figure 2 from the study by Griffiths et al. 34 is a good example of one‐hot vectorization for SMILES data. In the context of one‐hot encoded SMILES, the term vocabulary refers to the particular group of tokens that are contained in the SMILES strings. Element, unique symbol, and bond representations are some of these tokens. For instance, the vocabulary would include the letters ['C', 'O', '=', '(', ')'] if we take the SMILES strings 'CCO' and 'C=C' into consideration. As a result, the model can describe and handle the various structural features found in the SMILES data. To explain further, the vocabulary would be restricted to ['1', 'c', 'n'] if our data set contained SMILES such as 'c1ccccc1' (benzene) and 'n1ccccc1' (pyridine). This is because of the specific characters presented in these SMILES representations. In the construction of our one‐hot matrixes for each of the SMILES, we defined the embedding size as 128 to not lose the valuable information from long SMILES and to add less padding to the shorter SMILES, and we have set the vocabulary size as 57, which is the same as the dictionary size of the data set. Therefore, each one‐hot vectorized matrix corresponding to each SMILES has the size of 128 × 57. METHODS › Model validation

The detailed model development explanation can be found in Appendix S1. Figures S4–S7 depict the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence score, mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of our proposed LSTM VAE model, respectively. In our proposed model, KL divergence is used as a regularization term to encourage the model to learn the latent space that is more likely to produce samples that resemble the training data. It is obvious to say a steadily decreasing KL divergence score indicates that the model can learn a good representation of the latent space, where the distribution of latent variables is close to the assumed prior distribution. MSE is a metric for evaluating regression models, including LSTM VAEs. It measures the average squared difference between the predicted and actual values. A steady decrease in the MSE of our proposed model indicates that the model's predictions are close to the actual values. However, MSE is sensitive to outliers because it squares the difference, so it may not be the best metric to use in cases where there are extreme observations. Hence, we thought it was also important to consider the MAE metric, which is less sensitive to outliers compared with MSE because it does not square the difference. As can be seen in the performance graphics, the MAE metric also indicates stable learning along 250 epochs. Finally, we examined how our model performed according to the MAPE metric during training. Unlike MSE and MAE, which are expressed in the same units as the original data, MAPE is expressed as a percentage and can be useful for understanding the magnitude of the error. In the supplementary figures, Figure S7 demonstrates the MAPE performance, in there we can easily observe that in the training and validation data, MAPE scores are very close to each other, which indicates good learning quality, and there is no overfit through training.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The findings of this study demonstrated the effectiveness of using an LSTM autoencoder for computational drug discovery for HIV. The LSTM network was trained on a data set of SMILES‐encoded compounds, which were one‐hot encoded to create a matrix representation of each compound. The generative model obtained from the training phase was used to generate potential new drugs for HIV. Finally, the drug likeliness of the computationally generated drugs was confirmed according to LRoF. The use of deep learning for drug discovery has become increasingly popular in recent years, as it provides a powerful tool for generating new drugs with desirable properties. The LSTM network used in this study is a type of RNN that is specifically designed for processing sequential data, such as SMILES‐encoded compounds. The LSTM network can capture long‐term dependencies in the data, which is essential for identifying the complex relationships between molecular structure and drug activity. The one‐hot encoding technique used in this study is a widely used approach for encoding categorical data. In NLP applications, one‐hot encoding is used to represent words as vectors, where each dimension represents a specific word in the vocabulary. In this study, each character in the SMILES sequence was represented as a one‐hot vector, which allowed the LSTM network to learn the relationships between different chemical groups and their impact on drug activity. The results of this study showed that the LSTM autoencoder was able to achieve an accuracy of 92% during training using a data set of 1377 compounds. The generative model obtained from the training phase was then used to generate potential new drugs for HIV. The results of this evaluation were promising, suggesting that the computationally generated drugs may have potential therapeutic effects. To provide a comprehensive overview of our model's performance, we analyzed the distribution of predicted interaction scores for the generated molecules. Specifically, we highlighted the proportion of molecules that achieved interaction scores above the 0.9 threshold, showcasing the model's ability to generate compounds with high predicted efficacy against HIV. Finally, the drug likeliness of the computationally generated drugs was confirmed according to LRoF. This rule provides a set of guidelines for predicting the pharmacokinetic properties of potential drug candidates based on their molecular properties. The rule states that a drug candidate is likely to have good oral bioavailability and be well tolerated if it has a molecular weight of less than 500 Da, a log P value less than 5, no more than five hydrogen bond donors, and no more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors. The fact that the computationally generated drugs satisfied LRoF suggests that they have the potential to be developed as new drugs for HIV.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION › Limitations and future studies

Although our study provides promising results for computational drug discovery for HIV, there are several limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, the accuracy of our model is dependent on the quality and quantity of the training data. In this study, we used a data set of 1377 compounds to train our LSTM autoencoder model. Although this data set is relatively large, it is possible that a larger data set could lead to even more accurate generations. In addition, the quality of the data set may also affect the accuracy of the model. Second, although this evaluation method is prevalent in computational drug discovery, it cannot replace in vitro or in vivo testing. Although we highlighted the uniqueness of the generated compounds based on existing compound databases, we acknowledge that a more detailed similarity analysis would contribute to a deeper understanding of the model's performance. To calculate similarity/diversity between generated drugs and the drugs in the training data set, a Tanimoto coefficient would provide a valuable layer of analysis. 35 Therefore, further testing is necessary to confirm the effectiveness of the computationally generated drugs. Third, our model only considered LRoF as a measure of drug likeliness. Although this is a widely used rule in drug discovery, it is not the only factor that determines the success of a drug. Other factors such as toxicity, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics should also be considered in future studies. Finally, our study focused specifically on HIV drug discovery. Although the LSTM autoencoder model can be applied to other drug discovery efforts, the specific features of the target disease or condition must be considered when developing the model.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the development of our proposed generative AI model, based on the LSTM VAE architecture, has demonstrated the remarkable potential of computational drug discovery in revolutionizing the drug development process. By leveraging the capabilities of this model, we have showcased the ability to discover candidate compounds with desired properties for combating HIV. The application of advanced computational tools and techniques has allowed us to screen a vast number of compounds in a remarkably short period, enabling the identification of potential drug candidates in a more efficient and cost‐effective manner. Also, the ProTox‐II toxicity prediction program, 17 which uses computational models to foretell the toxicity of chemicals to aquatic organisms, was used in this study. All of the candidate drugs put forth in our study passed all 17 ProTox‐II toxicity tests, and none of them had an LD50 value of less than 2000 mg/kg, which denotes minimal acute toxicity. Furthermore, our study highlights the significant role of computational methods in optimizing the structures of potential drug candidates. By leveraging the power of the LSTM VAE, the proposed model could show a potential for enhanced efficacy and safety after clinical tests are performed. Although challenges remain, such as the accuracy of computational models and the need for experimental validation, our study serves as a testament to the potential of computational drug discovery to revolutionize the development of therapeutics. Through continued research and development, including the refinement of algorithms and the integration of experimental validation, the accuracy and reliability of computational models can be further enhanced.
