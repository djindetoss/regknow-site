Abstract

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) would progress to chronicity or death. Large language models (LLMs) may enhance clinical decision-making, yet their utility relative to physicians in DILI remains unclear. Therefore, we evaluated their performance in predicting DILI outcomes. We enrolled 943 DILI patients from three centers as internal and external cohorts. Based on 12-month follow-up, outcomes were classified as recovery, 6/12-month chronicity, and death. LLMs (Gemini-2.5 Pro, GPT-5.1, DeepSeek-3.2), hepatologists (Junior, middle, senior), and models (Hy's Law, nHy's Law, MELD Score) estimated probabilities of outcomes. LLM-Rules (VOTE, OR, AND) were applied to enhance stability. Model performance was assessed. For 6-month chronicity, the senior achieved highest AUROC (0.61) with an accuracy of 70%. Gemini-2.5 Pro and GPT-5.1 yielded AUROCs of 0.60 and 0.59, respectively, outperforming junior and middle hepatologists. Gemini-2.5 Pro demonstrated strongest agreement with senior (κ = 0.43). LLMs all exhibited lower accuracy and specificity than hepatologists. A similar result was observed in 12-month chronicity. For overall mortality, the senior achieved highest AUROC (0.87) with an accuracy of 83%. Gemini-2.5 Pro and GPT-5.1 achieved AUROCs of 0.86, outperforming junior hepatologist, Hy's Law, and nHy's Law. GPT-5.1 achieved strongest agreement with senior (κ = 0.25). LLM-Rules demonstrated stability for predicting outcomes across cohorts. OR and AND rules improved sensitivity and specificity, respectively. GPT-5.1 and Gemini-2.5 Pro showed AUROCs approaching senior hepatologists for DILI outcomes with limited accuracy and specificity. LLM-Rules demonstrated stable performance across cohorts with improved sensitivity or specificity, supporting the potential of multi-LLM approaches as clinician-supervised complementary tools.

Introduction

Drug‐induced liver injury (DILI) refers to hepatic damage caused by agents, which is a leading aetiology of acute liver failure in Western populations [1]. Without timely liver transplantation, DILI‐acute liver failure carries a high mortality rate [2, 3]. Furthermore, approximately 6%–39% of patients would progress to chronic DILI, potentially resulting in cirrhosis and significant deterioration in quality of life [4, 5]. With the increasing global DILI incidence, early identification of patients at risk for chronicity or fatal outcomes remains a critical clinical challenge. Primary care represents the critical first line for identifying patients at risk of adverse outcomes [6, 7]. Several predictive models have been developed for mortality or chronicity in DILI patients. The Hy's Law, nHy's Law, Ghabril Score, MELD score, DrILTox ALF Score, and Robles‐Diaz Model were constructed for predicting mortality [8, 9, 10, 11, 12], which could be used to predict mortality. Nevertheless, the precision could vary with physicians' experience. Moreover, tools for liver fibrosis were reported to be used infrequently in primary care [13], and these models for DILI outcomes might also be used infrequently. Several noninvasive models including prolonged total bilirubin (TBIL), clinic‐radiomics model, and metabolomics analysis were reported to predict chronic DILI [5, 14, 15], while these methods could be inconvenient in primary care. Therefore, accurate and straightforward predictive tools are needed to help physicians identify high‐risk DILI patients in routine and primary healthcare settings. Large language models (LLMs) represent artificial intelligence systems that utilise deep learning algorithms trained on massive internet‐derived datasets to generate human‐like text responses, which have demonstrated transformative potential across multiple facets [16]. LLMs also demonstrate prospective applications in medical diagnostics and clinical risk prediction emerging as a prominent area of scientific investigation [17]. Emerging studies demonstrated their potential in assisting with disease classification and clinical decision support, as well as in identifying metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, predicting immunotherapy response in hepatocellular carcinoma, and diagnosing focal liver lesions [18, 19, 20]. An evaluation study reported the potential of LLMs in answering questions on DILI [21], while their applications in DILI outcomes have not been explored in cohorts. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the performance of three generic LLMs (GPT‐5.1, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, DeepSeek‐3.2) in predicting DILI outcomes and compare them with hepatologists of different experience levels in a multicenter cohort, exploring their potential role in clinical decision support.

Materials and Methods › Patients

From November 2015 to March 2025, hospitalised DILI patients from three medical centers were enrolled. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ruijin Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Written informed consent of all subjects was obtained. This study was reported in accordance with TRIPOD‐LLM guidelines [22].

Materials and Methods › Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) Liver biochemistries met one of the following criteria [23]: (i) alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ≥ 5 × upper limit of normal (ULN); (ii) alkaline phosphatase (ALP) ≥ 2 × ULN (particularly accompanied by an increased γ‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) level with bone disease ruled out); (iii) ALT ≥ 3 × ULN and TBIL ≥ 2 × ULN; (3) Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM) score ≥ 6; (4) available follow‐up data for outcome assessment. Exclusion criteria: (1) other underlying liver injuries with definite etiologies, including systemic diseases; (2) patients receiving harmful drugs during the follow‐up period. Three hepatologists assessed patients based on above criteria. The final cohort enrolled 943 patients, who enrolled from Ruijin hospital were defined as internal cohort (N = 840), enrolled from other two medical centers were defined as external cohort (N = 103) (Figure 1). Notably, the external cohort might be small to result in instability in predictive performance. Therefore, the external cohort was interpreted as an external exploratory assessment. Materials and Methods › Outcomes

The primary endpoint of chronic DILI was defined as the evidence of persistent liver injury in liver tests, histology, or radiology over 6 months without other etiologies [24]. The secondary endpoint of chronic DILI was defined as persistent liver injury lasting for more than 12 months after DILI onset without other etiologies [25]. Overall fatal outcome was defined as the death from any cause or receipt of liver transplantation. The contribution of DILI was adjudicated as primary, contributory, or unrelated based on published criteria [26].

Materials and Methods › Data Collection

To ensure fair comparison and reflect primary care scenarios, all evaluators assessed identical standardised case summaries derived from easily available baseline clinical data. Clinical data were obtained from the electronic records, including demographic information (age, sex, height, weight, body mass index [BMI]), comorbid conditions (diabetes, malignancy, cardiovascular diseases), clinical signs and symptoms, as well as the laboratory parameters. These laboratory parameters included ALT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ALP, GGT, TBIL, direct bilirubin (DBIL), albumin (ALB), total bile acid, creatine, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein, low‐density lipoprotein, free fatty acids, C‐reactive protein (CRP), alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP), prothrombin time (PT), international normalised ratio (INR), white blood cell count, neutrophils%, eosinophils%, haemoglobin, and platelets. All variables were complete after applying exclusion criteria. According to R‐value (ALT/ALP ratio with normal upper limits at DILI onset), injured patterns were classified as hepatocellular (R ≥ 5), mixed (2 < R < 5), or cholestatic (R ≤ 2) [27].

Materials and Methods › Evaluators Predicting Chronicity and Overall Mortality

As shown in Figure 2A, we first integrated the clinical data including patient ID, clinical symptoms, and clinical characteristics. Next, a question document for each patient was generated based on clinical data after data anonymisation. Subsequently, the question documents were submitted to LLMs (GPT‐5.1, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, Deepseek‐3.2) and varied experience hepatologists (junior, middle, senior) for evaluating the probability of chronic DILI and overall mortality. The performance of each LLM and hepatologist was assessed (including prediction performance, kappa value, and evaluation time). All case documents were anonymised and encrypted in transit and at rest to protect patient privacy. For the LLMs, we employed the application programming interface for each LLM in python environment to process large volumes of data and ensure the consistency in responses. The question document and prompt were submitted to LLMs to evaluate the probability of chronic DILI and overall mortality. Each patient was assessed three times, generating the average probability as the final result [19] (see Figure 2B for the case example). The evaluation time of each LLM for each patient was recorded. Representative evaluation examples for chronic and overall mortality outcomes were provided in Figures S1A,B and S2. For hepatologists, question documents were independently reviewed by three hepatologists from Ruijin Hospital with medical doctorate degrees and expertise in liver diseases, particularly DILI. Based on years of clinical experience, they were classified as senior (RTL, 20 years), middle (RDM, 8 years), junior (YND, 3 years) hepatologists. Each evaluator independently estimated the probability of chronic DILI and overall mortality. Furthermore, we compared the performance between LLMs and existing models (Hy's Law [8], nHy's Law [9], MELD Score [28]) in predicting overall mortality to evaluate their clinical utility. To enhance performance stability, three rules were employed based on three LLMs. OR Rule (Sensitivity‐enhancing strategy): A patient is classified as positive if any of the three LLMs predicts a positive outcome. This rule maximises sensitivity at the cost of lower specificity. AND Rule (Specificity‐enhancing strategy): A patient is classified as positive only if all three LLMs consistently predict a positive outcome. This rule favours higher specificity and reduces false positives. VOTE Rule (Majority‐based consensus strategy): A patient is classified as positive when at least two of the three LLMs predict a positive outcome. This method balances sensitivity and specificity through majority consensus. Given the diversity of trigger drugs in patients, we further analysed the performance of evaluators separately in cases caused by traditional herbs or conventional drugs. Workflow of study design. Materials and Methods › Statistical Analysis

The performance of predictive models was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV). Optimal classification thresholds were determined in overall cohort using Youden index and were applied to internal and external cohorts. The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were compared using McNemar's test, while DeLong's test assessed AUROC. The Kappa analysis evaluated intra consistency (hepatologists group, LLMs group, rules group, existing model group) and interrater agreement by Fleiss' κ and Cohen's κ coefficient, respectively. κ values were interpreted as: ≤ 0.20 (slight), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (substantial), and 0.81–1.00 (almost perfect) agreement. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Python software (version 3.12.7).

Results › Baseline Characteristics

Table 1 summarises the clinical characteristics of DILI patients across the overall, internal, and external cohorts. Among 943 patients, the median age was 55 years and 66.60% were female. Overall, 606 patients (64.26%) recovered, 254 (26.94%) developed 6‐month chronicity, and 83 (8.80%) experienced overall fatal outcomes. Similar outcome distributions were observed in the internal and external cohorts, with recovery in 534 (63.57%) and 72 (69.90%), 6‐month chronicity in 229 (27.26%) and 25 (24.27%), and overall fatal outcomes in 77 (9.17%) and 6 (5.83%) patients, respectively. There were 213 (22.59%), 191 (22.74%), 22 (21.36%) patients progressed into 12‐month chronicity in overall, internal, external cohorts, respectively. Results › Performance of LLMs and Hepatologists in Predicting Chronic DILI

The performance of LLMs and hepatologists in predicting 6‐month chronicity was summarised in Figure 3A–E,L and Table 2. For 6‐month chronicity prediction, the cutoff probabilities were 20%, 21%, and 30% for junior, middle, and senior hepatologists, respectively; 17.83%, 23.33%, and 12.33% for Deepseek‐3.2, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, and GPT‐5.1, respectively. Among hepatologists, the senior showed the highest AUROC of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.57–0.65, Figure 3B) and best sensitivity of 0.41 (95% CI: 0.35–0.47, Figure 3D) with numerically highest accuracy of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.73, Figure 3C), while the middle hepatologist showed best specificity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.89, Figure 3E). Among LLMs, they presented similar AUROCs, while Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 showed the superior AUROC of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.56–0.65, Figure 3B) and 0.59 (95% CI: 0.55–0.63, Figure 3B) to junior and middle hepatologists, respectively. The Gemini‐2.5 Pro presented the best accuracy and specificity in LLMs, while remained inferior to middle and senior hepatologists (Figure 3C). Three LLMs presented superior sensitivity to three hepatologists, where the Deepseek‐3.2 showed best sensitivity of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.75–0.85, Figure 3D). Furthermore, LLM‐Rule presented an approaching AUROC of 0.60, superior to junior and middle hepatologists (Figure 3B). The AND rule presented the best accuracy (0.64, 95% CI: 0.61–0.68), superior to other rules while remaining inferior to senior hepatologist (Figure 3C). The AND rule also showed the best specificity (0.73, 95% CI: 0.69–0.76) than other rules and LLMs, inferior to middle and senior hepatologists (Figure 3E). The OR rule presented the best sensitivity of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.85–0.92) in all evaluators (Figure 3D). The performance of evaluators in predicting 12‐month chronicity was summarised in Figure S3A–E and Table S4. For 12‐month chronicity prediction, the cutoff probabilities were 9%, 13%, and 30% for junior, middle, and senior hepatologists, respectively; 17.37%, 16.67%, and 14.33% for Deepseek‐3.2, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, and GPT‐5.1, respectively. The senior hepatologist achieved the highest AUROC of 0.61. Among LLMs, Gemini‐2.5 Pro achieved the highest AUROC of 0.60, which increased to 0.61 using the AND rule. Furthermore, the AND rule increased the accuracy and specificity of LLMs, inferior to three hepatologists. The OR rule achieved the best sensitivity of 0.87. Results › Performance of LLMs and Hepatologists in Predicting Overall Mortality

The performance of LLMs and hepatologists in predicting overall mortality was summarised in Figure 3F–J,L and Table 2. The cutoff probabilities were 15%, 16%, and 30% for the junior, middle, and senior hepatologists, respectively, 15.77%, 14%, and 7% for Deepseek‐3.2, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, and GPT‐5.1, respectively. As shown in Figure 3G,H, the senior presented the numerical best AUROC (0.87, 95% CI: 0.82–0.91) and accuracy (0.83, 95% CI: 0.80–0.85) among all evaluators. GPT‐5.1, Gemini‐2.5 Pro, OR rule, and AND rule showed approaching AUROCs (0.86) to senior hepatologist, superior to junior hepatologist. Among LLMs, GPT‐5.1 presented the numerical highest accuracy of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.74–0.80), while the AND rule presented better accuracy of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.77–0.83), superior to junior hepatologist. As shown in Figure 3I, OR rule and Gemini‐2.5 Pro presented the highest numerical sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.93) among all evaluators; the senior hepatologist showed a sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.69–0.87). As shown in Figure 3J, the senior showed the best specificity of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.81–0.86), followed by AND rule (0.80, 95% CI: 0.77–0.82), inferior to junior hepatologist. We further compared the performance of Hy's Law, nHy's Law, MELD Score, and LLMs in predicting overall mortality (Figure S4A–E and Table S4). The optimal cutoff for MELD Score was 17.15. Among existing models, MELD Score achieved the best AUROC of 0.84, while Gemini‐2.5 Pro, GPT‐5.1, OR rule, and AND rule achieved slightly higher AUROC of 0.86. Among all evaluators, the MELD Score achieved the best accuracy of 0.81 and specificity of 0.82, followed by AND rule's accuracy and specificity of 0.80. The Gemini‐2.5 Pro and OR rule achieved the highest sensitivity of 0.86.

Results › Subgroup Analysis

The prediction performance of evaluators in patients caused by traditional herbs or conventional drugs was summarised in Table S6 and Figures S5–S8. For the 6‐month chronicity, the senior hepatologist achieved AUROC of 0.58–0.61 among cohorts in herb‐induced patients (Figure S5A), while the AUROC was 0.43–0.64 in conventional drug‐induced patients (Figure S5E). Among cohorts, three rules exhibited AUROC of 0.60–0.64 and 0.48–0.64 in herb‐induced and conventional drug‐induced patients, respectively (Figure S5A,E). For the 12‐month chronicity, the senior hepatologist, LLMs, and rules achieved similar AUROCs. Compared to herb‐induced cases, AUROCs presented an increasing level in conventional drug‐induced cases among overall and internal cohorts, while the level decreased in the external cohort (Figure S6A,E). For the overall mortality, among three cohorts, the senior hepatologist achieved AUROC of 0.89–0.96 and 0.82–1.00 in herb‐induced and conventional drug‐induced cases, respectively (Figure S7A,E). The AND rule and OR rule achieved AUROCs of 0.80–0.86 in herb‐induced cases, which increased to 0.89–0.95 in conventional drug‐induced cases. Similar increasing AUROCs were also observed in LLMs (Figure S7A,E). Furthermore, the AUROC of MELD Score increased to 0.82–0.96 in conventional drug‐induced cases from 0.73–0.85 in herb‐induced cases (Figure S8A,E). The accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of evaluators for chronicity and overall mortality were shown in Figures S5–S8B–D,F–H, and Table S6.

Discussion

Our study investigated the predictive performance of LLMs, hepatologists, and LLM‐Rules for chronicity or overall mortality in multicenter cohort. GPT‐5.1 and Gemini‐2.5 Pro exhibited AUROCs approaching senior for DILI outcomes with limited accuracy and specificity. The LLM‐Rules provided stable performance among cohorts with improved sensitivity or specificity, supporting the potential role of multi‐LLM strategies as clinician‐supervised complementary tools. The application of LLMs on predicting DILI outcomes remains to be explored in cohort. LLMs could employ deep learning techniques trained on extensive web‐based data corpora to produce natural language outputs resembling human communication, exhibiting revolutionary capabilities in diverse applications [16]. It was reported GPT could answer 76.9% of questions on cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, offering practical advice for patients [29]. A recent evaluation study reported the potential of LLMs in answering questions on DILI [21], while their applications in DILI outcomes have not been explored in cohorts. LLMs may offer a user‐friendly and accessible approach to support DILI management, particularly in primary care, underscoring their potential role as complementary clinical tools. The senior hepatologist achieved the best performance among hepatologists, suggesting that clinical experience may influence DILI prognostic assessment. Although all hepatologists had expertise in liver diseases and DILI, differences in experience may have affected their predictions. These findings support the potential role of accessible, clinician‐supervised decision‐support tools for clinicians with less DILI experience, particularly in primary care or resource‐limited settings. Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 exhibited AUROCs approaching senior hepatologist for chronic DILI with limited accuracy and specificity. For the 6‐month chronicity, we observed the best AUROC (0.61) of senior hepatologist, while Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 presented approaching AUROCs of 0.60 and 0.59. Moreover, Gemini‐2.5 Pro exhibited the best agreement (κ = 0.43) with senior than junior and middle among LLMs. Furthermore, Gemini‐2.5 Pro (AUROC = 0.60) and GPT‐5.1 (AUROC = 0.59) also achieved comparable AUROCs with senior (AUROC = 0.61) for 12‐month chronicity. GPT‐5.1 exhibited the best agreement (κ = 0.41) with senior hepatologist. However, LLMs showed limited accuracy and specificity in predicting chronic DILI, indicating higher false‐positive rates than hepatologists. Therefore, LLMs should be selected and used as complementary tools under clinicians' judgement and supervision. Overall performance of evaluators in predicting chronicity remained limited, highlighting the need for more reliable and accessible prediction tools. Several models with better AUROC were developed to predict chronic DILI, such as prolonged TBIL, clinic‐radiomics, and metabolomics [5, 14, 15], while their required data and methods were difficult to implement in primary care. Based on easily obtainable baseline data in primary care, we found Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 could provide approaching AUROCs with senior for chronicity. In the future, LLMs with advanced reasoning capabilities may further improve the risk stratification of LLMs for chronic DILI, supporting the identification ability of primary care clinicians. Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 demonstrated AUROCs for overall mortality approaching senior hepatologist. The senior hepatologist exhibited AUROC of 0.87 for overall mortality, while Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 exhibited approaching AUROCs of 0.86. Among LLMs, GPT‐5.1 demonstrated best agreement (κ = 0.25) with senior. Previous study evaluated the predictive models for overall mortality, MELD Model exhibited the best AUROC of 0.91, other models exhibited AUROCs among 0.64–0.82 [30]. However, our study revealed Gemini‐2.5 Pro and GPT‐5.1 demonstrated better AUROC (0.86) than MELD Score (0.84) to predict overall mortality. Our results indicated LLMs may support early identification of DILI patients at risk of overall fatal outcomes in primary care, facilitating timely referral and specialist evaluation, including transplant assessment when appropriate. Multiple‐LLMs could achieve stable performance among cohorts with high sensitivity and specificity in predicting DILI outcomes. We evaluated the stability of evaluators in predicting chronic or overall mortality among the overall cohort, internal cohort, and external cohort. Hepatologists and LLMs presented variation in performance among cohorts, while LLM‐Rules exhibited stable performance among cohorts. Moreover, based on the design strategy, the OR rule improved sensitivity and the AND rule improved specificity of LLMs, which was consistent with a previous study [19]. These findings supported the potential role of multi‐LLM strategies as clinician‐supervised complementary tools. Multiple‐LLMs could increase the performance of a single LLM among patients with various trigger drugs. Traditional herb was the leading cause of DILI in our cohort but was infrequent in western countries [24]. Given the heterogeneity of trigger drugs, we analysed the performance of evaluators separately in cases caused by traditional herbs or conventional drugs. We observed LLM‐Rules appeared to improve the AUROCs of individual LLMs for outcomes in both herb‐ and conventional drug‐induced liver injury. Furthermore, LLMs and LLM‐Rules exhibited better AUROCs for DILI outcomes in conventional drug‐induced cases than that in herb‐induced cases, indicating their potential to assist in early identification of high‐risk patients caused by conventional drugs. However, AUROCs for chronicity in conventional drug‐induced cases were lower in the external cohort than in the overall and internal cohorts, likely reflecting the limited sample size. Further adequately powered cohorts are needed to validate the performance of LLMs and LLM‐Rules in conventional drug‐induced liver injury. LLMs and LLM‐rules may support DILI management through rapid response generation and efficient information processing, particularly in settings with limited physician resources or high patient volumes. In primary care, especially rural areas, they may help identify DILI patients at high risk of chronicity or overall mortality based on easily available data, facilitating timely referral and specialist evaluation. However, LLMs cannot replace physicians and should remain clinician‐supervised complementary tools. Given their heterogeneous performance across AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and stability, physicians should select appropriate models or rules according to specific clinical scenarios and clinical judgement. Hallucination remains a key concern limiting the clinical application of LLMs, referring to generation unsupported or fabricated information beyond their training data [31]. It was reported that increasing reasoning capabilities might reduce hallucinated response rates [31], while testing data showed increasing rates on advanced reasoning capabilities. Prompt engineering might help mitigate hallucinations in LLM applications for liver diseases [18]. In our study, explicit prompts and standardised response templates were used to minimise hallucinated responses. Moreover, hallucination could cause randomness and instability in LLM responses. Therefore, each query was repeated three times to improve reliability and reduce random fluctuations [19], where LLM‐rules further aggregated repeated outputs from three LLMs to enhance response stability in our study. Given the unavoidable risk of hallucinations, clinical decision‐making should remain clinician‐led. LLMs and LLM‐rules should serve as complementary tools to support decision‐making through rapid response generation and efficient information processing. Repeated querying may be considered when outputs are unstable or clinically important. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study included only Chinese DILI patients, and validation in diverse racial patients is required to ensure generalisability. Second, predictive performance was evaluated using baseline clinical data only, without incorporating dynamic or imaging data, which may further enhance model performance. Three, only three representative LLMs were assessed, and future studies should evaluate a broader range of models and updated versions. Four, the external cohort might be small to result in instability in predictive performance. Therefore, the external cohort should be interpreted as an exploratory external assessment, and further validation in cohorts with sufficient patients is warranted. Finally, overall fatal outcome was defined as all‐cause death or liver transplantation to capture the overall mortality risk among DILI patients. Although this endpoint is broader and less disease‐specific than a strictly liver‐related fatal outcome, the contribution of DILI to fatal outcomes was adjudicated, and all fatal events in this cohort were classified as DILI‐related. Future studies with larger numbers of liver‐related fatal events are needed to specifically validate the prediction of DILI‐related death and transplantation.

Conclusion

GPT‐5.1, Gemini‐2.5 Pro showed AUROCs approaching senior hepatologists for DILI outcomes with limited accuracy and specificity. LLM‐Rules demonstrated stability across cohorts with improved sensitivity or specificity, supporting the potential role of multi‐LLM strategies as clinician‐supervised complementary tools.
