A deep learning framework for recognizing skin changes secondary to chronic venous insufficiency in clinical photographs: a multicentre validation study

Abstract

Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) produces heterogeneous lower-extremity skin changes that often mimic inflammatory dermatoses, complicating the differentiation between venous etiologies and conditions requiring dermatologic care. Coexisting venous signs further confound visual interpretation, leading to diagnostic variability. To address this unmet clinical need, we developed and externally validated a pose-guided, high-resolution deep learning pipeline to classify CVI-related skin lesions from routine clinical photographs. A deep learning framework was developed using a multicentre dataset (8672 images) from Korea University Guro Hospital, Chung-Ang University Gwangmyeong Hospital and Heartwell Vein Clinic. The pipeline integrated Sapiens-based pose estimation for limb localization and a U-Net model refined with Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) for skin segmentation. Multiple backbone architectures were evaluated, and ResNet-34 was selected based on optimal performance, taking refined masks and RGB images as input. Performance was evaluated on an internal test set, an independent clinical dataset (n = 183), and the Fitzpatrick17k dataset. ResNet-34 emerged as the optimal architecture, achieving an internal AUROC of 0.922 with balanced sensitivity (0.679) and specificity (0.943) at 1024 × 1024 resolution. Pose-guided skin segmentation improved discrimination compared to using raw images (AUROC 0.920) or skin masking alone (AUROC 0.905). Multicentre training enhanced generalizability, yielding an external AUROC of 0.877 and maintaining high performance on the Fitzpatrick17k dataset (sensitivity 0.918). Our pose-guided, high-resolution pipeline enables robust, objective detection of CVI-related skin lesions. By demonstrating high generalizability across multicentre and public datasets, this framework serves as a reliable decision-support tool to standardize clinical phenotyping and facilitate consistent triage and management of chronic venous disease across diverse care settings.

Introduction

Chronic venous disease (CVD) is a prevalent vascular disorder characterized by impaired venous return, elevated venous pressure, and progressive structural and functional alterations in the lower-extremity veins [1–3]. Dermatologic manifestations are common in this condition, ranging from hyperpigmentation and eczema to venous ulceration. Furthermore, CVD is frequently accompanied by lower extremity discomfort such as cramping, heaviness, oedema, pruritus and aching in the affected limb [4,5]. Accurate phenotyping of this spectrum of clinical manifestations is essential not only for diagnosis and severity assessment but also for predicting disease progression and guiding therapeutic strategies [6,7]. CVD is assessed according to the clinical, etiological, anatomical and pathophysiological (CEAP) classification [8]. The CEAP classification provides a standardized framework for describing these diverse presentations, with clinical stages defined as follows: C0 (no visible or palpable signs of venous disease), C1 (telangiectasias or reticular veins), C2 (varicose veins), C3 (oedema), C4 (skin and subcutaneous changes including pigmentation, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis (LDS) or corona phlebectatica), C5 (healed venous ulcer) and C6 (active venous ulcer) (Table 1). The term chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) is generally reserved for advanced stages (C3–C6), which involve significant tissue damage and oedema [9]. Clinically, distinguishing CVI-related lesions from other dermatoses is crucial because it directly influences management pathways and referral decisions. For example, if a patient with suspected CVD presents with a skin ulcer, the primary diagnostic objective is to determine whether the ulcer is caused by venous insufficiency. If so, venous interventions or surgery should be considered in addition to conservative treatment [10]. Conversely, if the skin lesion is not due to venous insufficiency but rather to another dermatological condition, the patient should consult a dermatologist and pursue a different treatment plan. In parallel with advances in computer vision and artificial intelligence, image-based analytical approaches for CVD have gradually expanded in dermatology. Early work demonstrated the feasibility of automated CVI assessment using non-standard modalities, such as infrared thermal imaging [11], but clinical adoption has been limited by the need for specialized equipment and by the lack of comparability with clinical photographs. More recent studies have therefore shifted towards deep learning on standard clinical images [12], including multi-task models for CEAP staging and related tasks; however, these approaches have not fully addressed the unmet clinical need to distinguish CVI-related cutaneous manifestations from other lower-extremity dermatoses in real-world practice. Despite progress in dermatologic imaging, research remains limited on AI-based classification of skin lesions secondary to CVD, particularly in higher CEAP classes, where cutaneous changes define the clinical presentation [11–17]. This gap may reflect the fact that CVI is primarily managed by vascular specialists rather than dermatologists in most cases, resulting in less familiarity with dermatologic phenotyping frameworks. In routine care, diagnosis and staging still rely predominantly on visual assessment, integrating lesion colour, morphology and distribution into the clinician’s judgment [18]. Importantly, visible venous signs such as varicosities or telangiectasias may coexist with unrelated dermatoses and can confound visual interpretation, leading to misattribution of lower-extremity skin lesions to CVI in real-world settings [19]. This challenge is illustrated in Figure 1, which shows a histopathologically confirmed psoriasis patient presenting with conspicuous lower-limb lesions despite visible popliteal varicosities. This visually driven decision process is inherently compatible with deep learning, which could support reproducible pattern recognition and reduce diagnostic variability across care levels. Using a comprehensive clinical photo dataset encompassing a wide range of lower-extremity dermatoses, this study aims to develop and validate a deep learning model for binary classification that distinguishes CVI-related cutaneous manifestations from non-CVI skin conditions. The proposed algorithm is intended to provide an objective, reproducible decision-support tool for clinicians across diverse clinical backgrounds, thereby enhancing diagnostic consistency, standardizing clinical phenotyping and facilitating timely referral in the real-world management of CVD. Patients/materials and methods › Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and involved a retrospective review of data collected for clinical purposes. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Korea University Guro Hospital (KUGH) (IRB No. 2025GR0073). The Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for informed consent, as this research was conducted retrospectively using anonymized images of the lower limbs.

Patients/materials and methods › Dataset overview

Our data were collected from the imaging database of the Department of Dermatology at KUGH between January 2017 and December 2024, comprising 8192 images from 927 patients. All clinical photographs at KUGH were acquired by dermatology residents and attending dermatologists using a digital camera (Canon EOS 90D; Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). To increase robustness to device- and environment-related variability, additional CVI-related images were obtained from two independent centres: Chung-Ang University Gwangmyeong Hospital (CAUGH; iPhone XR; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA) and Heartwell Vein Clinic (HVC; Canon 5D; Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), where photographs were acquired by trained clinical staff (nurses and physician assistants). An independent external validation dataset was collected at Korea University Anam Hospital (KUAH) during the most recent 3 years (January 2022 to December 2024) using a digital camera (Canon EOS 80D; Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Standardized photography protocols were employed, including frontal, lateral, posterior and close-up views to capture lesion morphology and distribution; as a result, multiple images (typically one to five images) were available per patient. For binary classification, diagnostic categories were grouped into CVI-related conditions (LDS, stasis dermatitis and pigmented purpuric dermatosis (PPD)) and non-CVI-related conditions (atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis, nummular eczema, psoriasis and vasculitis). Patient- and image-level distributions by diagnostic category and participating centre are summarized in Table 2. To minimize data leakage and reduce sampling bias, data were split at the patient level such that all images from a given patient were assigned to a single subset. KUGH data (including both CVI-related and non-CVI-related categories) were partitioned into training, validation and test sets at a 7:1:2 ratio. Because the CAUGH and HVC datasets contained only CVI-related samples, they were used exclusively for training. Model training, hyperparameter selection and performance evaluation were conducted strictly according to this predefined split. Specifically, KUGH data were split into training/validation/test sets (5974/783/1428 images), with an additional 480 CVI- related images from CAUGH (265 images) and HVC (215 images) included in the training set; external validation was performed on 183 images from KUAH. Patients/materials and methods › Clinical grouping, label definition and diagnostic ground truth

CVI-related categories were defined as dermatoses clinically attributable to venous hypertension in the lower extremities. Stasis dermatitis is characterized as an eczematous eruption commonly observed in the C4 stage of CVD and histopathologically characterized by dilated capillaries, extravasated red blood cells and hemosiderin deposition [20]. Lipodermatosclerosis is presented as an indurated, sclerotic inflammatory change of the lower leg associated with CVI. Pigmented purpuric dermatosis represents a group of chronic dermatoses marked by the presence of petechiae, pigmented purpuric macules and patches (Figure 2) [21]. Although some cases are associated with medication or underlying diseases such as diabetes or autoimmune disorders, ‘gravitational purpura’ is identified as a clinical subtype directly related to CVI. Notably, a Korean study reported venous reflux in 62.5% of PPD patients on Doppler ultrasonography [22]. Consistently, a Turkish study revealed variable degrees of venous insufficiency in 75.0% (15/20) of lower-extremity PPD patients, although varicose dilatation was clinically visible in only five patients [23]. Based on this collective evidence, PPD was included in the CVI-related group exclusively when lesions were localized to the lower extremities and clinically assessed as stasis-associated capillaritis. Conversely, cases with non-lower-extremity distribution or identifiable alternative triggers or underlying diseases such as drug-related purpura were strictly excluded. Non-CVI-related categories (psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis, nummular eczema and vasculitis) were selected as common differential diagnoses in lower-extremity eruptions (Figure 3). In particular, psoriasis and vasculitis cases were strictly derived from a biopsy-proven dataset to ensure diagnostic certainty, and atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis and nummular eczema were clinically diagnosed. Diagnostic ground truth was established by two board-certified dermatologists (K.E.K. and D.H.K.) through a two-stage process: the first reviewer (K.E.K.) screened the combined KUGH and KUAH images and the second (D.H.K.) independently confirmed eligibility, with only concordant cases retained. Inter-reviewer concordance was expressed as percent agreement, calculated for each diagnostic category, with 95% confidence intervals estimated using the Wilson score interval, which is appropriate for proportions based on small samples or values near the boundaries. The CVI-related CAUGH and HVC cohorts comprised patients whose venous reflux had been confirmed by duplex ultrasonography at specialized vein clinics prior to clinical photography. Because these diagnoses rested on objective reference standards rather than photographic judgment, these cases were not subject to inter-reviewer adjudication and were excluded from the concordance analysis. Inter-reviewer concordance among the photographically adjudicated categories was 90.0% (433/481). Agreement was 84.4% for CVI-related categories and 92.8% for non-CVI categories, with the lowest concordance observed for PPD (82.8%), consistent with its recognized diagnostic ambiguity. Patients/materials and methods › Image preprocessing and skin region extraction

To ensure consistent focus on the lower extremities, human pose estimation was first applied to each clinical photograph using Sapiens [24] to localize and segment the lower limbs. Specifically, the pose estimation results were used to identify anatomical landmarks such as the pelvis and hands. To minimize potential noise from non-relevant body parts, if the pelvis or hands were detected within the frame, the image was automatically cropped above the highest detected point of these landmarks, effectively isolating the lower limbs. Following limb localization, a skin region segmentation process was performed to isolate cutaneous areas relevant to CVI assessment. For this purpose, a U-Net model [25] was pretrained on an external, publicly available skin segmentation dataset [26], and then applied to the lower-extremity clinical images in the present study to identify skin regions. The resulting segmentation outputs were converted into binary masks and refined with the segmentation foundation model Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) [27]. Qualitative improvement in skin-region delineation resulting from this refinement process is demonstrated in Figure 4. The refined masks were subsequently used, along with the original RGB images, as inputs to the backbone classification network. This dual-input strategy enabled the model to focus on disease-relevant cutaneous features while reducing background noise. Patients/materials and methods › Model architecture and training strategy

To improve model robustness and generalization performance, data augmentation was applied during training. Augmentation techniques included random cropping, horizontal flipping, colour jitter and random colour temperature adjustment, simulating variations in camera distance, orientation, illumination and acquisition conditions commonly encountered in real-world clinical photography. For binary classification, the backbone model was trained to distinguish CVI-related cutaneous manifestations from non-CVI dermatoses. The classification task was formulated as a binary problem, and binary cross entropy was used as the loss function. To address the class imbalance (approximately 1:5, CVI:non-CVI), we applied weighted random oversampling of the minority (CVI) class during training. Model optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer [28]. Training was conducted on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, with the batch size optimized to leverage the available GPU memory capacity. The learning rate followed a cosine annealing schedule with a 5-epoch warmup period, where the learning rate increased linearly during the warmup and subsequently decayed following a half-cosine curve towards the final epoch to ensure stable convergence. All models were trained for up to 100 epochs, with early stopping applied to prevent overfitting. Training was terminated if the validation loss did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs (patience = 10). To ensure optimal generalization performance, the model weights corresponding to the minimum validation loss were saved and used for final evaluation on the independent test set. For patient-wise diagnosis, multiple clinical images acquired from the same patient were jointly considered. The model generated a classification probability for each individual image, and these image-level probabilities were averaged to obtain a single patient-level probability score. Final binary classification was performed by applying a fixed decision threshold of 0.5 to the aggregated patient-level probability.

Patients/materials and methods › Experimental design and model selection

To determine the optimal configuration for CVI classification, we systematically evaluated several technical components. First, we compared multiple representative deep learning models, including ResNet-34 [29], ResNet-101 [29], ConvNeXt [30], Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) [31] and Swin Transformer (Swin-T) [32], all trained under identical conditions to ensure a fair comparison. The influence of image resolution was further investigated by resizing images to 256 × 256, 512 × 512 and 1024 × 1024 pixels using the selected ResNet-34 backbone. Regarding preprocessing, performance was evaluated under three configurations: training on resized raw images, using full-frame images with skin segmentation masks, and employing a pose-guided skin segmentation pipeline that localizes the lower extremities before applying masks. Finally, to assess generalizability, we compared models trained on a single-centre dataset (KUGH) against those trained on a multicentre dataset (KUGH, CAUGH and HVC). Evaluations were conducted on an internal test set, an external clinical dataset (KUAH) and the publicly available Fitzpatrick17k dataset [33]. For the Fitzpatrick17k evaluation, 38 of 69 images labelled as stasis oedema were included, while images of non-lower-extremity sites or those with extensive ulceration were excluded to maintain consistency with our study criteria. Since the refined Fitzpatrick17k dataset consists exclusively of positive cases for stasis oedema, performance was evaluated solely based on sensitivity. We additionally compared four oversampling strategies for handling class imbalance – Unweighted, Dataset-balanced, Class-balanced and Internal-only – and selected Dataset-balanced, which achieved the best balance between AUROC and CVI sensitivity on the validation set (Supplementary Table 3). All reported metrics represent the average performance over five independent runs.

Results › Backbone architecture comparison

ResNet-based architectures generally outperformed transformer-based models across most evaluation metrics (Table 4). ResNet-34 achieved the highest AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) of 0.922 among all evaluated backbones, while also demonstrating balanced sensitivity and specificity. Although ResNet-101 and Swin-T exhibited high accuracy, this was accompanied by substantially reduced sensitivity. In contrast, ResNet-34 maintained a favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity while maximizing AUROC. Results › Effect of input image resolution

The input image resolution affected performance across all metrics (Table 5). The 256 × 256 setting achieved the highest AUROC (0.923) and sensitivity (0.736), whereas the 512 × 512 setting yielded the highest specificity (0.956), precision (0.746) and accuracy (0.911). The 1024 × 1024 setting achieved an AUROC of 0.922 while maintaining a balanced trade-off between sensitivity (0.679) and specificity (0.943). Discussion

CVD is associated with a broad spectrum of lower-limb cutaneous manifestations, which can complicate clinical diagnosis [1]. In patients presenting with lower-extremity discomfort – a common symptom of CVD – clinicians must determine whether concomitant skin lesions are attributable to venous insufficiency. In practice, accurate attribution can be challenging because these findings are frequently evaluated in vascular medicine settings rather than dermatology, and access to in-house dermatologic consultation is limited in many stand-alone vein clinics. Accordingly, this study aimed to develop a photograph-based decision-support tool to assist clinicians in identifying CVI-related cutaneous manifestations in real-world settings. Our findings demonstrate that an optimized deep learning pipeline can achieve high discriminative performance for CVI-related skin lesions, with ResNet-34 attaining a peak AUROC of 0.922 in internal testing. Given that our dataset exhibited a significant class imbalance (approximately a 1:5 ratio), AUROC was prioritized as the primary evaluation metric [36,37]. Among the evaluated configurations, incorporating pose-guided lower-extremity localization and skin segmentation improved discrimination, supporting the utility of anatomical priors to constrain analysis to relevant regions and reduce background variability. Input resolution also affected performance across metrics; although 256 × 256 achieved the highest AUROC (0.923) and sensitivity (0.736), 1024 × 1024 provided a balanced trade-off between sensitivity (0.679) and specificity (0.943) with a nearly identical AUROC (0.922), and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments to better preserve fine-grained cutaneous details, capturing subtle texture and colour changes characteristic of CVI manifestations. Furthermore, the superior performance of ResNet-34 over transformer-based architectures (ViT-B/16 and Swin-T) likely stems from the convolutional inductive biases – specifically locality and translation equivariance. These features are better suited for detecting localized visual cues in limited-size clinical datasets, whereas transformers typically require much larger-scale data to effectively learn global contextual representations. Error-pattern analysis further clarified clinically relevant failure modes. In internal validation, the model demonstrated high specificity by minimizing false-positive predictions for common non-CVI inflammatory dermatoses, such as psoriasis and atopic dermatitis – a discriminative capability largely preserved in external validation. However, diagnostic challenges arose with PPD, as the model showed overlap between CVI-related and non-CVI predictions. This finding is clinically plausible given the heterogeneous nature of PPD. Gravitational purpura, or stasis-associated capillaritis, is frequently associated with venous hypertension, whereas other PPD subtypes may share similar pigmentary and purpuric features despite non-venous etiologies [21–23]. In routine practice, distinguishing lower-leg PPD from CVI based solely on clinical photographs creates a diagnostic grey zone even for experienced clinicians, which likely accounts for the observed model ambiguity [38,39]. To address potential diagnostic ambiguity and further assess model robustness, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by retraining the model with all PPD cases completely removed from the CVI-related class (Supplementary Table 2; mean of five patient-level runs). Excluding PPD led to a marginal increase in AUROC from 0.922 to 0.966. Because AUROC evaluates overall discriminative performance across all possible thresholds, this highly stable, non-inferior performance demonstrates that the model’s core discrimination is remarkably robust and does not depend heavily on excluding borderline cases. The minor shifts in sensitivity (0.679–0.614) and specificity (0.943–0.977) reflect a slightly more conservative operational threshold after the positive class size was reduced, while accuracy (0.901–0.945) remained largely consistent, reflecting the underlying change in prevalence. This subtle trend aligns with the lower inter-reviewer concordance for PPD (Supplementary Table 1), confirming its clinical placement at the CVI boundary. Ultimately, maintaining PPD in our primary analysis ensures a more realistic, clinically unselected spectrum, confirming that our baseline AUROC of 0.922 represents a highly robust and pragmatic performance estimate for real-world deployment. The model also demonstrated improved robustness with multicentre training and maintained performance on independent external datasets. Multicentre training improved AUROC on the external KUAH dataset (AUROC 0.877), indicating that discriminative performance was preserved across different acquisition environments. In an additional evaluation on the Fitzpatrick17k dataset, performance was stable across skin types I–V, whereas sensitivity was lower for type VI. This result should be interpreted in light of the limited sample size and differences in skin tone distribution relative to our primarily Korean training cohort. These findings underscore both the feasibility of generalization and the need for more diverse multi-ethnic training data to ensure equitable performance. While our work extends deep learning to diverse validation settings, artificial intelligence in dermatology has historically been applied to image analysis for malignant tumour detection or the classification of common inflammatory dermatoses. Aggarwal utilized the Inception-v3 architecture to categorize five skin conditions, including acne, atopic dermatitis and rosacea, using a mix of public and clinical datasets, yielding average sensitivities of 0.653, specificities of 0.913, and F1-scores of 0.655 across five disease categories [40]. Zhao et al. focused on dermoscopic analysis, using ResNet-50 to distinguish rosacea from conditions such as acne and seborrheic dermatitis, reporting an accuracy of 0.914 and a precision of 0.898 [41]. Liu et al. employed an Inception-v4-based model to classify 26 common adult skin conditions in a teledermatology setting, reporting sensitivities of 0.48 and 0.57, and positive predictive values of 0.96 and 0.95, across two validation cohorts [42]. Furthermore, Yang et al. used deep learning to diagnose benign pigmented facial lesions, achieving an AUC of 0.98 with DenseNet-96, demonstrating diagnostic performance comparable to that of skin specialists [43]. In this context, our model achieved robust discrimination of CVI-related cutaneous manifestations from non-CVI-related dermatoses using routine clinical photographs (internal AUROC, 0.922), supporting the feasibility of extending dermatologic AI beyond inflammatory or neoplastic conditions to venous-origin skin pathology. Despite these advances, prior studies have not specifically addressed skin conditions associated with CVI in a photograph-based, real-world differential diagnosis setting. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to apply a deep learning-based classification framework to CVI-related skin lesions using routine clinical photographs with multicentre training and external validation. Beyond cutaneous disease, artificial intelligence has been increasingly investigated in venous diagnostics more broadly. A narrative review of AI in lower-limb vascular management documented growing arterial and venous applications while noting that evidence specific to CVD remains comparatively limited [44]. A systematic review of AI in phlebological diagnostics reported diagnostic accuracy exceeding 90%, with reduced inter-observer variability and image-analysis times shortened by more than half, predominantly through automated interpretation of duplex ultrasonography [45]; other work has extended AI to non-ultrasound modalities such as passive microwave radiometry [46]. These efforts, however, have largely focused on hemodynamic and structural assessments of the deep venous system – venous reflux and thrombus detection – rather than on the cutaneous sequelae of venous hypertension. Compared with inflammatory or neoplastic diseases, venous-origin skin pathology has received relatively limited algorithmic attention, and only a small number of studies have explored image-based AI for CVD/CVI. Krishnan et al. proposed CVINet, a deep learning-based diagnostic model trained on infrared thermal images of the lower extremities, demonstrating that automated CVI assessment is feasible by learning temperature distribution patterns; however, reliance on thermal imaging hardware limits direct integration into routine clinical photography workflows [11]. Oliveira et al. introduced VENet, a multi-task deep learning architecture trained on standard clinical images to jointly perform CEAP-based severity classification and lesion segmentation, supporting the utility of deep learning for CVD phenotyping across stages [12]. Furthermore, Shi et al. proposed an automatic CVI severity classification framework based on multi-scale semantic modelling to stratify severity, providing a proof of concept for automated assessment and focusing on severity estimation [47]. Collectively, these studies support feasibility, yet they leave an important gap regarding a photograph-based, real-world classification setting that directly addresses differential diagnosis against common non-venous dermatoses. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our dataset was predominantly composed of Asian patients, which may limit generalizability to broader ethnic groups and skin tone distributions. Although external evaluation on the Fitzpatrick17k dataset suggested stable performance across skin types I–V, statistical evaluation for type VI was precluded due to an insufficient sample size (n = 1). Future work should include more diverse, multi-ethnic datasets to better assess and improve robustness across skin tones. Second, the distributions of patients and images across diagnostic categories and centres were not uniform. This imbalance reflects real-world prevalence and clinical workflows, as patients with suspected venous insufficiency are often referred for vascular evaluation and intervention, after which dermatologic follow-up and repeat photography may be less frequent. Consequently, CVI-related categories may be underrepresented relative to common inflammatory dermatoses, potentially affecting model calibration and error patterns. Third, our current framework addressed binary discrimination of CVI-related versus non-CVI-related dermatoses. Extending the model to finer-grained staging (e.g. CEAP subclassification) and incorporating longitudinal images may provide more clinically actionable guidance for monitoring disease course and treatment response. Additionally, expanding the dataset to include earlier CVD stages, such as C1 (telangiectasias) and C2 (varicose veins), may further enable broader coverage across the disease spectrum. Additionally, establishing a definitive venous etiology solely from retrospective clinical photographs introduces inherent diagnostic challenges. Although the CVI-related cohorts from CAUGH and HVC were pre-confirmed with gold-standard duplex ultrasonography, and specific non-CVI cases were derived from a biopsy-proven dataset, the remaining cohorts relied on a photographic reference standard. To mitigate this, we implemented a rigorous two-stage verification workflow by board-certified dermatologists, excluding non-concordant cases. Nevertheless, future prospective studies incorporating universal patient-level duplex ultrasound evaluation and comprehensive CEAP staging across all participating centres are warranted to further validate clinical robustness. Future work will focus on translating this algorithm into a widely accessible, user-friendly clinical decision-support tool for routine care settings.

Conclusions

The proposed ResNet-34 model achieved high discriminative performance (internal AUROC 0.922) and showed consistent generalizability across independent external validation datasets. From a technical perspective, integrating pose-guided localization with high-resolution input enabled the model to effectively handle the inherent variability of routine clinical photographs, capturing subtle, localized vascular skin changes that may be difficult to detect with standard image-level approaches. The results support the feasibility of a practical, photograph-based decision-support tool that may aid consistent triage and referral decisions across diverse clinical settings with limited dermatologic expertise. Furthermore, by facilitating the early identification of subtle vascular-induced skin changes, this framework may help prevent progression to severe complications, such as intractable venous ulceration, thereby potentially reducing the long-term socioeconomic burden of CVD. Future efforts will focus on prospective clinical validation to evaluate the model’s real-world utility and its synergistic impact on clinician decision-making in outpatient workflows.
