Development and validation of a machine learning stratified prediction model for early warning of anti-tuberculosis drug-induced liver injury risk based on real-world data: a retrospective cohort study

Abstract

This study aimed to develop and validate stratified machine learning models for early prediction of anti-tuberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATB-DILI) risk, targeting both a general tuberculosis (TB) treatment population and a high-risk subgroup with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) co-infection, based on real-world clinical data. A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from 11,361 TB patients (3,787 ATB-DILI cases and 7,574 controls) and a CHB subgroup of 1,017 patients (339 cases and 678 controls) after propensity score matching. Ten machine learning algorithms, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest (RF), and XGBoost, were applied. Models were trained and validated using a 1:1 split and 10-fold cross-validation. Performance was evaluated using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. Model interpretability was enhanced using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). In the overall population, ensemble methods such as RF and XGBoost achieved AUCs of 0.960 and 0.954, respectively, on the validation set. In the CHB subgroup, RF and XGBoost performed even better, with AUCs of 0.994. Key predictors in the general population included ALT, eosinophil count, AST, and procalcitonin, while in the CHB subgroup, total bile acid, procalcitonin, ALP, and prealbumin were most influential. SHAP analysis revealed non-linear relationships between features and ATB-DILI risk, aligning with clinical knowledge. Stratified machine learning models, particularly ensemble methods, demonstrated excellent performance in predicting ATB-DILI risk and highlighted distinct injury mechanisms between general and CHB co-infected TB patients. This approach offers a clinically interpretable and accurate tool for early warning of ATB-DILI, supporting personalized risk assessment and management.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is recognized as a major global public health issue, remaining the leading cause of death from a single infectious disease agent worldwide, with an estimated 10 million new cases and 1.4–1.7 million deaths annually in recent years [1–3]. Antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury (ATB-DILI) is a significant and potentially severe adverse effect of TB therapy, particularly with first-line agents such as isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide. The incidence of ATB-DILI varies widely, with pooled estimates ranging from 2% to 28% depending on population, regimen, and diagnostic criteria [4–6]. ATB-DILI can range from asymptomatic transaminase elevations to acute liver failure, with risk factors including genetic polymorphisms, metabolic disorders, malnutrition, alcohol use, and co-infections such as chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [7–10]. The pathogenesis involves complex interactions between drug metabolism, immune responses, oxidative stress, and, more recently, epigenetic and gut microbiome alterations [11–14]. Advances in genomics, epigenetics, and microbiome research are beginning to clarify mechanisms and may enable personalized risk stratification and targeted interventions [13–15]. However, the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria and monitoring protocols remains a barrier to early detection and optimal management. Early diagnosis of ATB-DILI has traditionally relied on monitoring conventional liver function tests, particularly alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). However, these biomarkers are indirect and non-specific measures of hepatocyte injury and are often significantly elevated only after substantial liver damage has already occurred. This inherent delay in signal detection is particularly problematic in high-risk populations, such as patients with TB-CHB co-infection, who are at an increased risk for rapid and severe hepatotoxicity. In such patients, the underlying chronic liver inflammation and compromised hepatic reserve may accelerate the progression from initial injury to clinically significant or even fulminant hepatitis. Consequently, monitoring strategies based solely on periodic ALT/AST measurements may fail to provide timely warning, potentially leading to delayed intervention, worsened outcomes, and higher mortality. In recent years, machine learning technology has been increasingly applied in medical prediction models. It can integrate multi-dimensional clinical data and capture complex nonlinear relationships, providing new tools for disease risk prediction. Some studies have attempted to apply machine learning to predict the risk of ATB-DILI and have demonstrated promising potential, with some models achieving over 85% accuracy in external validation sets [16–18]. It is noteworthy that underlying liver diseases such as CHB are well-established high-risk factors for ATB-DILI. Patients with TB-CHB co-infection face a 2–3 times higher risk of developing ATB-DILI compared to those with TB alone, with an increased likelihood of severe liver injury and hepatic failure [19]. The distinct pathophysiology involves multi-layered synergistic effects: first, reactive metabolites generated during the hepatic metabolism of anti-tuberculosis drugs induce oxidative stress and cellular damage, while active hepatitis B virus replication exacerbates hepatocyte stress and amplifies immune responses, thereby intensifying drug-induced injury [20, 21]. Second, the underlying chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and reduced detoxification reserve associated with CHB significantly lower the liver’s tolerance threshold to subsequent drug toxicity [22, 23]. Furthermore, drug-triggered molecular pathways, such as ferroptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, are potentiated within the pro-inflammatory milieu [12]. These collective mechanisms confer a unique injury profile and risk spectrum in this population. However, most current prediction models target heterogeneous general populations, potentially overlooking the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and risk profiles of high-risk subgroups. Therefore, developing “stratified” prediction models tailored to populations with different risk characteristics may prove more accurate and clinically practical than a single “universal” model. Based on this rationale, this study aims to leverage large-scale real-world clinical data to systematically develop, validate, and compare multiple machine learning prediction models for both the general population receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment and the high-risk subgroup with comorbid CHB. The ultimate objectives are to develop high-performance, interpretable early warning tools for ATB-DILI risk and to gain deeper insights into the differences in risk profiles across these distinct populations.

Materials and methods › Study design and participants

The initial study population consisted of 37,361 pulmonary tuberculosis patients who received anti-tuberculosis drug therapy during this period. Diagnosis of ATB-DILI was based on the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method criteria [24], with a total score of ≥ 6 points required for confirmation, combined with laboratory abnormalities. Finally, 3,965 patients were identified for the case group and 33,396 for the control group. From this population, a subgroup of 3,352 patients co-infected with CHB was further selected (342 in the ATB-DILI group, 3,010 in the non-ATB-DILI group). Key inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of active pulmonary tuberculosis; (3) initiation of standardized first-line anti-tuberculosis therapy; and (4) availability of complete baseline clinical and laboratory data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) pre-existing severe liver diseases other than CHB (e.g., cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis); (2) concurrent use of other hepatotoxic drugs; (3) pregnancy or lactation; (4) human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection; and (5) incomplete or missing key data.

Materials and methods › Variable definition and data processing

Collected variables included demographic characteristics, complete blood count, blood biochemistry, inflammatory markers, and coagulation function. Variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded. For the remaining variables, missing values were handled using multiple imputation. To control for confounding bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed separately for the overall population and the CHB subgroup at a 1:2 ratio. Matching variables included age, sex. After PSM, the overall population ultimately included 11,361 patients (3,787 cases, 7,574 controls), and the CHB subgroup included 1,017 patients (339 cases, 678 controls).

Materials and methods › Machine learning model construction and validation

This study employed ten ML algorithms to build prediction models, all implemented in the R statistical computing environment (version 4.2.0). The specific algorithms and their corresponding R packages were: Logistic Regression (LR) and Lasso Regression (Lasso) using the glmnet package (v4.1-8); Decision Tree (DT) with the rpart package (v4.1.23); Random Forest (RF) with the randomForest package (v4.7-1.1); eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) with the xgboost package (v1.8.5.1); Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) with the gbm package (v2.1.9); Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) with the ada package (v2.0-5); Support Vector Machine (SVM) with the e1071 package (v1.7-16) or the kernlab package (v0.9-34); K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) with the class package (v7.3-23); and Naive Bayes (NB) with the e1071 package (v1.7-16). All models were constructed using the default hyperparameter settings provided by these packages to ensure a fair and reproducible baseline comparison. This standardized environment ensured the reproducibility and consistency of our modeling process. The dataset after PSM was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a 1:1 ratio. To ensure reproducibility, a fixed random seed (set.seed(2025)) was set prior to the data split. All models were trained using the original, non-standardized data. During the training phase, 10-fold cross-validation was used for model training and performance evaluation. Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using metrics including the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, and F1-Score. To statistically compare the discriminatory ability of different models, we performed DeLong’s test for pairwise comparison of area under the AUC between all models within each population and dataset split (training and validation). The tests were implemented using the pROC package (version 1.18.5) in R.

Materials and methods › Model interpretability analysis

To enhance the clinical interpretability of the models, the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) framework was applied to interpret the best-performing model. By calculating the SHAP value for each feature, its importance was quantified. SHAP dependence plots were used to visualize the nonlinear relationships between key features and the prediction outcomes.

Materials and methods › Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using R. Normally distributed measurement data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the t-test. Count data are presented as frequency (percentage), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results › Baseline characteristics of the study population

Using PSM, this study ultimately enrolled 11,361 patients receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment, comprising 3,787 patients in the ATB-DILI group and 7,574 patients in the non-ATB-DILI group. The two groups demonstrated good balance in baseline characteristics such as sex and age (all P > 0.05). Specifically, the proportion of males was 62.82% (2,379/3,787) in the ATB-DILI group and 63.49% (4,809/7,574) in the non-ATB-DILI group, with no significant difference (P = 0.4965). The mean age was 48.96 ± 0.31 years in the ATB-DILI group and 48.75 ± 0.22 years in the non-ATB-DILI group (P = 0.5837). Regarding laboratory parameters, the ATB-DILI group exhibited significantly higher levels of several liver function, inflammation, and immune-related markers compared to the non-ATB-DILI group (all P < 0.001), including ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bile acid (TBA), procalcitonin (PCT), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR). Detailed data are presented in Table 1. Among TB patients co-infected with CHB, a total of 1,017 patients were included after PSM, consisting of 339 in the ATB-DILI group and 678 in the non-ATB-DILI group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of sex (both 74.34% male) and age (both 50.85 years) (P = 1.000 and P = 0.9938, respectively). The ATB-DILI group showed significantly higher levels of multiple liver function and inflammation markers compared to the non-ATB-DILI group, including ALT (180.97 vs. 33.42 U/L), AST (156.98 vs. 41.02 U/L), ALP (126.41 vs. 104.85 U/L), TBA (23.66 vs. 12.03 µmol/L), PCT (253.90 vs. 210.40 ng/mL), etc. (all P < 0.001). Detailed data are provided in Table 2. Results › Comparison of machine learning model predictive performance

In the training set for the overall population (Fig. 1A), ensemble methods demonstrated superior performance, with RF and XGBoost achieving perfect scores (1.000) across all evaluation metrics. AdaBoost and SVM also showed strong predictive capability. In contrast, baseline classifiers like NB and KNN underperformed, particularly in specificity (NB: 0.398). The AUC analysis (Fig. 1B) confirmed these findings, with RF and XGBoost attaining perfect scores of 1.0000, followed by AdaBoost (0.981) and SVM (0.959). DT and NB showed the lowest discriminatory ability with AUC values of 0.823 and 0.819, respectively. Pairwise statistical comparison using DeLong’s test (Fig. 1C) revealed no significant difference between RF and XGBoost (NS, p ≥ 0.05), while both models significantly outperformed all other classifiers (p < 0.05). When evaluated on the validation set (Fig. 2A), the top-performing models maintained robust performance, though with some attenuation in certain metrics. RF achieved the highest accuracy (0.904), sensitivity (0.946), and F1 score (0.924), while XGBoost attained the highest precision (0.924). NB continued to demonstrate poor performance, particularly in specificity (0.492). The AUC results (Fig. 2B) reinforced the superiority of ensemble methods, with RF (0.9604), XGBoost (0.954), and AdaBoost (0.945) showing excellent discriminatory power, while DT (0.816) and NB (0.779) remained the weakest performers. Statistical comparison of AUCs (Fig. 2C) indicated that XGBoost significantly outperformed all other models in the validation set (p < 0.05). Among patients with CHB co-infection, the training set performance (Fig. 3A) showed RF and XGBoost again achieving perfect scores across all metrics. AdaBoost exhibited perfect sensitivity (1.000) with near-perfect results in other metrics. While DT, GBM, and SVM demonstrated strong overall performance, DT showed relatively lower specificity (0.907). KNN and LR achieved identical accuracy (0.886), with LR showing marginally higher specificity. Lasso and NB displayed the weakest performance, with NB showing the lowest specificity (0.279). The AUC analysis (Fig. 3B) supported these observations. DeLong’s test for pairwise AUC comparisons (Fig. 3C) showed no significant difference among AdaBoost, XGBoost, and RF (NS, p ≥ 0.05), whereas these three ensemble methods significantly outperformed all remaining models (p < 0.05). In the CHB subgroup validation set (Fig. 4A), ensemble methods continued to demonstrate superior performance. RF and XGBoost were the top performers, both achieving the highest accuracy (0.963). XGBoost recorded the highest sensitivity (0.991), while RF attained the highest specificity (0.916) and F1 score (0.973). The AUC analysis (Fig. 4B) confirmed these findings. RF (0.994) and XGBoost (0.994) achieved near-perfect discriminatory power, followed closely by AdaBoost (0.992) and GBM (0.990). Conversely, NB (0.812), KNN (0.866), and LR (0.879) demonstrated the lowest AUC values, highlighting their limited discriminatory ability in this subgroup. Statistical comparison via DeLong’s test (Fig. 4C) further clarified that RF, XGBoost, and AdaBoost did not differ significantly from each other (NS, p ≥ 0.05), but as a group they exhibited significantly higher AUCs than all other models (p < 0.05). Results › Feature importance analysis

Based on the optimally performing XGBoost model in the validation set, we further analyzed the top 10 most important predictive features it identified (Fig. 5A). These features, listed in descending order of importance, were: ALT, Eosinophil Count (EOS), AST, PCT, Platelet Count (PLT), MLR, Fibrinogen (FIB), Direct Bilirubin (DBIL), Hemoglobin (HGB), and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN). Baseline characteristic comparisons showed that all these indicators exhibited significant differences between the ATB-DILI and non-ATB-DILI groups (all P < 0.001, see Table 1). However, when individually assessing the predictive performance of these indicators, their AUC was all below 0.7 (Fig. 5B). In stark contrast, the XGBoost model, by integrating these 10 features, achieved a significantly higher AUC of 0.965 in the validation set. This indicates that the ML model constructed by combining multiple indicators has significantly superior predictive efficacy compared to any single indicator, highlighting the core value of comprehensive assessment in ATB-DILI risk prediction. In the CHB subgroup, we performed a feature importance analysis on the best-performing RF model and identified the top 10 key predictors (Fig. 6A). These factors included: TBA, PCT, ALP, Prealbumin (PA), ALT, AST, Cholinesterase (ChE), C-Reactive Protein (CRP), CD4/CD8 ratio, and Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT). Among them, TBA, PCT, ALP, PA, ALT, AST, ChE, and CRP showed highly statistically significant differences between the ATB-DILI and non-ATB-DILI groups (all P < 0.001), whereas the differences for the CD4/CD8 ratio and GGT were not significant (P > 0.05). Their inclusion among the top predictors by the machine learning model suggests they may contribute to risk prediction through complex interactions with other features, a pattern that univariate analysis cannot capture. Consistent with the findings in the overall population, these key indicators had limited predictive ability when used alone, with none achieving an AUC exceeding 0.7 (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the RF model, by integrating these features, achieved an exceptional AUC of 0.994 in the validation set, once again demonstrating the necessity and superiority of combined multi-dimensional indicator prediction in this high-risk CHB population. Results › Model interpretability analysis based on SHAP

To gain deeper insight into the decision-making mechanism of the XGBoost model, we employed SHAP for interpretability analysis. The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 7) revealed that the features contributing most to the model’s predictions were ALT, EOS, AST, and PCT. Among these, liver function markers (ALT, AST) had the highest SHAP values, indicating that the degree of hepatocyte injury is the most core basis for the model’s prediction of ATB-DILI. Concurrently, immune and inflammation-related indicators (such as PCT, MLR, EOS) also showed significant contributions, suggesting the role of immune dysregulation in ATB-DILI occurrence. SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 8) further elucidated the non-linear relationships between key features and the prediction outcome. Taking ALT as an example, its SHAP value continuously increased with rising ALT levels within the range of 0 to 1000 U/L, indicating that higher ALT levels correspond to a higher model-predicted risk of ATB-DILI, which aligns perfectly with the clinical significance of this marker. Similarly, indicators like EOS, AST, and PCT exhibited positive correlations with SHAP values, further validating the biological plausibility of the model’s decision logic. SHAP analysis of the RF model for the CHB subgroup, via the summary plot (Fig. 9), indicated that the features with the highest contribution included PCT, TBA, ALP, ALT, and AST. This result underscores that in this high-risk population, systemic inflammation markers (PCT) and hepatobiliary function indicators (particularly TBA and ALP associated with cholestasis, and ALT and AST associated with hepatocyte injury) collectively form the cornerstone of the model’s predictions. SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 10) clearly illustrate the effects of these key features. For instance, PCT levels showed a clear positive correlation with SHAP values within the range of 0–10 ng/mL, meaning higher PCT levels were associated with a greater model-predicted risk of ATB-DILI. TBA, ALP, ALT, and AST also exhibited similar monotonically increasing relationships, where elevated levels pointed towards a higher risk of liver injury. These visual analyses not only enhance the model’s credibility but also provide clinicians with an intuitive framework for assessing risk factors, emphasizing the importance of monitoring comprehensive inflammatory and hepatobiliary function indicators in patients co-infected with CHB. Discussion

AT-DILI is a significant clinical challenge, complicating TB treatment and patient outcomes. This study successfully developed and validated stratified machine learning models for predicting ATB-DILI risk in both a general TB treatment population and a high-risk CHB co-infected subgroup. Our findings demonstrate that ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost and RF, achieved excellent predictive performance (AUCs up to 0.994), significantly outperforming single laboratory indicators and traditional models. The distinct sets of key predictors identified for the general population (e.g., ALT, EOS, AST, PCT) versus the CHB subgroup (e.g., TBA, PCT, ALP, PA) highlight different dominant injury mechanisms—primarily hepatocellular in the former and mixed hepatocellular/cholestatic in the latter. Specifically, the elevation of transaminases (ALT/AST) points to direct hepatocyte necrosis as the core event in the general population, whereas the prominence of TBA and ALP in the CHB subgroup underscores a concomitant cholestatic component, likely exacerbated by the underlying chronic liver disease [25, 26]. The application of SHAP enhanced model interpretability, revealing non-linear relationships aligned with clinical knowledge. This stratified approach not only provides accurate, data-driven tools for early ATB-DILI warning but also underscores the importance of tailoring risk prediction to specific patient subgroups. Compared with previous studies that developed single “universal” models for heterogeneous TB populations [16, 18], our study introduces a novel stratified modeling strategy. By proactively constructing separate models for the general population and the high-risk CHB subgroup—a population with distinct pathophysiology and a 2–3 times higher ATB-DILI risk [19]—we achieved superior predictive performance. Notably, our model for the CHB subgroup attained a near-perfect AUC of 0.994, exceeding the performance benchmarks set by prior universal models. More importantly, the differing sets of key predictors between our two models (e.g., ALT/AST vs. TBA/ALP) provide model-derived evidence for different dominant injury mechanisms, offering deeper pathophysiological insight beyond mere performance gains [25, 26]. Clinical prediction models are essential tools in modern healthcare, designed to predict disease risk, diagnostic outcomes, treatment efficacy, or prognosis by analyzing patients’ clinical data. Traditional statistical methods, such as logistic regression and Cox regression, have long dominated clinical prediction but exhibit significant limitations when handling high-dimensional data, nonlinear relationships, and complex interactions [27, 28]. In contrast, machine learning methods, with their robust data processing capabilities and flexibility, are increasingly becoming vital supplements to clinical prediction models, offering new solutions for the early prediction of complex diseases and personalized treatment. In the field of predicting ATB-DILI, machine learning techniques have demonstrated broad application prospects. Multiple studies have utilized various machine learning algorithms to develop efficient prediction models and achieved remarkable results. For instance, Lai [29] constructed nine ATB-DILI prediction models using artificial neural networks, support vector machines, and random forests, based on seven clinical factors and four genetic data points. Among these, the artificial neural network performed particularly well, achieving a prediction accuracy of 88.67%. Zhong et al. employed the XGBoost algorithm, combined with patient indicators such as alanine aminotransferase levels, cumulative pyrazinamide dose, and cumulative ethambutol dose, to develop a high-accuracy prediction model that achieved an accuracy of 90% in the validation set [18]. Additionally, Feng et al. developed a deep learning model based on gene expression data. By collecting DILI-related gene expression data from the ArrayExpress database and performing feature gene selection and parameter optimization, they successfully achieved early prediction of DILI [30]. These studies indicate that machine learning techniques hold significant advantages in the early prediction of ATB-DILI. By leveraging advanced algorithms such as artificial neural networks, support vector machines, random forests, and deep learning, researchers can construct highly accurate prediction models, providing strong support for clinical practice. These models not only handle complex clinical data but also identify potential risk factors, thereby enabling personalized treatment plans for patients and reducing the incidence and severity of ATB-DILI. Machine learning has facilitated the identification of novel biomarkers for AT-DILI. DNA methylation patterns in genes such as CYP2D6 and NAT2, as well as serum microRNA-122, have emerged as promising diagnostic and prognostic markers, with some methylation signatures achieving high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.94) [11, 31]. Feature selection techniques in machine learning models help optimize predictive performance by focusing on the most relevant clinical and molecular features [32–34]. Machine learning is also used to predict patient adherence in TB treatment when liver enzyme levels rise, with neural networks and random forests performing best [35]. However, challenges remain, including data heterogeneity, limited sample sizes, and the need for external validation. Interpretability and clinical integration of ML models are ongoing concerns [33]. In this context, overfitting and generalizability remain critical considerations in model validation and deployment. While some models achieved perfect AUC (AUC = 1) on the training set, their strong validation performance (e.g., AUC > 0.95) supports generalizability. Overfitting was mitigated via independent validation, cross-validation, and regularization. Future multicenter validation is recommended to further ensure robustness. In summary, machine learning offers promising tools for predicting and understanding antituberculosis drug-induced liver injury, with models integrating clinical, genomic, and molecular data showing high accuracy. Advances in biomarker discovery and model optimization are improving early detection, but further research is needed to address data limitations and enhance clinical applicability.

Conclusion

This study confirms that a machine learning-based stratified prediction strategy can effectively identify patients at high risk for ATB-DILI. The XGBoost and RF models demonstrated near-perfect discriminatory ability in the overall population and the CHB subgroup, respectively. The inherent differences in key predictors between these populations reveal distinct underlying dominant mechanisms of liver injury. This study not only provides a powerful data-driven tool for the early and precise warning of ATB-DILI but also offers a “stratified modeling” approach that can inform risk prediction research for other diseases.
