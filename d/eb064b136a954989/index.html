Building Hybrid Pharmacometric-Machine Learning Models in Oncology Drug Development: Current State and Recommendations

Abstract

Classic and hybrid pharmacometric-machine learning models (hPMxML) are gaining momentum for applications in clinical drug development and precision medicine, especially within the oncology therapeutic area. However, standardized workflows are needed to ensure transparency, rigor, and effective communication for broader adoption. In this tutorial, we review pharmacometric (PMx) and machine learning (ML) reporting standards and evaluate them against hPMxML works in oncology contexts as a motivational example to identify current deficiencies and propose mitigation strategies for future efforts. The revealed gaps include insufficient benchmarking, absence of error propagation, feature stability assessments, and ablation studies, limited focus on external validation and final parametrization, and discrepancies between the performance metrics chosen and the original clinical questions. To address these, we propose a checklist for hPMxML model development and reporting, consisting of steps for estimand definition, data curation, covariate selection, hyperparameter tuning, convergence assessment, model explainability, diagnostics, uncertainty quantification, validation and verification with sensitivity analyses. This standardized approach is expected to enhance the reliability and reproducibility of hPMxML outputs, enabling their confident application in oncology clinical drug development, while fostering trust among all stakeholders.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) into the pharmaceutical industry is positioned to transform drug development, offering new tools to enhance insights and improve clinical decision‐making. Clinical pharmacology, for instance, might benefit from these methods to improve the adaptive design of Phase I trials [1], tailor product‐specific guidance [2], or automatize non‐compartmental analysis [3]. Health authorities (HAs) might see opportunities to establish a new regulatory framework [4] with AI/ML assisting regulatory decisions, as it occurred for the SAVE‐MORE trial in COVID‐19 patients [5] sponsored by the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). Pharmacometrics (PMx), on the other hand, might profit from boosting its traditional inferential strengths with the predictive power of ML to enhance clinical outcome assessment [6, 7, 8, 9]. In this sense, on top of classic AI/ML applications, PMx is experiencing a wave of methodological hybridization with AI/ML techniques as emphasized in [7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16] and in software domain activities, such as [15, 17, 18]. Although developed for different scopes or contexts of use (CoU), the majority of current hybrid PMx‐ML clinical applications can be broadly classified into three main categories, based on their goals of integrating PMx and AI/ML. The first category includes ML algorithms employed to scan extensive covariate pools against individual parameter estimates of PMx models (as targets). The process is intended to identify clinically meaningful and/or statistically influential covariates for subsequent inclusion in the PMx framework, effectively supplementing or accelerating traditional (bias‐prone) covariate search methods such as stepwise covariate modeling (SCM) [11, 19]. The second category deals with AI/ML architectures trained on PMx model‐derived metrics (AUC, Cmax, growth rates) and observed baseline or time‐varying covariates to support exposure‐response, regression (biomarker or pharmacokinetics‐pharmacodynamics (PK‐PD) metrics), survival analysis, or classification tasks [8, 12, 20, 21, 22]. The last category leverages AI/ML‐based models to infer unknown dynamical equations from data for PK‐PD and disease progression modeling, with the possible incorporation of inter‐subject variability and the usage of new (undiscovered) high‐dimensional modalities [7, 10, 23, 24, 25]. Building on these premises, cross‐industry discussion groups (such as the IQ Clinical Pharmacology Leadership Group's AI/ML Working Group) and regulatory agency committees have been established to foster the integration of AI/ML principles in clinical development. Although, the aim of these initiatives is to promote alignment on AI/ML‐based clinical products in high‐risk CoU, most of their work is primarily focused on review of use cases of AI/ML [26, 27, 28], sidelining methodological must‐haves or solutions to incorporate into the statistical analysis plan. The TRIPOD‐AI [29], CONSORT‐AI [30], and SPIRIT‐AI [31] reporting guidelines and the PROBAST‐AI form [32] are to date the most advanced documents discussing AI/ML model building from a technical viewpoint, with Food and Drug Administration (FDA)'s [33, 34] and European Medicines Agency (EMA)'s [35] primers bringing regulatory insights on top. FDA, in particular, has provided a list of general questions‐to‐be‐addressed—on (1) human‐led governance, accountability, and transparency; (2) data quality, reliability, and representativeness; (3) model development, performance, monitoring, and validation—to minimize pushbacks when AI/ML interventions are part of the submission package [33]. In this sense, adoption of a risk‐based credibility framework based on a “model influence‐versus‐decision consequences” matrix is strongly encouraged at each developmental step to meet these regulatory expectations [34]. Nonetheless, all these “good practice” efforts tend to be quite universal and unspecific to accommodate AI/ML models in a broad pharmaceutical context, from clinical research to manufacturing, and usage., from classical regression tasks to generative AI. Thereby, they may focus less on the specificities of model‐informed drug development (MIDD) field and related data preparation workstreams, or on ad hoc precautions necessary when AI/ML is combined with other modeling techniques, often used in PMx. In this sense, hPMxML model practitioners are currently working in isolation, approaching the hybrid modeling in a plethora of ways, due to the lack of unified reporting and developmental practices specific to these new methods, for example, error propagation handling and causality. Because of this setup, the full potential of this modeling partnership might be hindered or slowed down as transparency, reproducibility, and clinical adoptability are negatively impacted. Building on this gap, the present article is intended to advocate a mindset shift in the current ML and PMx landscape in favor of workflow standardization and harmonization, as Figure 1 shows. In particular, our aims are fourfold. First, to equip pharmacometricians willing to incorporate ML techniques in some daily tasks, as well as ML experts willing to apply their know‐how to clinical pharmacology, with a roadmap for hPMxML model building and validation steps that ensure compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) standards and regulatory expectations. Second, as collaboratively crafted by PMx and AI/ML experts, to align PMx and ML communities in terms of terminology and respective workflows, thereby easing cross‐functional work within different divisions of the pharmaceutical industry. Third, to avoid a “lost‐in‐translation” case from fragmented PMx‐ML exercises to clinical development routine [36], guaranteeing that questions for real‐world usage are addressed technically and clinically. Fourth, in line with cross‐setting expert opinions on the PMx‐modelers‐of‐the‐future [37], to provide educational material for training the next generation of ML‐aware pharmacometricians. While our work stems from an analysis of the current status of hPMxML in oncology as a motivational case, the proposal is intended to be guidance for scientists across various disease domains. The vignette compares the current approach in integrating PMx and machine learning modeling with our envisioned one. While today's efforts are isolated with modelers producing their model report and analysis at the cost of generalizability, reproducibility, and communicability, the introduction of a checklist of good development and validation practices may help in harmonizing results, while endorsing this new hybrid modeling in high‐risk contexts.

Review Workflow for PMx and ML Guidelines

Well‐established technical guidelines and working protocols were reviewed to identify must‐haves and nice‐to‐haves reporting elements for the PMx and AI/ML communities, respectively. For the AI/ML domain, insights on optimal development and reporting standards were derived from Collins et al. (TRIPOD‐AI) [29], Norgeot et al. (MI‐CLAIM) [38], Luo et al. [39], and Navarro et al. [40, 41]. For specifics on prognostic studies, Dhiman et al. [42] and Altman et al. [43] were considered, while the PROBAST‐AI document [32] was utilized to inspect the ML landscape in terms of biases and mitigation strategies. Musuamba et al. [44], Lim et al. [45], Schmidt et al. [46], and Bergsma et al. [47] were reviewed to gain a pictorial overview of established PMx workflows, from model assumptions to model credibility assessment and validation. Reporting standards for traditional statistical models employed in prognostic studies were derived from Kempf et al. [48] and Janssens et al. (GRIPS) [49], while expectations on the selection of validation metrics came from Steyerberg et al. [50]. To facilitate meaningful cross‐domain comparisons between pharmacometric and machine learning approaches, the identified documents were organized according to a stepwise modeling framework (see keyword paragraph and Tables S1 and S2). This framework reflects the typical progression of model development, including: defining the question of interest or context of use, data selection and preparation, specification of features or covariates, model development and training, evaluation and validation, performance assessment, and analysis of uncertainty, sensitivity, and model limitations. These stages were aligned with concepts outlined in the FDA draft guidance on Artificial Intelligence to Support Regulatory Decision‐Making for Drug and Biological Products [34]. An occurrence analysis was performed to identify commonalities and distinctions across the two domains. Elements found exclusively in one domain and not addressed in the guidance were treated as domain‐specific in the comparison.

Review Workflow for hPMxML Model Candidates and Evaluation Criteria

Publicly available hPMxML models in oncology clinical development contexts were assessed for reporting completeness and quality against the unified PMx and ML standards previously identified. An initial set of candidate works was identified by the four sequential queries (i) “(Machine Learning OR Machine Learning‐based OR Interpretable Machine Learning OR Explainable Machine Learning OR Neural Differential Ordinary Equations OR NODE OR Neural‐ODE) AND (Pharmacometrics)”, (ii) “(Pharmacometrics) AND (Machine Learning) AND (Cancer OR Tumor OR Oncology)”, (iii) “(Neural ODE) AND (Cancer OR Tumor OR Oncology)”, and (iv) “(Survival Prediction) AND (Machine Learning) AND (Clinical Pharmacology) AND (Oncology) NOT (Genetics AND Radiomics AND Metabolomics AND Imaging)”. The queries were restricted to a period ranging between 01 January 2020 and 15 December 2024, with evident applications to the oncology disease area. Reviews, comments, editorials, tutorials, comparative/observational studies, case reports were discarded, as well as original articles not presenting a clear quantitative use case nor a clinical purpose nor cancer‐specific synthetic data. Candidates making use of bioinformatic processing of omics/genetic sequencing or imaging techniques were deemed not eligible. Examples of unsupervised AI/ML were considered beyond the intention of this work and therefore dropped. For a full description of the review implementation, refer to the PRISMA flow diagram in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). Each high‐level element resulting from the comparison of PMx and AI/ML workstrams was dissected to identify objective metrics for assessing the selected models. Evaluation metrics included reporting of data description (with delineation of applied data manipulations), rationale for performance metric selection, presence of an uncertainty quantification intended as a confidence interval for results, statements on validation techniques and dataset representativeness, standards of model explainability and interpretability, presence of model verification via sensitivity or robustness analysis, benchmarking, and code sharing. Ad hoc metrics emerging from the integration of PMx with AI/ML, that is, report of causal findings and rationale for error propagation handling, were also added. Categories scoring less than 50% in a normalized frequency analysis of report completeness across identified published works were considered inadequately conformed.

Identified Similarities and Differences in PMx and ML Workstreams

Routinely adopted checklists for optimal development and validation of PMx and ML models emphasized substantial similarities in building precautions and reporting standards, alongside core specificities. Grounding on both techniques, we argue that hPMxML practitioners should be aware of these distinctions, either methodologically and terminology‐wise, before commencing any modeling endeavor and should be compliant to both while designing the analysis plan. In this sense, as Table 1 shows, PMx and AI/ML workflows agree on (i) having a CoU‐specific plan, (ii) declaring assumptions towards model credibility criteria, (iii) justifying the statistical adequacy of the framework and its verification via sensitivity analysis and robustness check, (iv) demonstrating the cases where the model fails (model boundaries), and (v) disclosing code and model parameters. While the alignment also extends to the quantification of any uncertainties, this aspect might be handled differently by each domain. With the bootstrap technique bilaterally adopted, PMx has found alternatives in Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR) methods [51], asymptotic distribution properties, and/or clinical trial simulation plots generated from covariance‐based samplings, while AI/ML in variance/ensemble‐based methods [52] or conformal prediction [53] depending on the model architecture. Besides, although both PMx [54, 55] and AI/ML guidelines theoretically advocate for external validation to ensure model generalizability, in practice, this step is much more established in the AI/ML field. Indeed, even when PMx models are intended for predictive tasks, validation against other datasets might be postponed or applied to different CoU [56], with in‐distribution validation in the form of (prediction‐corrected) Visual Predictive Check (pc‐VPC) [57], Numerical Predictive Check (NPC) or Posterior Predictive Check (PPC) [58] implemented as a faster supplement. Moreover, while AI/ML routinely performs data splitting for internal validation purposes, PMx often uses the entire dataset (hence, no data splitting) for model development, particularly in early phases, to avoid any harmful loss of data that might impact covariate detection and estimate precision. Besides, AI/ML protocols put emphasis on benchmarking the product outputs against competitors and on unraveling a contextual model explainability, which are usually never sustained or taken for granted in PMx, respectively. Finally, while data augmentation via oversampling strategies is commonly applied in AI/ML to mitigate imbalances or enrich portions of interest in the data, PMx routine discourages any data modification of this type (apart from synthetic entries for data imputation) [54] to avoid biases on model parameter estimates. State of the Field for hPMxML Models in Oncology

To illustrate the current status of the hPMxML literature against discussed PMx and ML must‐haves and quality checks, an extensive review of oncology hybrid models was carried out. 29 works (see Table S3) were deemed suitable to be included in our exemplificative analysis. For 2 (7%) of them, the primary objective was methodological, with test cases on in silico or real oncology data, while for the remaining, it was clinical. 14 (48%) included clear components from both AI/ML and PMx/statistical modeling, with the large majority using PMx‐derived metrics as either inputs or targets for the AI/ML intervention and 2 implementing neural‐ODE techniques to complete an ordinary differential equation (ODE) system. 18 (62%) papers had the primary AI/ML component used for regression, half of which were for time‐to‐event settings, and 11 for binary or multi‐class classification. A comprehensive list of metrics, derived from PMx and AI/ML workflows and expectations, was used to evaluate the reporting quality of the selected hPMxML within a drug development and regulatory framework. For each metric, we verified if reporting details were met or not. Figure 2 shows the results of our evaluation through barplots counting the frequency of comprehensively reported items across the analyzed models. Major deficiencies (below the average cut‐off of 50%) were found in error propagation handling, robustness assessment (such as feature stability or multi‐start consistency), metric completeness, benchmarking, and material sharing. While we found the clinical question clearly stated in the paper introduction for all cases, we recognized a general lack of compliance to the ICH E9(R1) addendum (estimand definition in the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use) [59], especially for the declaration of the population‐level summary measure and intercurrent event handling strategy when applicable. We acknowledged a comprehensive data description in terms of the clinical context (trial or treatment details, population characteristics, predictor and clinical endpoint rationale), but a milder one in terms of possible data manipulation techniques applied (average ~75%). We found internal validation, whether in the form of a training/test split or of cross‐validation techniques (most employed) or of jackknife validation, fully covered, but less than 25% incidence of external validation. Comparison of AI/ML architectures was achieved in roughly 55% of works, with 7 manuscripts showing benchmark efforts against competitor models or SoC techniques (e.g., Stein pharmacodynamic model in oncology or SCM in covariate searching). The majority of classification and survival analysis works missed providing a strong rationale for the selection of the predictive performance metric (area under the Receiver Operating Characteristics curve (AUCROC) vs. Precision‐recall curve in imbalance set, discrimination (AUC/Cindex) vs. calibration metrics), while alignment between research questions and metrics was fully achieved in regression tasks. Comparison with literature expectation or upfront definition of successful criteria as per CoU were almost never discussed. Model explainability was well analyzed (> 75%), with the latest technologies, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [60], which allow for either local interpretability and causal insights via conditional effects on a feature on the selected outcome, still under‐used (< 18%). In terms of model inspections, we found that sensitivity analysis on thresholds or cutoffs was quite well performed (almost 75% occurrence), although rooms for possible user‐based biases in model development could still be present, while subgroup analysis was approached mostly via subgroup‐specific architectures only or ML‐derived stratification. No analysis was conducted on understanding which cohorts were better predicted by a unique model. Error propagation was never discussed, leading to underestimation of the uncertainty and inability to decouple the source of variability. Finally, no works attempted a robustness assessment via a multi‐start approach nor feature stability checks. Checklist Proposal for hPMxML Model Building

Despite the advantages and interest in hPMxML methodologies in clinical development contexts, the need for harmonization is still unmet. To address this demand and to guarantee the trustworthiness and credibility of these methods, we present a comprehensive checklist for hPMxML model building (Table 2), primarily intended to accommodate randomized clinical trial (RCT) datasets as the backbone of data‐driven drug development. The proposed checklist is structured in seven blocks—clinical setup, data preprocessing, model training, model validation, secondary analysis, applicability domain, and benchmark studies—each including distinct and detailed subpoints. Descriptors were sign‐coded (Δ = mandatory, Ο = optional) based on their regulatory weight—as derived from [34]—to alert users on which tasks to prioritize. This is expected to be particularly beneficial in cases where development time is tight or when users first approach this new hybrid modeling modality and might feel overwhelmed by the number of listed items. The last three columns labeled as Cat correspond to the three categories of hPMxML models presented in the introduction, namely Cat. III = neural‐ODE systems to compensate for missing mechanistic terms.

Main Considerations

An upfront deep knowledge of the CoU is a must‐have condition to develop ML‐based models as it drives most of the preliminary assumptions, technical choices, and benefit–risk evaluations along the full model development process. In this sense, we encourage the frame of the clinical question—target population, endpoint of interest, treatment conditions, summary measures, and intercurrent event [59]—and the definition of “model success” criteria to be jointly agreed a priori by clinical stakeholders and modelers, based on risk appetite and competitor performances. In addition, once the intended model usage is clearly defined, we suggest a critical and statistical analysis of the final filtered database to validate model utility toward goal achievement. In this sense, we refer to [71, 72] for simulation studies on minimum sample size in clinical AI/ML settings and to [73] for R‐based computational support in the calculation. In addition, to gain a preliminary understanding of the overfitting likelihood for the planned setup, we refer to [74] for an overview on covariate pool versus sample size in specific classification contexts. In terms of communicability and usability awareness, a clear discussion on the selected sample size and secondary analysis on performance degradation when the number of training examples is reduced is nonetheless beneficial to provide indications on which drug development stages the model could serve. To ensure reproducibility, we recommend that data engineering processes be documented as a sequential list of applied transformations. In instances where data manipulation is not conducted prior to AI/ML training, an explicit statement should be included to inform practitioners and prevent any misconceptions. In this regard, while data imputation is typically performed before model development, its implementation may vary by model type. For example, NONMEM scripts and tree‐based methods handle missing data automatically, whereas Lasso does not. A more transparent and unbiased—though time‐consuming—approach would be to conduct imputation separately for the development and (cross‐)validation sets/folds, integrating it as part of model training. In this regard, differently from PMx routine, a random or stratified splitting of the entire dataset into development and internal validation sets is mandatory to prove the generalizable capability of the ML‐based component. While this is the bare minimum, we strongly suggest embarking on hPMxML efforts only in the presence of external validation sets that are clinically and statistically evaluated as representative of the intended target population or for which covariate shift can be rationalized. In this regard, an upfront comprehensive side‐by‐side comparison of longitudinal observations or covariate values between developmental and externally collected datasets is mandatory to detect and, in case, quantify this data shift phenomenon. In this latter case, an early interaction with clinical statisticians is strongly suggested for alignments on the setup validity, as well as for strategies to account for it. This is especially relevant in cases where practitioners may want to use real‐world data (RWD) sources—electronic health records (EHRs), claims data, patient‐reported outcomes, digital health devices—which notoriously have higher dimensionality, more missingness, heterogeneity, and subgroup under‐representativeness compared to RCT [75]. In this case, a stringent selection criteria of the baselines and an understanding of the operational differences between clinical practice and trial protocols is fundamental to accomplish translatability and validity assessments [76]. If these look promising, modelers are called to include and detail confounding mitigation strategies, such as censoring‐informed propensity score matching (with information on selected distance metric, pairing method, and ratio), in addition to the data processing and model validation items of Table 2. As part of model robustness analysis, we encourage checking the stability of the ML‐selected features (both presence and ranking) across cross‐validation folds and a multi‐start approach with different seeds towards the verification of influential outliers and of model robustness, respectively. For an overview of the different measures available to assess this consistency, we refer the reader to [77, 78]. In this regard, we warn practitioners to carefully evaluate clinical interpretability, collection costs, and patient burden of each feature before selecting correlation‐aware measures, that is, measures that judge as interchangeable highly‐correlated features, over unaware ones. In addition, ablation studies via leave‐one‐out covariate or model part should be conducted to further disentangle the true impact of the component of interest, phasing out the likelihood of mediation or compensation effects. In terms of hPMxML‐specific diagnostics, we believe error propagation quantification, emerging from the parallel or sequential combination of PMx and AI/ML optimization procedures, plays a crucial role in model assessment. Notably, this quantification might be dependent on the integrative engine put in place, that is, the hPMxML category the model belongs to. For instance, for the first category (ML methods testing covariates against target EBEs), a percentage dropout of key covariates could be applied in the final PMx framework to quantify the uncertainty of the ML‐based covariate selection. For the second group (regression/classification ML methods including PMx‐derived covariates), samples from the conditional distribution could be used to create plausible PMx‐derived metrics at the individual level, and error propagation could be computed via bootstrapped training sets incorporating these covariates without replacement. For models in the third category (neural‐ODE models automatically retrieving missing terms), readers can refer to [79]. In terms of hPMxML model validation, we state (i) selection of CoU‐relevant performance metrics as a trade‐off between model assessment and stakeholder familiarity and (ii) explainability of ML components as regulatory cornerstones. For the first, fairness auditing approaches for ethical considerations should be factored in at this stage and guide the identification of an ad hoc measure, particularly if preventive strategies have not been applied during data analysis (data augmentation) or model development, or if test sets exhibit imbalances in clinically‐relevant covariates, such as age, sex, race, and comedications. The literature here provides effective metrics for bias evaluation to ensure accurate predictive performances for underrepresented groups. For the second, significant theoretical work has been done in recent years to unbox AI/ML models and render them more understandable and transparent. The compendium of these efforts is represented by feature importance plots [80], identifying which input variables are most influential in building the input–output relationship; partial dependence plots [81], visualizing how outputs change as a consequence of a controlled input variation; SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) plots [82], assigning importance weight to each input for individual output; LIME (Local Interpretable Model‐agnostic Explanations) [83], explaining individual outputs through a local approximation of the model. Interestingly, additional methods are available on top of SHAP to convert the feature ranking into clinically‐sounding hazard ratios in survival settings [84] or to plug in direct acyclic graphs among features [85], thereby reading feature importance weights from a causal perspective with no confounding effects or mediations. While the clinical question should clearly translate into a primary research endpoint, we emphasize the International Council for Harmonization ICH E9 recommendation on the use of secondary analysis for additional model verification. Among the others, we strongly suggest performing subgroup analysis on pre‐defined and CoU‐meaningful cohorts to unravel patient or disease subtype behavior and identify those on which the ML‐based model is most performative. In addition, we advise conducting local sensitivity analysis on any pre‐set thresholds or cut‐offs within clinically reasonable variation space in order to make hPMxML free from developer biases and to prove model robustness. Finally, to shift towards a unified approach, we advocate for publishing model codes, whether on shared online platforms (as done via the Drug Disease Model Resources (DDMoRE) repository [86]) or as supporting material within a manuscript, to facilitate re‐use, re‐run, and (occasionally) debug by other practitioners. Given the stringent confidentiality measures on proprietary data in pharma, we acknowledge the difficulty of data sharing on top of modeling scripts in several cases. However, if feasible, we encourage sharing at least a synthetic data template mimicking the format of the real dataset—particularly in terms of headers and column names/types—to guide the work of Clinical Data Programmers.

Conclusions

The pharmaceutical sector welcomes opportunities to evaluate novel technological and modeling advancements in the clinical routine, provided that these add‐ons are well‐shaped, endorsed, and extensively validated. Foreseeing opportunities for hPMxML models, we brainstorm around a long‐lasting adoptability of these models in clinical drug development. While we recognize some successful ML and hPMxML efforts in the exemplified clinical oncology literature, we note a lack of standardization in the developmental and reporting procedures and some deficiencies in light of GCP and health authority requirements. Although it is also true that many reviewed papers were primarily exploratory rather than designed for guiding drug development decisions, we believe they served as crucial starting points to critically evaluate what is necessary for real‐world usage of these technologies across indications. Therefore, to cope with identified gaps in current works, we present our proposal of a comprehensive checklist for optimal and regulatory‐compliant hPMxML model building and validation for clinical drug development overall, beyond the oncology disease therapeutic area per se. Indeed, while oncology has been primarily touched on here—as it catalyzed most of hPMxML efforts so far—similar methodological deficiencies were detected in other diseases, as shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S2). Besides, all non‐oncological hPMxML applications we revised (see Table S4) employed classical regression or classification tasks, leveraging a diverse range of established theory and libraries. Some of these approaches, however, are still maturing in the context of time‐to‐event scenarios typical of cancer modeling, thereby making oncology a more challenging use case for us to brainstorm around methodological best practices. While resulting from an extensive and objective review of works and related pitfalls, we acknowledge that the checklist is produced by a unique pharma, thereby being possibly prone to some biases, and we therefore call the community to action for improvements. Nevertheless, we believe that this first version of the checklist is already in good shape to ensure methodological rigor, reproducibility, and trust among clinical stakeholders, as well as a higher probability of technical success and approval for hPMxML models across all therapeutic areas. We hope that this checklist can strengthen cross collaboration between pharmacometricians, clinical pharmacologists, AI/ML experts, and regulatory agencies to drive innovation in clinical drug development overall. However, it is important for us to caution the Clinical Pharmacology and PMx community against being swayed by AI/ML per se. Clinical modelers should adhere to and build models upon the principle of parsimony, as exemplified by Occam's Razor. Conventional statistical and PMx methods should always be the starting point and the reference, and only when they fail to produce satisfactory results, and under very precise conditions, that is, enough high‐quality data, practitioners may want to explore ML‐based alternatives.
