Abstract

Inflammation plays a critical role in ischemic stroke (IS). This study aimed to identify inflammation-related genes and explore potential pharmacological agents for future preclinical validation in IS. Transcriptome data were integrated to identify inflammation-related genes, which were functionally characterized and evaluated for diagnostic potential, with single-cell analysis and computational drug prediction. Four inflammation-related genes, C-C chemokine receptor type 7 (CCR7), CD7, CD96, and interleukin-7 receptor (IL-7R), were identified from integrated transcriptome analyses. These genes showed promising diagnostic potential (area under the curve (AUC) > 0.8) and were functionally associated with cytokine signaling, immune interactions, and calcium homeostasis. Drug-gene interaction and molecular docking analyses indicated that capecitabine and ruxolitinib are potential candidates for modulating CD96 and IL-7R. This study reveals four inflammation-related genes with preliminary diagnostic value and proposes capecitabine and ruxolitinib as candidate drugs for future preclinical research on IS.

Introduction

Stroke is a cerebrovascular disease with high mortality and disability rates, including ischemic stroke (IS) and hemorrhagic stroke [1, 2]. IS is caused by insufficient blood supply to the brain due to stenosis or even occlusion of the carotid or vertebral artery systems, accounting for 87% of all strokes [3]. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease data, the number of deaths caused by IS has increased from 2.04 million to 3.29 million, and it is estimated that this number will reach 4.9 million by 2030 [4]. Current treatment methods, including drug and interventional thrombolysis, face limitations due to the narrow treatment window and associated risks [5]. In addition, many clinical trials investigating neuroprotective agents have failed to demonstrate any clinical benefits [6]. Iadecola et al. [7] and Moskowitz et al. [8] conducted extensive research on neuroinflammation, emphasizing the crucial role of glial cells and immune responses in the progression of stroke-related brain injury. The pathological mechanisms of IS include intracellular Ca2+ overload, impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, neuroinflammatory response, and neuronal apoptosis [9–11]. Post-ischemic inflammation, which begins swiftly and can last for days to weeks, induces glial activation, immune cell infiltration, and cytokine release that exacerbate oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and blood-brain barrier disruption [12–14]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore potential inflammatory biomarkers for IS to improve early diagnosis, optimize treatment stratification, and guide the development of new treatment strategies, with subsequent validation required to confirm their reliability. Bioinformatics analysis is a powerful analytical tool that can use sequencing data and the corresponding demographic and clinical information to shed light on disease states [15]. Gene chip technology systematically identifies gene expression patterns and regulatory networks, aiding in the discovery of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and candidate targets for therapeutic development [16–18]. However, bioinformatic analyses of IS remain limited. Consequently, this study utilized expression data to investigate the molecular biological mechanisms underlying IS by analyzing biological function at the level of gene expression to generate important references to facilitate an understanding of the morbidity and treatment of IS.

Materials and methods › Accession of bioinformatics data

Gene expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [19]. The datasets GSE16561 (63 samples: 24 control, 39 disease) and GSE37587 (34 disease samples) were processed using the GPL6883 annotation file. To minimize batch effects between datasets, the expression matrices were merged and batch correction was performed using the ComBat function in the Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA, v3.50.0) R package. Since both datasets were generated using the same GPL6883 platform, additional cross-platform normalization was not required. Single-cell data from GSE174574, comprising 3 control and 3 disease samples, were also retrieved. Dataset details are summarized in Table 1. Materials and methods › Differential expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Limma package (v3.58.1), with an adjusted P-value < 0.01 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) and |logFC| >0.5. DEGs were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps.

Materials and methods › Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) function annotation

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the ClusterProfiler R package (v4.6.2) [20].

Materials and methods › Consistency clustering of inflammation-related genes

Consensus clustering was applied to inflammation-related gene expression profiles to define inflammatory subtypes related to IS. The analysis involved 50 iterations, each resampling 90% of the samples. The optimal cluster number was determined by assessing the cumulative distribution function and consensus matrix.

Materials and methods › Enrichment analysis of inflammation-related subtypes

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, v4.6.2) was used to construct a co-expression network. Gene modules were identified based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM) and hierarchical clustering.

Materials and methods › Weighted gene Co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA, v1.72-1) R package [21] was used to construct a scale-free co-expression network. A soft thresholding power of β = 7 was selected to achieve a scale-free topology fit index (R²) >0.85. Network connectivity was assessed through the adjacency matrix and TOM. Genes were then clustered based on their expression profiles to form distinct gene modules.

Materials and methods › Feature selection with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression

The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was employed for feature selection and diagnostic model construction using the glmnet package (v4.1–8) in R. To evaluate the incremental value of the LASSO regularization and to justify its use for feature selection, we performed a comparative analysis against a standard logistic regression model without any penalty term, which was trained on the complete set of 15 candidate hub genes (Fig. S2; Table S1). The optimal penalty parameter (λ) for the LASSO model was determined through 10-fold cross-validation (cv.glmnet function) exclusively on the training cohort. This process leverages L1 regularization (alpha = 1) to automatically shrink the coefficients of non-essential variables to zero. The final model was constructed using the λ value that yielded the minimum mean cross-validated error (λmin).

Materials and methods › Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, v1.50.0) was performed to evaluate the enrichment of gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) database, converting gene-level expression into pathway-level scores.

Materials and methods › Immune cell infiltration analysis

The Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm was used to estimate the proportions of 22 immune cell types from the transcriptome data [22], with a permutation test (1000 permutations) and a confidence score threshold >0.7 to filter reliable results.

Materials and methods › Analysis of transcriptional regulation of key genes

Potential transcription factors regulating key genes were identified using RcisTarget (v1.19.2). The analysis was performed using the motif annotation database ‘motifAnnotations_hgnc_v9’. The normalized enrichment score (NES) for motifs was calculated based on recovery curves and the area under the curve (AUC).

Materials and methods › Construction of MicroRNA (miRNA) network

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) targeting the key genes were retrieved from the miRcode database. The interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape.

Materials and methods › Quality control

Quality control for single-cell data was performed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0). Cells were filtered based on unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, gene numbers, and mitochondrial/ribosomal gene content (median absolute deviation (MAD), threshold = 3). Doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4).

Materials and methods › Data standardization and cell annotation

Data were normalized using LogNormalize. Cell cycle scores were calculated, and highly variable genes were identified. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Harmony (for batch correction, v1.1.0), followed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell types were annotated with SingleR and the CellMarker database.

Materials and methods › Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) analysis

The Single-cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC, v1.2.6) workflow was used to infer gene regulatory networks. GENIE3 (v1.24.0)identified co-expression modules, followed by motif enrichment analysis to define regulons (a transcription factor (TF) and its targets). Regulon activity was scored to identify cell states.

Materials and methods › Predictive analysis of drug interactions for key genes

Potential interactions between key genes and drugs or compounds were queried using the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb).

Materials and methods › Molecular Docking

The 3D structures of proteins (from PDB or AlphaFold) and compounds (from PubChem) were prepared for docking. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock with a genetic algorithm (50 runs). In this study, a binding energy threshold of −5.0 kcal/mol was applied to define a significant binding interaction, consistent with established practices in molecular docking for identifying potential bioactive compounds [23, 24]. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected, and interactions were visualized with PyMOL.

Materials and methods › Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 8 IS patients and 8 healthy controls. The demographic characteristics and inclusion/exclusion criteria of study participants are summarized in Table S3. Blood samples were collected within 24–72 h after stroke onset for patients and on the day of health examination for healthy controls. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Cat. # RN102, Edgar) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA using the SuperScript III RT kit (Cat. # A502, EXONGEN). Quantitative PCR was performed with the SYBR qPCR Mix (Cat. # 4472920, ABI-Invitrogen) on a Longgene Q2000B Real-Time PCR system. Each 10 µL reaction contained 5 µL SYBR Green mix, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA, and 2 µL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol was: 95℃ for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 10 s, 55℃ for 20 s, and 72℃ for 15 s. Triplicate analyses were conducted for each sample. Primer sequences for the housekeeping gene Actin and the target genes C-C motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7), CD7, CD96, and interleukin-7 receptor (IL-7R) are listed in Table S2. Actin served as the internal control, and melt curve analysis confirmed the specificity of amplification. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method, with the healthy control group as the reference. Statistical analysis was conducted using the Student’s t-test, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Materials and methods › Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism 8. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results › Identification of intersecting genes relevant to both IS and inflammation

Figure 1 illustrates the study design. We analyzed two IS-related transcriptomic datasets (GSE16561 and GSE37587), comprising 24 control and 73 disease samples. PCA revealed a strong batch effect before correction (Dim1: 99.5% variance; Fig. 2A), which was effectively mitigated using ComBat (Dim1: 40.5%, Dim2: 11%; Fig. 2B). Differential expression analysis identified 216 DEGs (103 upregulated, 113 downregulated; FDR < 0.01, |logFC| >0.5; Fig. 2C–D). Intersection of these DEGs with 200 inflammation-related genes from the literature [25] yielded 11 overlapping genes (Fig. 2E).

Results › LASSO regression feature model

LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation selected four genes from the 15 hub genes for a diagnostic model (Fig. 6A–B). The risk score was calculated as: (0.042 × CD7) + (0.052 × CD96) + (0.085 × IL-7R) + (0.220 × CCR7) (Fig. 6C). The model demonstrated discriminative capacity in the training set (AUC = 0.997; Fig. 6D) and showed potential for generalization in an external cohort (GSE58294, AUC = 0.708; Fig. S8). This performance gap between training and external validation, although indicative of potential overfitting, was influenced by several factors, including technical batch effects and biological heterogeneity between datasets. Despite this decline, the model maintained statistically significant discriminatory ability in the external dataset, confirming that the identified four-gene signature captured biologically relevant signals of IS.

Discussion

IS is one of the most common causes of death and disability worldwide, posing a significant threat to human health [26]. Acute cerebral ischemic and hypoxic injury in IS can induce a series of complex pathophysiological processes, including focal brain injury and a global inflammatory response, and the inflammatory cascade reaction induced by cerebral ischemia is considered to be the major pathological factor [27–29]. Advances in molecular biology have increasingly focused on the microenvironmental changes and their pathological mechanisms after IS [30, 31]. Therefore, the interplay between genetic factors associated with inflammation and those linked to IS may help identify potential diagnostic biomarkers, prognostic indicators, and candidates for therapeutic development, thereby enhancing our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the disease. Using machine learning methods, we identified four genes (CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R) with preliminary diagnostic potential for IS, requiring substantial validation before clinical application. CCR7 is a marker for naive T cells, activated T lymphocytes, and central memory T cells. Signals mediated by CCR7 and C-C motif chemokine ligand 21 (CCL21) control the lymphatic migration and lymph node homing of several immune cells, including T cells and DCs [32]. In addition, activated T cells can enter the brain after stroke, and these cells may lead to tissue damage. Jiang et al. analyzed the association between cytokines and stroke using Mendelian randomization and showed that CCR7 may be a prognostic factor for stroke [33]. These findings align with the outcomes of the present research. The results of GSVA and immune infiltration in this study showed that high expression of CCR7 and IL-7R was associated with the IFN-α response pathway, and the upregulation of CCR7 was positively correlated with the decrease in the proportion of activated mast cells. Some studies have shown that IFN-α/β can inhibit histamine release from mast cells [34]. In addition, another study showed that histamine protects brain cells from hypoxic damage by upregulating HO-1 [35], these data suggest that CCR7 and IL-7R may influence post-stroke neuroinflammation by modulating mast cell activity through IFN-αsignaling, providing a potential mechanistic link between immune cell regulation and central nervous system protection. CD96 is an inhibitory receptor on the surface of NK cells, which can negatively regulate the secretion of IFN-γ by NK cells, thereby alleviating neuroinflammatory damage [36, 37]. In addition, other studies have shown that CD96 is also involved in T cell differentiation and activation [38], suggesting that CD96 may affect immune tolerance and inflammation regulation, thus regulating the immune response and pathological progression of IS. IL-7 is a cytokine produced in the bone marrow, thymus, and other tissues. IL-7 and IL-7R support of CD4+ T cell homeostasis by promoting T cell survival, proliferation, and de novo generation [39]. After IS, the massive mobilization and functional changes of peripheral CD4+ T cells are considered important drivers of the inflammatory response. The IL-7/IL-7R signaling pathway may amplify the pro-inflammatory response by maintaining the expansion of effector T cells, exacerbating blood-brain barrier damage and neuronal apoptosis. CD7 is an immune receptor expressed on the surface of T cells, and its known ligands include K12 protein and galectin-1 [40, 41]. Studies have shown that the co-stimulation of K12 and CD28 antibody can promote T cell proliferation [42]. In addition, the activation of CD7 mediated by CD7 monoclonal antibody affects the adhesion of NK cells and T cells to fibronectin [43–45], indicating that CD7 not only participates in cell proliferation but may also promote the infiltration of immune cells into damaged brain tissue by regulating the adhesion and migration of immune cells, thus exacerbating the pathological progression. Studies on the association between CD7 and stroke have not yet been reported, and further studies are needed. Although bioinformatics analysis showed that CCR7, IL-7R, CD7, and CD96 were generally downregulated, experimental validation revealed the opposite trend for CCR7, which was upregulated in patients with ischemic stroke. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the biphasic immune response after stroke. While the downregulation of IL-7R, CD7, and CD96 may reflect systemic immune suppression characterized by impaired T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity, the increased expression of CCR7 may indicate central neuroinflammatory activation and enhanced recruitment of immune or microglial cells to the ischemic area. Therefore, ischemic stroke leads to a biphasic immune imbalance in which central inflammation coexists with peripheral immune suppression, rather than uniform immune activation or suppression. This explanation is consistent with previous studies that reported different central and peripheral immune kinetics after stroke [46, 47]. Single-cell sequencing technology can construct cell atlases [48] and research in the fields of developmental biology [49] and immunology [50]. This technology identifies cell type-specific gene expression patterns, revealing the functional states, differentiation dynamics, and interactions of different cell populations. Single-cell sequencing analyses have revealed that CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R are all expressed in endothelial cells, microglia, astrocytes, macrophages, epithelial cells, and monocytes. In addition to the expression patterns of our key marker genes, our single-cell transcriptome data also provide an unbiased overview of the immune landscape, revealing a distinct neutrophil infiltration in IS samples, which is largely absent in the control group. Given the growing evidence that neutrophils exhibit remarkable functional plasticity and that specific subpopulations play crucial non-classical roles in inflammation resolution and immune regulation [51, 52], it greatly expands our understanding of their complex functions in stroke pathology. There is growing evidence that immune cell infiltration and subsequent inflammatory responses contribute to stroke pathogenesis [53]. We assessed immune cell infiltration through the application of CIBERSORT to gain deeper insights into the significance of immune cell infiltration in IS. The results of this study showed that naive B cells, activated dendritic cells activated, Macrophages M2, activated mast cells, neutrophils, activated NK cells, naive CD4+ T cells, and Tregs. The analysis of correlation between critical immune cells indicated that the expression levels of CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R correlated positively with naive CD4+ T cells, while demonstrating a notable negative correlation with mast cells. Consequently, we hypothesize that the reduced expression of CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R may enhance the infiltration of CD4+ T cells and mast cells, contributing to IS pathophysiology. Notably, CD4+ T cells possess the capability to differentiate into either pro-inflammatory Th1 cells or anti-inflammatory Th2 cells, thus playing a dual role in either causing damage to or providing protection for the brain [54, 55]. Mast cells are participants in ischemic brain edema [56]. After ischemic injury, activated mast cells exhibit degranulation activity of gelatinase-positive granules, which is closely related to brain swelling [57]. Another study suggested that inhibiting the degranulation of mast cells in the brain can suppress microglial activation and central nervous system inflammation [58]. Therefore, Gaining insight into the various types, phenotypes, and biological roles of immune cells during distinct phases of stroke may offer a foundational framework for the development of immunotherapeutic strategies targeting stroke. Although we employed cross-validated LASSO regression to optimize the model and control for overfitting, a performance gap persisted between the training and external validation sets. This discrepancy likely stems from several factors inherent to genomic studies: technical batch effects across distinct datasets, the limited training cohort size relative to the high feature dimensionality, and the underlying biological heterogeneity of ischemic stroke patients. Importantly, the model maintained statistically significant discriminatory power in the independent validation cohort, demonstrating that the identified four-gene signature (CCR7, CD7, CD96, IL-7R) transcends these technical and biological variations to capture a core pathophysiological signal in ischemic stroke. Based on the biological relevance of this four-gene signature established above, we further explored its therapeutic potential. The results showed that three drugs (OSE-127, Ruxolitinib, and glycosylated recombinant human IL-7) interact with IL-7R, and one drug (capecitabine) interacts with CD96. Two drugs (phlorizin and anti- CAR-T cell preparations) interact with CD7. In silico molecular docking analyses revealed that capecitabine binds strongly to CD96, with a binding energy of −5.14 kcal/mol, indicating a stable and favorable interaction. This suggests that capecitabine may modulate CD96-mediated pathways, potentially enhancing its therapeutic efficacy in IS. Similarly, ruxolitinib exhibited robust binding to IL-7R, with a binding energy of −4.21 kcal/mol, consistent with its reported ability to reduce neuroinflammation and tissue injury following IS via inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [59]. These in silico interactions suggest that both drugs could serve as candidates for future preclinical validation, as they may influence critical immune-modulatory pathways in IS. Capecitabine-CD96 and ruxolitinib-IL-7R interactions highlight potential avenues for targeted immunomodulatory strategies, warranting further preclinical validation. Although our research has made progress in many aspects, there are still some limitations. First, the RT-qPCR validation was conducted on a small cohort of only 8 IS patients and 8 healthy controls, which constrains the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Consequently, the observed expression patterns of CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R should be considered preliminary and exploratory in nature. Second, while efforts were made to minimize batch effects by utilizing multiple public datasets, the combination of different array datasets may still impact result stability. Third, our drug predictions are based solely on in silico analyses. Although we used a standard binding energy threshold of −5.0 kcal/mol for significance, and the docking results for capecitabine-CD96 (−5.14 kcal/mol) and ruxolitinib-IL-7R (−4.21 kcal/mol) were favorable, these findings remain computational predictions. Crucially, they are not currently supported by any direct experimental or clinical data. The static nature of docking cannot replicate the dynamic cellular environment, account for pharmacokinetics, or guarantee in vivo efficacy. Therefore, these proposed drug-target interactions are strictly hypothesis-generating and necessitate validation through future in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Conclusion

Our bioinformatics analysis suggests CCR7, CD96, CD7, and IL-7R may play roles in IS pathogenesis through inflammation and immune cell infiltration. These genes provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of IS and show preliminary potential as early diagnostic candidates, with their status as formal biomarkers requiring confirmation through large-scale clinical validation and functional studies. Their expression patterns provide initial data that could contribute to future development of non-invasive diagnostic tools, though additional validation (e.g., in diverse patient populations, different clinical settings) is essential to ensure these tools are reliable for timely diagnosis and improved prognostication. Furthermore, they represent candidates for preclinical investigation of potential immunomodulatory strategies, consistent with their initial diagnostic potential, offering new avenues for exploring the regulation of immune responses, reducing brain injury, and improving recovery. Future studies will investigate the molecular mechanisms by which these genes influence IS progression, providing the foundation for exploring targeted gene-based interventions.
