Abstract

Pediatric oncology drug development remains uniquely challenging due to the rarity and biological heterogeneity of childhood cancers, ethical considerations in trial conduct, and the limited feasibility of large, randomized studies. Despite these barriers, recent years have seen a notable acceleration in the approval of oncology therapies for pediatric populations, driven by advances in molecularly targeted treatments, evolving regulatory requirements, and innovation in trial design. Reducing nonclinical data requirements and increasing the adaptation of model‑informed drug development approaches are also contributing to advances in pediatric oncology treatment by supporting dose selection, optimizing study design, and reducing unnecessary patient burden. In this review, recent regulatory requirements from the United States, the EU, and other key regions on pediatric oncology drug development are discussed. Thirty-three drugs with oncology indications in pediatric populations approved by the US FDA between 2018 and 2025 are reviewed. These approvals provide examples of how nonclinical and clinical data were generated with a focus on strategies for dose-finding and justification. Common challenges and considerations related to clinical operations and formulation development in pediatric populations and the emerging use of real-world data, external controls, and artificial intelligence/machine learning are also discussed.

Introduction

The advancement in the understanding of cancer biology, novel treatment modalities, personalized medicine, and supportive care has increased the 5‐year survival rate of pediatric cancers from 63% in the mid‐1970s to 87% in 2015‐2021 in the United States. 1 , 2 , 3 As an example, for acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL), the most common type of leukemia in children (ages 0‐14 years) and adolescents (ages 15‐19 years), the 5‐year survival rate has steadily increased from 14% in the late 1960s to an impressive 90% in 2026. 4 , 5 Despite these successes, cancer remains the leading disease‐related cause of death in children and adolescents with an estimated 9680 and 5660 new cases and 1090 and 730 deaths, respectively in 2026, 5 highlighting the need for continued research and innovative treatment approaches. In recent years, there has been a notable increase in the number of approvals of oncology drugs for pediatric populations by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). FDA approvals have exhibited a sustained upward trajectory since 2017, consistently exceeding 5 approvals per year, with more than 15 distinct drug‐indication approvals recorded in 2025 alone (Figure 1). The current approval landscape is dominated by small molecules and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), while newer modalities such as antibody drug conjugates (ADCs), bispecific antibodies, radiopharmaceuticals, and cell therapies remain relatively limited. Drug approvals span a broad range of pediatric malignancies, led by leukemia but extending to multiple solid and other rare cancers, indicating expanding therapeutic coverage across pediatric cancers. Importantly, most approved drugs include indications for infants, children, and adolescents, whereas approvals that explicitly include neonates remain uncommon, underscoring persistent evidence gaps in the youngest and most developmentally vulnerable patient population. This surge in drug approvals can largely be attributed to the strategic requirements and incentives established by legislation in the United States, such as the Research to Accelerate Cures and Equity (RACE) for Children Act in 2017, 6 , 7 and regulatory requirements such as initial Pediatric Study Plans (iPSPs) by the FDA. These requirements have played a pivotal role in moving the initiation of pediatric study from a median of 0.04 years after adult approval before the RACE Act to a median of 2.8 years before the adult approval after the RACE Act, 8 significantly fostering the development of pediatric oncology therapeutics. 9 As a result, this article focuses on FDA approvals, while acknowledging the important role existing legislation outside of the US plays in support of global pediatric drug development. The importance of clinical pharmacology in pediatric oncology was previously articulated by Shebley et al in 2019. 10 Building upon this foundation, this review synthesizes key clinical pharmacology considerations derived from selected oncology drugs approved between 2018 and September 2025 (Table 1, see Clinical Study Design section for the selection criteria). In addition, it offers a detailed roadmap for stakeholders in pediatric oncology, encompassing essential aspects of regulatory engagement, preclinical data requirements, practical considerations for pediatric oncology trials, advances in quantitative pharmacology, and the emerging role of novel approaches and technologies in pediatric oncology drug development (Figure 2). Together, these evolving pediatric regulatory requirements have elevated clinical pharmacology to play a central role in pediatric oncology drug development, with exposure matching, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling, and other quantitative approaches increasingly forming the evidentiary basis for regulatory decision‑making. Regulatory Requirement

Key Differences Between the US and EU Regulatory Framework in Pediatric Drug Development

Regulatory Requirement › Key Regulatory Frameworks and Global Considerations

Pediatric requirements have become an increasingly early and integral component of oncology drug development. The United States and the EU now apply markedly different regulatory triggers—target‐based in the United States and condition‐based in the EU—requiring sponsors to plan pediatric strategies proactively and coordinate globally from the outset.

Regulatory Requirement › United States: PREA, RACE, and BPCA

In the United States, the Pediatric Research Equity Act (PREA) requires pediatric assessments for most new drugs and biologics unless waived or deferred. Sponsors must submit an iPSP no later than 60 days after the End‐of‐Phase 2 meeting and before beginning pivotal trials, with the FDA expecting the iPSP to be agreed prior to New Drug Application (NDA)/Biologics License Application (BLA) submission. 47 , 48 PREA allows full or partial waivers—including cases where pediatric studies would be impossible, unethical, or unlikely to be beneficial—and deferrals when studies must occur after adult data become available. 47 , 49 Deferral requests must include specific milestones and timelines. 47 , 49 For example, the development of nivolumab (a PD‐1 inhibitor) with relatlimab‐rmbw (a LAG‐3 inhibitor) as a fixed‐dose combination (FDC) received a partial waiver from the requirements of PREA for patients <12 years of age, presumably due to the rarity of melanoma in pediatrics, the indication the sponsor seeks approval for, despite the clinical relevance of LAG‐3 expression across pediatric tumors. 13 The RACE for Children Act was passed by the US Congress in 2017 and took effect on August 18, 2020, significantly expanding PREA requirements for oncology. RACE links pediatric obligations to a drug's molecular target or mechanism of action if that target is relevant to pediatric cancer biology, irrespective of the adult indication. 50 , 51 It also eliminated the orphan exemption for targeted oncology therapies, meaning many oncology programs now face pediatric obligations much earlier than historically anticipated. 50 PREA and RACE impose mandatory requirements but provide no exclusivity incentives. 48 In contrast, the Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act (BPCA) provides a voluntary pathway in which sponsors may obtain six months of pediatric exclusivity by conducting FDA‐requested studies. Historically, the FDA sometimes issued BPCA Written Requests (WRs) for studies that were also required under PREA, allowing sponsors to receive exclusivity for fulfilling mandatory pediatric obligations. However, current FDA guidance clarifies that the Agency no longer expects to issue WRs solely for studies or planned studies required under PREA. In oncology, this shift is reinforced by the RACE for Children Act, under which molecularly targeted pediatric studies are now mandated under PREA and constitute the primary driver of pediatric oncology drug development. 49 , 52 In addition to de novo development under PREA, RACE, and BPCA, lifecycle strategies such as the 505(b)(2) and 505(j) pathways—which enable the development of modified versions of approved drugs and generic medicines, respectively—can support the adaptation or repurposing of existing oncology therapies, including new formulations or dosing approaches, to expand availability of treatments in pediatric populations.

Regulatory Requirement › European Union: Paediatric Regulation and the PIP Framework

The EU Paediatric Regulation requires agreement on a pediatric investigation plan (PIP) with EMA's Paediatric Committee (PDCO) no later than completion of adult PK studies. 53 The PIP establishes binding pediatric measures, study designs, and timelines, including requested waivers and deferrals. Compliance is verified at marketing authorization and when sponsors seek new indications or line extensions. 53 , 54 PDCO may grant product‐specific or class waivers where the relevant disease does not occur in children or where evidence indicates the product would not be safe, effective, or provide meaningful benefit in children. 55 Deferrals allow pediatric studies to be initiated or completed after submission of the Marketing Authorization Application, but require clear deadlines and formal modification for any subsequent changes. 53 , 54 Unlike the US target‐based RACE framework, EU pediatric requirements are condition‐based. PDCO uses a structured hierarchy to define the “condition”, which may be broader or narrower than the proposed adult indication, depending on pediatric medical need. 53 , 55 Completion of all agreed PIP measures can yield substantial incentives: a 6‐month Supplementary Protection Certificate (SPC) extension for non‐orphan medicines and two additional years of orphan market exclusivity for orphan products. 53 EMA has also formalized the stepwise PIP (sPIP) approach for exceptional cases involving significant scientific uncertainty. An sPIP allows agreement on minimum core components of the plan while deferring detailed pediatric elements to predefined milestones. 56 Lastly, the EU framework includes the Paediatric Use Marketing Authorisation (PUMA), which provides 10 years of data and market protection for off‑patent medicines developed exclusively for pediatric use, thereby supporting the repurposing of established therapies to expand availability for pediatric populations.

Regulatory Requirement › Global Challenges and Strategies for Harmonization

Differences between US and EU frameworks (Table 2) create challenges for designing coherent global pediatric oncology strategies. US obligations are triggered by biological target relevance, whereas EU obligations depend on potential pediatric use within a defined condition. 50 , 53 , 55 These discrepancies are particularly complex for rare molecular alterations, biomarker‐defined populations, and combination regimens. Given small and internationally dispersed pediatric oncology populations, early and coordinated regulatory engagement is essential. In the United States, sponsors may seek advice through established FDA meeting frameworks and oncology‐specific interactions via the Oncology Center of Excellence. 57 , 58 In the EU, while the PIP procedure is the definitive mechanism for determining pediatric requirements, EMA Scientific Advice can be used to discuss methodological issues such as extrapolation, formulation development, and study design. 59 , 60 Such advice complements but does not replace PDCO decision‐making. 53 , 54 , 60 TransAtlantic collaboration mechanisms play an important role. The FDA–EMA pediatric cluster is a regulator‐only forum where agencies exchange views on topics such as extrapolation, target relevance, and justifications for waivers and deferrals; sponsors can be informed of the outcome of these cluster meetings via the common commentary process. 61 Parallel Scientific Advice (PSA) enables sponsors to obtain coordinated input from the FDA and EMA on specific scientific questions and is particularly valuable for pediatric oncology programs involving complex endpoints, dose optimization, extrapolation strategies, or sequencing of pediatric versus adult studies. 62 Outside the United States and the EU, most jurisdictions do not impose formal pediatric requirements. For example, in Japan, recent amendments to the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Act and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) notifications require sponsors developing adult oncology drugs to explicitly consider and document pediatric development plans during clinical trial consultations, reflecting an emerging expectation for early lifecycle pediatric planning despite the absence of binding approval conditions. Chinese health authorities encourage early consideration of pediatric development and have introduced several incentive‑based policies to support this area. 63 Great Britain operates a national GB PIP procedure similar to the EU system, while other regions generally rely on scientific advice and case‐by‐case determinations. 64 Key Differences Between the US and EU Regulatory Framework in Pediatric Drug Development

Nonclinical Evidence Generation

Animal models continue to play an important but increasingly contextualized role in preclinical pediatric oncology drug development, supporting investigation of tumor biology, evaluation of antitumor activity, and characterization of safety prior to clinical testing. Their use is expected to be scientifically justified and aligned with pediatric‐specific disease biology, and incorporated within a broader, fit‐for‐purpose evidence strategy rather than relying on adult cancer paradigms. In this context, regulatory agencies emphasize that adequate proof‐of‐concept data—derived from integrated nonclinical and, where appropriate, clinical evidence—is required to justify initiation of pediatric oncology trials. 65 The FDA's pediatric oncology framework encourages early integration of pediatric considerations into development programs and prioritizes generation of mechanistically relevant and clinically translatable nonclinical data. 51 , 66 , 67 Recent efforts by the FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) to modernize nonclinical testing—particularly through adoption of new approach methodologies (NAMs) and the 3Rs principles—replacement, reduction, and refinement—have further refined expectations for evidence generation. 68 Nonclinical packages are thus increasingly context‐of‐use driven, incorporating both conventional models and NAMs within a weight‐of‐evidence framework to support regulatory decision‐making. In many cases, pediatric clinical studies may be supported by adult clinical data combined with targeted nonclinical studies in relevant models, particularly for therapies for advanced cancers, life‐threatening hematologic disorders, 69 or well‐characterized mechanisms. 70 , 71 Within this paradigm, juvenile animal studies (JAS) are no longer considered routine. Instead, their use is hypothesis‐driven and limited to scenarios where a drug may affect developmental processes. When conducted, JAS should address specific mechanistic questions related to postnatal development and inform pediatric dose selection and long‐term safety monitoring. 67 A systematic analysis of 74 FDA‐approved pediatric products found that JAS findings resulted in direct changes to product labeling in only a small proportion of cases (8%), supporting a more targeted, question‐driven application rather than routine use. 72 Although no specific model is mandated, sponsors are expected to justify the biological relevance and translational value of chosen systems, align endpoints with pediatric disease mechanisms, and incorporate model‐informed drug development (MIDD) approaches where appropriate. Increasingly, this includes integration of NAMs—such as human‐relevant in vitro systems, in silico modeling, and functional assays—to improve prediction of pharmacology and safety. Initiatives such as the Pediatric High‐Risk Cancer Preclinical Model Resource further support access to clinically relevant models and translational research. 73 Collectively, these strategies enable nonclinical data to be evaluated based on their capacity to inform pediatric dose selection, characterize risk, and support extrapolation under PREA and related frameworks. NAMs are playing an increasingly central role by providing human‐relevant data that complement, and in some cases reduce reliance on, traditional animal studies. 74 Consistent with the FDA's roadmap to modernize nonclinical testing, NAMs are being applied across pharmacology and safety assessments through integrated, fit‐for‐purpose approaches. Technologies such as organoids, organ‐on‐chip platforms, and computational modeling allow mechanistic interrogation of drug effects while improving translational relevance. 75 , 76 In pediatric oncology, where limited patient populations, ethical constraints, and variable predictive value of animal models present challenges, these approaches offer advantages by enabling high‐content testing with minimal patient‐derived material. Together, the integration of NAMs with conventional models supports a more predictive, efficient, and ethically aligned nonclinical framework for pediatric oncology drug development.

Clinical Evidence Generation

Beyond the regulatory expectations for pediatric drug development outlined in the Regulatory Requirement section, the FDA and the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) provide guidance on practical approaches for generating clinical evidence to support pediatric drug approval (Figure 3). In particular, ICH E11A Pediatric Extrapolation clarifies the scientific principles and framework for extrapolating data from a reference population, commonly adults, to a pediatric target population. 77 Central to this framework is a systematic evaluation of similarities and differences in disease biology, drug pharmacology, and expected response to treatment. Within this extrapolation framework, the regulator recognizes a continuum of development approaches tailored to the level of residual uncertainty. Exposure matching is appropriate when the disease course and mechanism of action are sufficiently similar between pediatric and adult populations, and when clinical benefit is primarily driven by systemic exposure such that achieving adult‑like exposure in pediatric patients is expected to confer comparable efficacy. Enrollment of adolescents in adult oncology trials is also encouraged when cancers that are biologically similar to adult malignancies or share the same molecular target and body size–adjusted PK is expected to be similar to adults, enabling earlier access and generation of PK and safety data within adult programs. For younger pediatric populations, where maturational changes in absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) may meaningfully impact exposure, population PK (PopPK) and physiologically based PK (PBPK) modeling are particularly appropriate to inform initial dose selection, account for developmental physiology, and support dose refinement prior to or in conjunction with limited pediatric PK data collection. (See Clinical Study Design and Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies sections for examples of each approach.) When exposure matching alone does not adequately support extrapolation, PK/PD‑based approaches using biologically relevant biomarkers can confirm pharmacologic activity and guide pediatric dose selection. In contrast, when disease course, response, or endpoint relevance differs between adults and children, pediatric efficacy and safety studies are needed, often using single‑arm or externally controlled designs because randomized controlled trials may not be feasible due to rarity or ethical concerns. For ultra‑rare pediatric malignancies, the FDA may also accept external controls or real‑world data (RWD) to contextualize treatment effects when randomized trials are not feasible. Ethical and Operational Considerations of Clinical Studies

Pediatric oncology research poses unique ethical challenges because the affected population is small, vulnerable, and faces life‐threatening illnesses with limited alternatives. Clinical pharmacology decisions—dose selection, sampling intensity, extrapolation, and study design—are inseparable from these ethical considerations. Because most pediatric tumors are rare and geographically dispersed, traditional development pathways are often infeasible. Seamless or platform designs can reduce operational burden by consolidating infrastructure, sharing control arms, and enabling response‐adaptive decisions (see Clinical Study Design section). 78 Avoiding unnecessary pediatric trials is an important ethical topic. The 2022 Pediatric Oncology Subcommittee of the Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) underscored the limited value and potential harm of duplicative trials with “next‐in‐class” agents when class pharmacology, exposure–response, and safety are already well characterized, and no plausible pediatric differentiation is expected. In such cases, selective waivers or streamlined evaluations may be warranted. Conversely, sponsors should demonstrate clear biological or clinical differentiation to justify additional pediatric exposure. These positions argue for prioritizing pediatric studies with the highest probability of benefit and the lowest risk of redundancy. These ethical considerations closely mirror the scientific and ethical criteria applied by regulators when evaluating requests for pediatric waivers, deferrals, or streamlined development programs under PREA and the EU Paediatric Regulation. Dose‐finding and combinations also raise specific ethical tensions. Beginning with monotherapy may characterize first‐in‐kid PK/PD and toxicity attribution but can expose kids to suboptimal antitumor activity in life‐threatening settings. Starting directly with standard of care (SOC) combinations better reflects real‐world practice but complicates causality for adverse events and intensifies drug–drug interaction risks. MIDD—PopPK, PBPK, and exposure–response modeling—can ethically de‐risk both strategies by supporting biologically plausible starting doses, anticipating drug–drug interactions (DDIs), and shortening periods of non‐therapeutic exposure. 79 Limited blood volumes in infants and young children pose procedural burden, mandating consideration of sparse sampling, microsampling (e.g., capillary or dried blood spot through devices like the Tasso OnDemand kit for pediatric or Neoteryx Mitra blood collection kit), and high‐sensitivity assays. Under such constraints, model‐based approaches generally outperform noncompartmental analysis for PK parameter precision, enabling optimal sampling windows through D‐optimality approaches, 80 , 81 smaller cohorts, and principled borrowing across age strata. Prudent extrapolation is central to ethical study design. When pediatric tumors diverge biologically from adult disease, adult data should inform rather than dictate dose or benefit–risk assessments. On the other hand, for molecularly defined, tissue‐agnostic targets with comparable biology across ages, exposure matching can be sufficient to select pediatric doses and support labeling. Finally, the timing of pediatric initiation must weigh early access against uncertainty in developmental safety. Juvenile animal findings can inform monitoring plans, but their translational precision is limited. Regulators encourage integrating nonclinical signals with human exposure margins, PBPK modeling considering ontogeny, and long‐term follow‐up, rather than relying on animal data alone. 79

Clinical Study Design

Clinical Study Design › Review of Oncology Drugs with Approval in Pediatric Populations

To reflect recent clinical study considerations, this review focused on oncology drugs approved by the FDA in pediatric populations between January 2018 and September 2025. During this period, a total of 43 unique oncology drugs were approved, regardless of the number of indications each drug was approved for. For drugs within the same pharmacologic class and with the same pediatric indications, the drug with a wider age approval range or the drug with an earlier approval date was included. As such, 33 drugs were included (Table 1), and 10 drugs (asparaginase Erwinia chrysanthemi recombinant, mirdametinib, bosutinib, dasatinib, entrectinib, pralsetinib, repotrectinib, selumetinib, tovorafenib, and trametinib) were excluded in this review. Understanding why oncology drugs approved in adult populations fail to demonstrate efficacy and/or safety in pediatric populations can be instructive. A recent review on drugs approved by the FDA between 2015 and 2022 in non‐oncology therapeutic areas in adult populations identified that 15% (32 in 211) of drugs did not receive approval in pediatric populations despite pediatric trials being conducted. 82 Among those 32 drugs, more than half (56%) failed to demonstrate effectiveness, 22% did not demonstrate sufficient safety, and 22% had both effectiveness and safety insufficiency. An inadequate study design, such as insufficient control of confounding variables, inappropriate dose and study endpoint selection, lack of a control arm, or small sample size, was the common reason for failed pediatric trials. A further evaluation of the failure rate and reasons associated with the failed pediatric trials in oncology between 2018 and 2025 is out of scope for this review. Between 2018 and 2025 (Table 1), the average interval between adult and pediatric indication approval was 5.2 years. The longest delay was observed for rituximab, with adult and pediatric approvals occurring in 1997 and 2021, respectively. Notably, 15 drugs received adult and pediatric indications concurrently at the time of NDA or BLA approval, reflecting increasing integration of pediatric development into earlier stages of oncology programs.

Clinical Study Design › Dose‐Finding Studies

Dose‐finding Phase 1 oncology studies, in both pediatric and adult populations, typically include a dose‐escalation phase followed by a dose‐expansion phase. The primary objective of dose‐escalation is to systematically characterize safety, tolerability, and PK across small, escalating dose cohorts. The subsequent dose‐expansion phase aims to refine the dosing schedule and further define toxicity and PK profiles, while providing preliminary evidence of antitumor activity to inform later‐phase development. Often in pediatric Phase 1 studies, dose escalation may be substantially streamlined when exposure–response relationships can be extrapolated from adult data and when disease biology and clinical course are expected to be similar between pediatric and adult populations (Figure 3). Under these circumstances, an initial cohort focused on PK assessment can be sufficient to identify a pediatric dose that achieves adult‐comparable therapeutic exposure, followed by a single expansion cohort without the need to explore multiple dose levels. Furthermore, modeling and simulation to match PK in adolescent patients to adults in the absence of clinical data in adolescents of the same indication have led to approval for several drugs in this age group, including nivolumab‐relatlimab FDC, vorasidenib, and cabozantinib due to difficulty of enrollment in pediatric populations. 13 , 20 , 21 Such exposure‐driven pediatric trial designs align with regulatory expectations under PREA, RACE, and PIP frameworks, particularly when pediatric development is initiated early and traditional sequential adult–then–pediatric development pathways are no longer feasible. When disease similarity or exposure–response comparability between adults and children cannot be reasonably assumed, or when therapies are specific to pediatric populations, more comprehensive PK characterization and dose‑finding programs are required prior to confirmatory safety and efficacy studies. Traditional rule‐based 3+3 design is still commonly used for dose escalation (Table 3) due to its simplicity and conservative safety profiles, as it does not rely on the assumption of a dose‐toxicity relationship. Because pediatric phase 1 oncology studies are frequently initiated after the adult recommended Phase 2 dose (RP2D) has been established, pediatric dose exploration is usually less extensive compared to adults, with the majority of two or three dose levels (Table 3). To mitigate the prolonged escalation timelines and potential exposure to subtherapeutic doses inherent to traditional 3+3 design, alternative rule‐based approaches such as accelerated titration 128 or rolling 6 129 designs have been increasingly adopted. Among 27 pediatric Phase 1 or Phase 1/2 dose‐finding studies reviewed (Table 3), 13 employed a 3+3 design, 8 used a rolling 6 design, 4 studies evaluated a single dose level (atezolizumab, daunorubicin and cytarabine liposome, granule formulation of selumetinib, and nilotinib), and 1 study (calaspargase pegol) tested two pre‐specified dose levels. In most 3+3 studies, pediatric starting doses were set at one level below the adult RP2D or approximately 80% of the adult dose, whereas some studies, such as inotuzumab ozogamicin in Japanese pediatric, pembrolizumab, dordaviprone, and selpercatinib, initiated at the adult RP2D or approved dose using allometric scaling. A similar pattern was observed for rolling 6 designs, with most studies starting at adult‐equivalent doses adjusted for body size (brentuximab vedotin in Japanese pediatric patients, blinatumomab, revumenib, nivolumab, larotrectinib, and selpercatinib), and two studies (inotuzumab ozogamicin and crizotinib) started at one level below adult dosing, while the other six started at adult RP2D or approved dose with allometric scaling. Ten drugs were approved without a dedicated pediatric dosing‐finding study, and the majority of these approvals were limited to adolescents, with only one drug in children, three in infants, and one in neonates. Bayesian designs like the continual reassessment method (CRM) or adaptive logistic design have been proposed. In recent years, model‐based dose escalation designs, including modified toxicity probability interval (mTPI), 130 Bayesian Optimal Interval Design (BOIN), 131 and new ruled‐based designs such as interval 3+3 (i3+3) 132 and backfill i3+3 (Bi3+3), 133 have been widely used in adult FIH trials. Compared with traditional 3+3 designs, these new designs have a higher likelihood of identifying true maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and assigning fewer participants to toxic dose levels. In the Phase 1 dose‐finding and dose‐confirmation study of pembrolizumab in pediatric patients (KEYNOTE‐051), the mTPI study design was used to determine the pediatric RP2D (Table 3). This design allows flexible dose de‐escalation from the initial dose of 2 mg/kg Q3W to a minimum of 1 mg/kg Q3W based on the proportion of patients with dose‐limiting toxicities such that the target probability of toxicity is around 25% for the MTD. 86 With BOIN receiving FDA's designation for dose‐finding trials, 134 we expect to see more implementation of these new designs in pediatric dose escalation trials. Given the small sample size of the majority of pediatric oncology studies and the frequent inclusion of heterogeneous tumor types and age ranges, master protocols—particularly basket and umbrella trials—have gained popularity in recent years. Basket trials are particularly attractive for targeted therapies, as they evaluate treatments across multiple tumor types defined by shared molecular alterations or biomarkers rather than histology or anatomic location. 135 Selpercatinib, a selective RET inhibitor, was evaluated in LIBRETTO‐001 basket study enrolling patients with RET fusion‐positive or RET‐mutant tumors across multiple histologies, supporting its tumor‐agnostic approval (Tables 1 and 3). 98 , 99 , 136 Similarly, larotrectinib, a tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitor, was developed through integrated adult (LOXO‐TRK‐14001 [NCT02122913]) and pediatric (LOXO‐TRK‐15003 [NCT02637687]) basket trials with seamless dose‐escalation, expansion, and Phase 2 evaluation. Within this platform, body surface area (BSA)‐based dosing and multiple formulations (capsule and liquid) were evaluated, and PK modeling enabled exposure matching across age from 1 month to 18 years. 44 By sharing infrastructure and enabling information flow across cohorts, such platform designs conserve scarce pediatric resources and speed up development, provided that tumor selection and combination strategies are carefully prespecified. Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies › Approval Experience

Dose selection in pediatric oncology is subject to heightened regulatory scrutiny because it frequently constitutes the primary evidentiary basis for extrapolation decisions and pediatric labeling under PREA, RACE, and the EU Paediatric Regulation. Pediatric dosing strategies must therefore balance between efficacy and safety while accounting for developmental changes in physiology across age groups. Among the 33 drugs reviewed (Table 1), heterogeneity in pediatric dosing approaches was observed. Eleven drugs were approved for adolescents (≥12 years) with dosing aligned to adult regimens. Several adolescent approvals used fixed adult dosing, including nivolumab‐relatlimab FDC (Opdualag), pembrolizumab subcutaneous formulation (Pembrolizumab Qlex), lutetium Lu‑177 dotatate, and tazemetostat. Others apply body weight (BW) thresholds, most commonly 40 kg, to determine whether adolescents receive adult fixed dosing (≥40 kg) or BW‐based dosing (<40 kg). Pembrolizumab exemplifies a mixed strategy with BW‐based dosing in adolescents (capped at the adult fixed dose) and fixed dosing in adults, whereas ipilimumab remains universally weight‑based. Four drugs approved for children aged ≥2 years retained adult‑consistent BW‐based dosing—brentuximab vedotin and atezolizumab (capped at adult doses), tagraxofusp‑erzs (without a cap), and selpercatinib, which uses a 50‐kg cutoff for adolescent tiered dosing and BSA‑based dosing in younger children (with a minimum BSA threshold). Seventeen drugs were approved for infants and older populations (≥1 year for most; rituximab down to 6 months), of which nine used BSA‑based dosing. Several drugs employed mixed strategies contingent on age, BW, or BSA cutoffs, including gemtuzumab ozogamicin (0.6‐m2 BSA cutoff with multiple BSA tiers), blinatumomab (45‐kg cutoff for fixed vs BSA‑based), azacitidine (age‑ and BW‐dependent transitions between BW and BSA dosing), revumenib (40‐kg cutoff), and Larotrectinib (1.0‐m2 BSA cutoff). Additional agents—dordaviprone, cabozantinib, and dabrafenib—used weight‑tiered regimens. Emapalumab, the only therapy approved from the neonatal period onward, employs BW‐based dosing.

Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies › Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

When disease biology and pharmacologic response are considered similar across age groups, exposure‐matching is the primary rationale for pediatric dose selection (Figure 3). PopPK models incorporating allometric scaling are commonly used for adolescents and older pediatric patients to adjust for body size and composition. Selpercatinib provides a representative example. PopPK modeling across about 500 participants demonstrated that adolescents weighing <50 kg would experience higher exposures at the adult dose (160 mg twice daily [BID]). A reduced dose of 120 mg BID achieved adult‐comparable AUC and Cmax, with BW identified as a primary covariate on PK; sex, race, and age did not meaningfully affect exposure. Exposure–QTc modeling further predicted that the higher exposures in lower‐weight, for example, <50 kg adolescents could result in mean QTc prolongation exceeding 20 ms, supporting selection of the lower 120 mg BID dose. PK and safety data from adolescents (15‐17 years) enrolled in LIBRETTO001 were consistent with those of adults, reinforcing this exposure‐driven extrapolation approach in RET‐driven thyroid cancer and MTC. Other use cases of PopPK to support pediatric dosing include calaspargase pegol‐mknl and asparaginase Erwinia chrysanthemi in ALL. 137 An exposure‐matching approach was utilized to support the approval of nivolumab‐relatlimab FDC in adolescent patients with melanoma. Modeling and simulation predicted adolescent exposures relative to the adult efficacious targets, enabling efficacy extrapolation in pediatric subjects ≥12 years and ≥40 kg, where disease biology and expected exposure–response relationships for immune‑oncology therapy are generally consistent with adults. In addition, safety data from Studies CA224047 and CA224020 showed comparable profiles across body weight (<50 vs ≥50 kg) and younger or older adults (aged 18‐30 vs ≥30 years). These findings support exposure‑matching and PopPK‑based justification of adolescent dosing for nivolumab‐relatlimab FDC when disease characteristics and exposure–response relationships are similar to adults. Therapeutic mAbs have demonstrated great success in numerous adult cancers. Although historically pediatric mAb PK differences were attributed primarily to body size, recent analyses indicate that tumor type and age may also influence clearance. 138 A PopPK analysis of nivolumab and ipilimumab demonstrated that, after adjusting for body size, pediatric patients aged 1‐17 years with solid tumors or central nervous system tumors had lower clearance than adults, while clearance in pediatric lymphoma patients was similar. The identified tumor type–specific, age‐related PK differences in pediatric patients are important considerations for optimizing dosing of mAbs and biologics in children.

Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies › Mechanistic Modeling Approaches

Mechanistic modeling approaches, such as PBPK and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP), are particularly valuable for dose selection in younger children and infants, in whom rapid developmental changes in organ function, enzyme maturation, and body composition substantially affect drug ADME. By incorporating age‑dependent maturation of enzymes and transporters, disease‑specific factors, and developmental pharmacology, PBPK models support rational dose selection in the youngest pediatric populations. 139 Barrett et al similarly emphasized PBPK as a mechanistic, bottom‑up approach that overcomes the limitations of empirical dose adjustments and enables extrapolation to neonates and infants. 140 The authors outlined a workflow for pediatric PBPK applications, including dose selection and trial design evaluation, while noting varied confidence levels in PBPK modeling. Subsequent evaluations further underscore PBPK's utility and areas for refinement. 27 , 28 Zhou et al. demonstrated that PBPK models accurately predicted pediatric drug concentration–time profiles for most drugs studied, with 58 of 67 predictions within twofold and 43 within 1.5‑fold of observed values—particularly for drugs primarily metabolized by CYP enzymes in children aged 1 month and older. 141 In contrast, Leong et al, in reviewing PBPK models submitted to the FDA (midazolam, theophylline, and omeprazole), found that they generally underestimated clearance in neonates, infants, and young children, 142 highlighting the need for improved model specification and verification frameworks. PBPK modeling has been increasingly accepted by regulators for the prediction of pediatric DDI when clinical studies are not feasible. DDI risk in children differs from adults due to the ontogeny of CYP enzymes and transporters. By integrating in vitro and clinical data in adults with age‐dependent physiology (e.g., enzyme maturation, organ size, plasma proteins, and renal function), PBPK informs dose adjustments in pediatric patients and supports regulatory submissions, as illustrated by dordaviprone, for which PBPK was used to predict interactions with CYP3A modulators and transporter substrates. 37 , 143

Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies › Integrated and Emerging Modeling Approaches

Modeling approaches are complementary rather than exclusive. Integration of PBPK, popPK, and optimal design strategies has been shown to improve dose selection and study efficiency in pediatric oncology. 144 For example, combined modeling approaches accurately predicted pediatric docetaxel exposure and informed PK bridging strategies. More recently, revumenib applied an integrated MIDD framework: PopPK showed exposure similarity between adults and pediatric patients using BSA‐adjusted doses; PBPK confirmed exposure alignment across children aged 1‐17 years and with adults; and exposure–response analyses revealed plateaued efficacy with increased safety risks at higher exposures, underscoring the importance of precise pediatric dose selection. 39 Regulatory guidance also supports Bayesian approaches to borrow adult information when extrapolation is justified. Although broader application in pediatric oncology is still emerging, proof‐of‐concept examples across therapeutic areas illustrate how Bayesian and QSP frameworks can enhance the robustness of pediatric bridging strategy and dose selection, with continued refinement needed to support routine decision‐making. 134 , 145

Model‐Informed Dosing Strategies › When Adult Exposure Matching Is Not Appropriate for Pediatric Dosing

Although exposure‐matching has been a cornerstone to select pediatric dose, particularly for small molecules, there are scenarios where extrapolation may fail or be insufficient. For example, for drugs, largely mAbs, exhibiting target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD), integration of PK with PD and target‐occupancy considerations are often necessary to optimize pediatric dosing because changes in PK parameters are less than body weight‐proportional, potentially stemming from different target expressions between adults and children. 146 Simulations demonstrated that different dosing strategies may be needed depending on how to saturate the target expression—BW‐based dosing is preferable for achieving consistent target concentrations, while fixed dosing may be more appropriate when targeting the same absolute amount. 146 Another example where extrapolation may not be sufficient is the development of chimeric antigen receptor T‑cell (CAR‑T) therapy in children. Unlike small molecules or biologics, CAR‐T dosing in pediatric patients is not based on achieving exposure comparable to that of adults. Although dosing is generally scaled by body weight, the effective exposure is determined by in vivo proliferation and persistence of the cells, which are influenced by disease burden, lymphodepletion, and CAR design rather than the administered dose itself. 147 Furthermore, when a drug has a narrow therapeutic range with a large interindividual PK variability, simply relying on adult exposure‐matching may be insufficient and requires an individualized dosing approach. Alemtuzumab is an anti‐CD52 mAb that is widely used as part of conditioning regimens to induce profound immunosuppression. The pediatric study illustrates that standard weight‐based dosing in pediatric patients does not result in consistent systemic exposure, with substantial interindividual variability driven by factors such as body weight and baseline lymphocyte count. 148 Moreover, clinical outcomes were strongly associated with exposure rather than administered dose, indicating that achieving a predefined exposure—such as one derived from adults—may not ensure optimal benefit–risk in pediatric populations. Instead, the authors developed a PopPK model for individualized precision dosing in pediatric allogeneic HSCT for nonmalignant diseases. 148 Other scenarios where adult exposure‐matching may fail or be insufficient include when children have cancer types that differ from those typically seen in adults, when pediatric tumor biology differs from adult disease, when the pediatric indication involves ongoing, simultaneous treatments, when evaluating anti‐cancer agents by adding the treatment to existing regimens, or when long‐term safety issues are not captured by short‐term PK equivalence. In these situations, additional efficacy and safety studies in pediatric populations will be needed to elucidate PK and response to treatment (Figure 3). Mechanistic modeling approaches with consideration of a broader set of criteria, including factors unique to pediatric physiology, disease characteristics, and potential interactions with other chronic therapies, could be considered to aid starting dose selection in pediatric populations.

Biopharmaceutical and Formulation Challenges

As outlined in earlier sections on regulatory expectations and the role of clinical pharmacology in pediatric oncology drug development, biopharmaceutical and formulation considerations are equally critical for enabling safe and effective pediatric dosing. Age‑appropriate formulations frequently become binding elements within agreed pediatric development plans, influencing development timelines, post‑marketing commitments, and ultimately pediatric labeling. Both the FDA PREA guidance 66 and ICH E11(R1) 149 emphasize the need for appropriate pediatric formulations, scientifically justified extrapolation strategies, and careful assessment of excipients and dosing approaches in children. Common challenges and examples relevant to pediatric oncology oral formulation are summarized in Table 4. Young children, especially neonates and infants, exhibit distinct gastrointestinal (GI) physiology that affects drug dissolution, gastric pH, gastric emptying, activity of bile and pancreatic fluid, enzyme maturation, and intestinal permeability. 150 These developmental differences make age‐appropriate formulations essential. FDA and ICH guidance highlight considerations such as swallowability, palatability, manipulation feasibility, and dose flexibility. Despite clear regulatory expectations and financial incentives such as pediatric exclusivity, the development and sustained availability of age‐appropriate formulations remain limited. Quantitative evidence shows that fewer than half of pediatric drug development programs are supported by age‐appropriate formulations, with only 42.4% of studies using an age‐appropriate formulation and 56.9% relying exclusively on adult solid dosage forms, even in incentivized settings. 159 This reliance persists in younger populations where formulation needs are greatest, including exclusive use of adult formulations in 26.3% of studies in children under 2 years and 44.9% in those aged 2‐5 years. Consistent with these findings, broader analyses indicate that only approximately half of drugs have an age‐appropriate formulation available at the time of labeling, highlighting a persistent gap between regulatory intent, incentive structures, and real‐world implementation. 159 Bridging strategies are often required when pediatric‐specific formulations differ from adult formulations. For example, trametinib included a pediatric oral solution with relative bioavailability (rBA) evaluated in adults to bridge systemic exposure between formulations. Stability is particularly important for liquid formulations, which may degrade more rapidly than solids. In addition, excipients tolerated in adults (e.g., benzyl alcohol, propylene glycol, sorbitol, and ethanol) may pose age‐specific safety risks, necessitating reformulation or reduced concentrations. From a clinical pharmacology perspective, rBA studies are typically required when formulations differ, accounting for age‐appropriate physiology, dose proportionality, and administration practices (e.g., tablet dispersion and capsule opening). Children have different feeding patterns and meal compositions relative to adults, which can influence drug absorption. Regulatory guidance therefore recommends evaluating food effects when dietary patterns differ in ways that may alter exposure, particularly in children <6 years of age, where GI physiology and diet differ substantially from adults. 160 Standard adult high‐fat meals are often inappropriate for children; age‐appropriate meal compositions should inform pediatric assessments. Modeling approaches, including PBPK, may reduce the need for dedicated pediatric food–effect studies but remain limited by uncertainty in younger populations. 161 The PBPK model developed for entrectinib, a kinase inhibitor approved in pediatric patients older than 1 month of age with NTRK gene fusion‐positive solid tumors, illustrated the challenges. 162 The PBPK incorporated a mechanistic oral absorption model and was able to capture the observed PK in pediatric age between 4‐ and 20‐year‐old by accounting for the solubility changes due to the GI bile salt. 163 However, the model overestimated the exposure in patients less than 4 years old, potentially because of the larger uncertainty in the absorption process and CYP3A ontogeny. 162 Although oral administration is generally preferred in pediatric populations due to convenience and its noninvasive nature, many oncology drugs such as mAbs, ADCs, and cytotoxic chemotherapies are given intravenously. In these cases, adult drug concentrations are typically appropriate for pediatric patients, and additional formulation development may not be required. However, given the frequent use of body‐size‐based dosing, careful consideration should be given to dose‐appropriate packaging, including single‐use vials optimized for pediatric dosing to minimize drug wastage and support accurate administration. Novel Approaches and Data Types

The increasing regulatory acceptance of real‐world data, external controls, and adaptive evidence generation reflects pragmatic responses by health authorities to the constraints imposed by mandatory pediatric development requirements in rare oncology populations. The rarity, heterogeneity, and geographic dispersion of pediatric tumors demand approaches that maximize information generation per participant while minimizing burden. Consequently, an expanding methodological toolkit—including RWD, synthetic and external control arms, MIDD, analytic advances in PK, and emerging applications of artificial intelligence (AI)—is rapidly reshaping feasibility and evidentiary expectations in pediatric oncology. Regulators have explicitly supported innovative trial designs, such as basket/umbrella/platform approaches and adaptive designs, to make rare‐cancer programs feasible while preserving inference quality. When randomized trials are impractical, rigorously curated external comparators can contextualize outcomes from single‐arm pediatric studies, particularly when methods such as propensity matching and prespecified sensitivity analyses are applied. 164 Recent pediatric approvals illustrate both the potential and limitations of these approaches. In the development of eflornithine for high‐risk neuroblastoma, the multicenter NMTRC003b Phase 2 trial relied on a propensity‐matched external comparator derived from the ANBL0032 dataset to provide primary efficacy evidence, demonstrating how external controls can inform regulatory decisions when randomization is impractical. 38 For larotrectinib, RWD comparisons in TRK‐fusion cancers consistently showed improved survival outcomes relative to SOC, providing evidence that complemented its seamless, tissue‐agnostic basket‐trial framework. 44 More recently, the approval of caplacizumab‐yhdp in pediatric patients 12 years of age and older with a rare, acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was based on an observational, retrospective chart review study with PK/PD simulation demonstrating the caplacizumab‐yhdp exposure and suppression of von Willebrand factor antigen in pediatric patients were similar to adults at the approved dose. 165 These examples demonstrate how the novel approaches can accelerate drug approval in pediatric patients when the disease is rare. Regulators support strategies to accelerate pediatric evidence generation through parallel enrollment of adolescents in adult trials, once initial adult safety, PK, and exposure–response data are available. This strategy leverages biological and developmental continuity between adolescents and adults and is supported by regulators when justified by MIDD and disease similarity. 79 Parallel enrollment reduced redundant pediatric dose‐escalation studies and accelerated access to investigational agents in high‐need indications. Advances in AI/machine learning (ML) tools offer additional opportunities to streamline pediatric oncology development, and the FDA has encouraged these innovations to promote drug development. 166 Several AI/ML applications have been successfully utilized to facilitate data analysis for NDA or BLA, for example, outcome prediction, covariate selection/confounding adjustment, anomaly detection, and RWD phenotyping. 167 In pediatric oncology, AI/ML has mainly been used to perform risk stratification, predict treatment response, and optimize dose. 168 , 169 Although at its nascent stage, AI‐enabled medical devices developed for pediatric populations are expected to increase, and these devices can improve disease diagnosis or serve as a clinical decision support tool to improve productivity and confidence of healthcare providers. 170 Other potential applications of AI/ML include enhanced site‐ and patient‐finding for ultra‐rare molecular subsets, automating eligibility screening using structured and unstructured clinical data, and improved tumor‐response assessment through ML‐assisted imaging. AI/ML can also support adaptive MIDD frameworks by refining exposure predictions and dose selection as data accumulate, although transparent validation and regulatory oversight remain essential. Finally, close collaboration with regulators facilitates fit‐for‐purpose study designs (e.g., seamless or platform architectures) and shared control arms. Recent FDA guidance outlines acceptable approaches for decentralized clinical trials, including tele‐consent, home‐health visits, and remote procedures, which may reduce geographic barriers and improve equity in trial access. Complementary guidance on digital health technologies enables the use of validated remote measurements for activity, sleep, neurological function, or symptom burden that are particularly relevant to pediatric oncology patients. 171 Together, these regulatory‐enabled innovations support more efficient, patient‐centered pediatric oncology drug development while preserving scientific rigor.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Future directions in pediatric oncology drug development increasingly focus on accelerating access to targeted therapies through molecular profiling, transitioning from histology‑driven strategies to tissue‑agnostic approaches, and leveraging advanced modeling to optimize dosing. Key priorities include the rapid advancement of immunotherapies, expansion of precision‑medicine trials, and strengthened global collaboration to address rare and high‑risk pediatric cancers. Drug development is progressively guided by shared oncogenic driver mutations across pediatric malignancies rather than by the organ of origin alone, enabling broader application of targeted therapies. These efforts extend beyond kinase inhibition to include emerging targets such as transcription factors and epigenetic regulators. Precision‑medicine trials, including initiatives such as Pediatric MATCH, 172 play a central role in matching targeted therapies to specific genomic alterations in rare tumors while minimizing the number of patients required for clinical evaluation. Continued innovation in cellular therapies, particularly CAR‑T therapies, remains a major focus, alongside the growing potential of mRNA‑based vaccines and gene therapies for the treatment of refractory childhood cancers. Given the rarity of pediatric cancers, future progress will depend heavily on sustained global partnerships among regulatory agencies, industry, and academic consortia, such as the Children's Oncology Group, to enable efficient, large‑scale, and collaborative clinical studies. Innovative clinical trial designs, including master protocols, model‑based dose escalation, and the use of single‑arm or externally controlled studies, have been successfully implemented to reduce development timelines, costs, and patient burden. The increasing adoption of MIDD approaches has further accelerated dose selection, dose justification, and regulatory decision‑making in pediatric oncology. Despite these advances, mortality rates for several pediatric cancers, including high‑grade gliomas and certain sarcomas, remain unacceptably high. Moreover, persistent disparities in pediatric cancer incidence and outcomes across race, ethnicity, geography, and other social determinants of health continue to pose significant challenges. Continued research and the development of efficient, equitable therapeutic strategies remain critically needed to improve outcomes for all children with cancer.
