Abstract

Over the past decade, machine learning (ML) has established a transformative paradigm for investigating amyloidogenic polypeptides. Characterized by their propensity to form ordered, β-sheet-rich fibrillar aggregates, these polypeptides are centrally implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD). This review systematically evaluates the application of ML across critical domains of amyloid investigation: sequence-based amyloidogenicity prediction, structural biology, inhibitor design, and amyloid-related clinical objectives. We further summarize the diverse array of ML architectures currently employed in amyloid research, spanning classical algorithms (e.g., support vector machines and random forests) to advanced deep learning frameworks, such as the transformer framework, protein language models (PLMs), etc. Additionally, we spotlight emerging frontiers, namely, the integration of ML with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, the use of generative AI for de novo inhibitor design, etc., while addressing persistent bottlenecks in the field, including data standardization, model interpretability, and rigorous experimental validation for in silico predictions. Ultimately, this review serves as a foundational roadmap for emerging researchers and a definitive reference for established investigators operating at the intersection of artificial intelligence and amyloid biology.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease, primarily arise from misfolded amyloid proteins that aggregate into insoluble fibrils and accumulate within cells. These neurodegenerative diseases account for more than half of all dementia cases. Over 50 million people worldwide live with dementia, with a projected increase to 152 million by 2050. Prominent examples of such proteinopathies include amyloid-β and tau in Alzheimer’s disease, α-synuclein in Parkinson’s disease, the prion protein in Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, mutant huntingtin in Huntington’s disease, and superoxide dismutase 1 in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Even though these amyloidogenic polypeptides have different amino acid sequences and physiological configurations, they all aggregate into a similar “cross-β” spine structure. These β-strand configurations fix at certain angles relative to the fibril axis and are stabilized by tight interdigitation of dry side-chain interfaces between adjacent β-sheets that exclude water, called “steric zippers”. − This highly structured configuration exhibits exceptional thermodynamic stability and resistance to proteolytic degradation, which further causes its pathological accumulation in neurons. , Recent cutting-edge techniques in X-ray crystallography, cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy have aided in characterizing the amyloid protein structures within the amyloid fibrils. − These developments have exhibited molecular-level accuracy and captured the kinetic profiles of aggregation pathways. − However, these devices also manifest some practical limitations: excessively high infrastructure costs, combined with time-intensive workflows for sample preparation and data collection, which are fundamentally incompatible with the high-throughput screening of polypeptide sequences or amyloid protein mutations in diverse environments. Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a transformative methodology for investigating amyloid proteins. − By extracting complex patterns from existing amyloid data sets, ML models can screen the enormous amino acid sequence space at speeds that are several orders of magnitude faster than traditional wet-lab techniques. Computational research has evolved through several stages, starting from classical ML architectures using hand-crafted physicochemical features to end-to-end deep learning algorithms and ultimately culminating in the current era of protein language models (PLMs) and generative artificial intelligence (AI). − Among the most famous AI models, the three most prominent examples of PepMLM, AlphaFold, and RoseTTAFold, designed based on the attention algorithm, have fundamentally reshaped the paradigms of protein structure prediction, molecular design, and biomolecular engineering. − Some derivative deep-learning models built on top of these algorithms have been utilized in amyloidogenic polypeptide research. This review focuses on research applying ML to amyloidogenic polypeptide research, which is divided into 5 sections: sequence-based amyloidogenicity prediction, amyloid protein structure predictions, inhibitor design, clinical objectives, and current challenges and future orientations. In each field, we elaborate on the applied ML algorithms and their applications in amyloid problems, which focus on methodological innovations and their biological significance. Subsequently, the current challenges and prospects are discussed. Hopefully, this Review can serve as a roadmap for researchers entering the field and as a reference for those already working at the intersection of ML and amyloid research.

Sequence-Based Prediction of Amyloidogenicity

Predicting and classifying amyloidogenicity from the amino acid sequence of certain polypeptides is one of the most established fields in ML applications. In general, classical ML methodologies rely on a standardized workflow encompassing data set curation (such as the Waltz, , Amy and Cross-β Database (DB)), physicochemical feature extraction (including hydrophobicity, β-sheet propensity, net charge distribution, and the continuous length of aggregation-prone segments), model development (utilizing algorithms like Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, and Support Vector Machine (SVM)), and empirical evaluation (employing tools like SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)). Building upon this paradigm, various methodological frameworks have been developed to capture specific nuances of the underlying sequence-to-function relationship. One of the early representative examples is where Rousseau et al. experimentally explored the sequence space of amyloid hexapeptides and used the derived data to build Waltz, a widely used web-based tool that leverages position-specific scoring matrices to identify amyloid-forming sequences. Subsequently, based on 1,415 hexapeptides from the Waltz database, Budapest Amyloid Predictor (BAP) trained a linear SVM to predict amyloidogenicity through a position-specific amyloid matrix. It was found that the six positions in a hexapeptide contribute differently to amyloidogenicity. Other ML models take some other ML model or their combinations to classify amyloidogenicity. iAMY-SCM applied the scoring card method for predicting and analyzing amyloid proteins. Its ML architecture is shown in Figure a. The use of a simple weighted-sum function in conjunction with the propensity scores of dipeptides can achieve remarkable efficacy for the amyloid protein identification. The FISH Amyloid method introduced a co-occurrence matrix approach that identifies amyloidogenic segments by analyzing the pairwise amino acid preferences within experimentally characterized amyloid-forming regions. Some ML models adopt other specific features to characterize amyloidogenicity. PredAmyl-MLP employed a multilayer perceptron trained on features selected via maximum relevance maximum distance, and a binomial distribution, as demonstrated in Figure b. Even basic neural networks are capable of capturing nonlinear sequence–amyloidogenicity relationships. FoldAmyloid, a web server, predicted amyloidogenic regions based on the probability of hydrogen bond formation and residue packing density. The transition from classical ML to ensemble methods could potentially enhance prediction robustness. The PATH model uses experimental aggregate structures as templates for threading query peptides, then applies ML on the resulting energy terms to classify amyloidogenicity. Combining multiple classifiers through voting or stacking could theoretically outperform individual models. AMYPred-FRL combined six different ML models, including extremely randomized tree, extreme gradient boosting, k-nearest neighbor, logistic regression, random forest, and support vector machine, with recursive feature elimination. The predictions generated by these base classifiers were fed into a final meta-based model for classification, as illustrated in Figure c. This stacked baseline framework predicts amyloidogenicity from different angles, with accuracy higher than any individual algorithm. Kajava et al. built a Cross-β database, consisting of known cross-β amyloids formed under natural conditions. Constructing a specific feature construction pipeline based on amino acid sequence, shown in Figure d, they subsequently built and benchmarked several ML algorithms and found that the Extra Trees ML algorithm had the highest accuracy. Based on a similar framework, the ENTAIL was built on a Naive Bayes with unbounded support and an ensemble classifier using over than 4000 molecular descriptors. It was found that a balanced data set is very important for accurate prediction. MILAMP , is trained on heterogeneous data sources and exploits common predictive patterns across these tasks through a multiple instance learning-based workflow. It is designed for amyloid protein analysis, including amyloid protein recognition, amyloid hotspot mapping, and quantitative assessment of the impact of mutations on amyloidogenic propensity. Collectively, sequence-based ML predictors have efficiently mapped amyloid propensity across large peptide libraries and remain the most extensively investigated within this domain. However, they inherently overlook the influence of the experimental environment, like temperature, peptide concentration, pH, etc., post-translational modifications, and interactions between peptides. This could be the direction for future ML model design. The limitations of classical ML approaches operating on hand-crafted features have driven the adoption of deep learning architectures, especially pretrained PLMs, that can learn feature representations directly from unlabeled sequence data and directly transfer that knowledge to the amyloid prediction task. PLM embeddings capture long-range sequence dependencies and evolutionary constraints that are relevant to amyloid formation but inaccessible to other approaches like sliding-window feature engineering. A strategy is to use PLMs as a fixed feature extractor or to fine-tune it on the amyloid-specific task, thereby leveraging the evolutionary and biophysical patterns encoded in the model’s embeddings from training on millions of natural protein sequences. AggBERT, built by semisupervised fine-tuning of the pretrained ProtBERT language model on the amyloid classification of hexapeptide task, demonstrates that transfer learning from large-scale PLMs can match or exceed the performance of task-specific architectures trained from scratch. AggNet further couples pretrained protein large language model (Evolutionary-Scale Modeling 2 (ESM-2)) embeddings with AlphaFold2 structural predictions within a unified deep learning framework and combines evolutionary-scale language model representations with predicted three-dimensional structural information, yielding high predictive performance for discriminating amyloid and nonamyloid peptides, identifying aggregation-prone regions, and predicting protein aggregation propensity. Its architecture is shown in Figure a. The TSLAmy framework extends this idea with a two-stage architecture: the first stage uses autoencoders for unsupervised clustering of the sequence space and the ESM-2 PLM for feature extraction; the second stage performs aggregation prediction on the learned representations, enabling the model to systematically explore all 64 million possible hexapeptides and rank them by aggregation propensity, a scale that would be intractable with experimental methods alone. A deep-learning model built on top of a pretrained PLM model, leveraging bidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) layers, was trained on the Waltz hexapeptide database to classify amyloidogenic or nonamyloidogenic peptides and locate aggregation-prone regions within proteins. Similarly, Li et al. applied bidirectional LSTM with an attention algorithm to identify amyloid proteins and also obtained favorable predictive performance. The AMYGNN framework utilized graph convolutional networks (GCNs) trained on non-Euclidean structural data for predicting the trends of amyloid formation from specific peptide sequences, as illustrated in Figure b. By making use of complex, higher-order sequence-structure interactions, their performance is better than that of traditional ML models. Therefore, compared to traditional ML models, end-to-end deep-learning models can capture more complex relations in peptide sequences and are inherently convenient to integrate structure information into amyloidogenic prediction. Additionally, they remain relatively less explored, leaving substantial potential for further investigation. A fundamental limitation in many current ML models is the treatment of amyloidogenicity as an intrinsic binary sequence property. In reality, amyloid formation is a highly complex thermodynamic and kinetic process that is heavily dependent on environmental conditions, including local protein concentration, pH, ionic strength, temperature, agitation, and the presence of membranes or cofactors. Furthermore, predictions must distinguish between different phenomena: mapping aggregation-prone regions, predicting hexapeptide aggregation propensity, distinguishing functional from pathological amyloids, and assessing the macroscopic fibril formation. The inability of existing ML methods to address these aspects probably stems from the limited data sets. Partitioning the chemical space into excessively small regions yields insufficient data within each region, precluding adequate sampling of the broader chemical space. Nonetheless, these regions represent promising directions for future investigations. Amyloid Protein Structure Predictions › Fibril-Structure Prediction

Moving beyond binary classification of amyloidogenicity, ML can also be used to understand the atomic-scale arrangement of amyloid fibrils, their nucleation and growth mechanisms, as well as the correlation between sequence mutations and structural polymorphism. These objects require ML models to predict the three-dimensional molecular structures and dynamic processes across time scales from microseconds to hours. Remarkable progress has been made by the application of deep learning to predict amyloid fibril structures. Landau et al. assessed AlphaFold2-ColabFold in predicting structures of diverse antimicrobial and disease-associated amyloids, featuring mixed cross-α and cross-β conformations. The model tended to generate helical structures, indicative of its bias toward monomeric helical states or membrane-activated peptide forms. RibbonFold is a modified AlphaFold2 framework, with its architecture illustrated in Figure a. It couples a polymorphism-oriented loss function and explicitly generates ensembles of plausible polymorphic structures and the structural landscape underlying amyloid polymorphism, which is rarely captured by traditional structural prediction approaches. Multiple instance learning frameworks are naturally suited to amyloid prediction. CANYA, an interpretable convolution-attention hybrid network, was designed to deduce protein aggregation (amyloid formation/nucleation) from amino acid sequences. It merges convolutional local feature extraction and attention-based global contextual perception to capture the multiscale structural properties of amyloids. Unlike most globular proteins that adopt a single native structure, amyloid proteins can populate many different fibrous configurations based on their environment, such as pH, temperature, and ionic strength, and the secondary structure of surrounding proteins, like the presence of seed fibrils. For example, based on cryo-electron microscopic (cryo-EM) investigation, − the tau protein has been found to adopt over 20 different polymorphic structures throughout the various tauopathies. These different polymorphs have some biological consequences: structural diversity can cause differences in toxicity, seeding capacity, and interactions with cellular components. Most of the well-known ML models, such as AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold, primarily predict protein native folds by leveraging evolutionary correlations derived from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs). These models are inherently incapable of capturing the dynamic conformational transitions underlying amyloidogenesis. Their static structural predictions cannot provide insights into aggregation kinetics or transient oligomeric intermediates. Thus, a reliable ML model that can predict the full ensemble of accessible polymorphs, as well as their relative stabilities under diverse conditions, is the direction for the future. Some achievements have been made, such as RibbonFold, but a well-trained ML model based on extensive data sets is still a big challenge in this field. Amyloid Protein Structure Predictions › Spectroscopy-to-Structure Inference

The combination of ML with experimental techniques, such as cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR), and X-ray crystallography, is also a highly promising field for exploring self-assembly of peptides into amyloid-like nanostructures. For example, a specialized deep neural network (FNet) deep-learning model trained on single-molecule fluorescence lifetime imaging data can separate an image of highly overlapping fibrils into single fibril images, which contribute to tracking the growth and kinetics in amyloid fibrils (Figure b). It facilitates proposing a general nucleation mechanism of the amyloid-β peptides, where a fibril is formed on the surface of an oligomer with a different structure, that remain undetectable via bulk measurements or manual analysis. In a parallel work, a transformer-based ML framework has been developed to predict the three-dimensional atomic structures of monomeric intrinsically disordered aggregation-prone segments of amyloidogenic proteins from two-dimensional infrared spectral data. By accurately reconstructing the conformational dynamics of disease-associated peptides, this model serves as a versatile tool for elucidating protein misfolding mechanisms and evaluating drug efficacy, establishing interpretable correlations between two-dimensional spectra and structure. Therefore, the training of ML on experimental data has exhibited a promising field, yet numerous obstacles remain. In the experiment, the amyloid fibril sample often exists as a highly complex mixture, making its purification complex and challenging. Although some ML frameworks have proven to be well-behaved in extracting and predicting fibril geometric structures and fibrous growth dynamics, these models still struggle to extract low-abundance structures from noisy image data obtained from these heterogeneous mixtures. Building a more sensitive ML model could be a potential area for future work.

Amyloid Protein Structure Predictions › Coupled Molecular Dynamics (MD)-ML Prediction

The amyloid structures can also be investigated through the combination of MD simulations and ML. Since MD simulations are simple, fast, and cost-friendly, high-throughput MD can be easily realized and utilized to build databases for ML training. In addition, MD can readily discover and validate the structural mechanisms of fibrous proteins, key molecular interactions governing self-assembly, and the effects of sequence modifications on aggregation pathways. Integrating ML with these computational results has the potential to be a highly promising strategy. A representative example is the ML4MD framework, which integrates active learning with MD simulations to look for fibril-prone sequences. This approach iteratively refines ML’s predictions based on MD-derived aggregation data to guide the discovery of previously unrecognized fibril-prone sequences. Beyond constructing data sets, MD simulations can further assist in mechanistic exploration and serve as a validation tool for ML predictions. amyAMP represents a generative deep learning architecture predicated on a Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Network with Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP) to learn and design peptides with combined antimicrobial and amyloidogenic properties (Figure c). Validated through subsequent MD simulations, this framework is specifically engineered to extract latent biological features and autonomously generate peptide sequences exhibiting dual antimicrobial and amyloidogenic properties. Thus, ML and MD coupling could be a potentially powerful approach to investigate amyloid protein structure, yet it still struggles to bridge the spatiotemporal dilemma, such as the gap between liquid–liquid phase separation and solid-phase nucleation during amyloid aggregation. The amyloid aggregation involves multiscale evolution processes, spanning monomer and oligomer formation, liquid–liquid phase separation, and irreversible fibrillization. Conventional all-atomistic MD simulations are limited by computational time and resources, struggling to reproduce the processes from monomer to fibrillization. While coarse-grained models may be able to simulate part of the mesoscopic aggregation procedures, they still lack sufficient accuracy in capturing microscopic interactions, including hydrogen bonds and π–π stacking interactions, that govern the polymorphic specificity of amyloid fibrils. Thus, the integration of ML with MD simulations presents significant potential but faces formidable challenges in predicting the whole aggregation process of the amyloid protein. MD labels inherently carry the biases of the underlying empirical force fields and suffer from sampling limitations. While ML potentials (neural network force fields) can accelerate calculations, they do not automatically resolve the rare-event sampling problem critical to amyloid nucleation, meaning enhanced sampling techniques remain indispensable.

Drug Discovery and Inhibitor Design

The computational investigation of amyloid inhibitors via ML has emerged as a compelling frontier in modern drug discovery. Identifying β-site Amyloid Precursor Protein Cleaving Enzyme 1 (BACE1) inhibitors reducing Aβ production has been one of the major focuses of ML-driven drug discovery for Alzheimer’s disease. BACE1, also known as β-secretase, is mainly expressed in the neurons of the brain. It is a transmembrane aspartyl-protease, responsible for producing toxic Aβ from the amyloid precursor protein, and its inhibitors hold promising therapeutic value for disease intervention. Several studies have applied classification-based ML to distinguish BACE1 inhibitors from noninhibitors using chemical structure descriptors. Kumar et al. applied multiple ML classification models, including support vector machines, random forests, and XGBoost, clustering molecular descriptors of 3536 BACE1 inhibitors. Subsequent structural analysis of the 11 resulting subclusters successfully identified key molecular fragments that distinguish active and inactive BACE1 inhibitors. Dhamodharan et al. developed the two-target 2D-QSAR models, which were constructed based on dual acetylcholinesterase and BACE1 inhibitory N-benzyl piperidine derivatives via ML algorithms, with satisfactory statistical fitness and predictive performance. Specific molecular descriptors, like structural (Cl_Count, Num_Terminal_Rotamers), electro-topological (ES_Count_dO), electronic (Dipole_Z), and spatial (Shadow_nu) features, were identified as critical determinants of BACE1 enzyme inhibitory activity. Developability-related constraints represent an alternative strategy to suppress Aβ aggregation. The streaMLine platform integrates solid-phase peptide synthesis, high-throughput screening, and random forest-based quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling (Figure b). This platform combined solid-phase peptide synthesis, high-throughput screening, and random forest-based quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling. By systematically synthesizing and testing thousands of peptides, researchers converted the nonamyloidogenic secretin backbone into a potent GLP-1 receptor agonist. SHAP analysis identified specific amino acid substitutions that improved selectivity while maintaining solubility and avoiding fibrillation, directly addressing the amyloid formation problem that plagues native GLP-1 therapeutics. Utilizing ML to identify direct Aβ aggregation inhibitors offers another promising therapeutic strategy for disease intervention by halting the pathological transition of Aβ monomers into toxic fibrillar aggregates. The Amylo-IC50Pred web platform, integrating random forest classifiers, regression models, and multiclass classification, predicts the inhibitory potency of small molecules against Aβ aggregation and uncovers key molecular properties for rational inhibitor design. By training on experimentally characterized inhibitors, hydrophobicity, shape, charge distribution, and molecular symmetry of small molecules were identified as critical contributors to model performance, which aids Alzheimer’s drug discovery. Quantitative structure–activity relationship modeling is used to compare numerous ML models predicting the inhibitor potency, negative logarithm of IC50 of candidate compounds, which are then validated by molecular docking based on their binding affinity. A lead compound (CHEMBL5080033) with a pIC50 of 8.67 M and a binding energy of about 7.6 kcal/mol was discovered. Iterative ML workflows have been applied to discover polymorph-specific small-molecule inhibitors of tau aggregation (Figure a), where each round of prediction, testing, and retraining progressively improved compound activity. Many amyloid peptide aggregation inhibitors were developed via integrating ML screening, computational docking, and validated in vitro experiments and in vivo verification. Compared to traditional drug design, the prediction and validation of Aβ aggregation inhibitors using ML present distinct challenges. Compared with well-characterized enzymes such as BACE1, Aβ aggregation is a complex, multistep process characterized by transitions from monomeric peptides, oligomeric aggregates, and heterogeneous, dynamic fibrillar structures, without a well-defined binding site. These configurational complexities and dynamic structures limit the application of molecular docking in screening small-molecule inhibitors. Nevertheless, ML-guided virtual screening represents a powerful alternative. ML can learn from existing inhibitor structures and design new potential compounds without the knowledge of the complicated amyloid protein assemblies. It is crucial to recognize that for the discovery of direct aggregation inhibitors, computational docking scores and ML-predicted IC50 values are vastly insufficient on their own. Amyloid aggregation is a highly dynamic and heterogeneous process, meaning that static structural docking often fails to capture the complex interactions with transient toxic oligomeric intermediates. Furthermore, computational predictions cannot account for experimental artifacts, such as small molecules quenching or interfering with thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assays, which frequently lead to false positives. Therefore, ML-driven virtual screening must be strictly coupled to orthogonal experimental validation. Promising candidates require comprehensive evaluation through biophysical counterscreens, morphological assessments (e.g., transmission electron microscopy or atomic force microscopy), cross-seeding inhibition tests, and rigorous cellular cytotoxicity assays to confirm their true therapeutic potential. Amyloid-Related Clinical Objectives

The application of ML to clinical amyloid research facilitates the detection, diagnosis, and prognostic evaluation of amyloid-related disorders. Amyloid Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging, which enables in vivo detection of cerebral Aβ deposition and serves as the predominant imaging modality for assessing brain Aβ burden in clinical practice and research, has emerged as a major research focus for ML, driven by analytical studies in this domain. Visual assessment of amyloid PET scans relies on the availability of radiologist expertise. In contrast, quantification of amyloid burden typically involves Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) for processing and analysis, which can be computationally expensive. AmyloidPETNet is a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on 1538 PET scans, which can determine brain amyloid PET positive or negative independent of expert radiologists and structural MRI data. Thompson et al. compare multiple ML models trained on paired MRI/PET data from 1,847 participants to infer Aβ + from standard anatomical MRI. They adopted five types of ML algorithms: conventional ML models (logistic regression, XGBoost, and shallow ANNs), 2D/3D CNN-based deep learning, hybrid artificial neural network (ANN)–CNN architectures, CNN-based transfer learning, and 3D Vision Transformers. Results demonstrated that shallow/deep neural networks and traditional ML models showed promise for predicting amyloid positivity using routine structural brain MRI. A ML protocol combining a transformer-in-transformer architecture with two-dimensional MRI showed that deep learning can extract structural distributions, the ensemble of conformations populated during aggregation, for four-category dementia diagnosis, achieving high diagnostic accuracy and reliability metrics. Lee et al. proposed a one-step quantification method for amyloid PET via a deep-learning model to quantify the amyloid PET regardless of multicenter images and various radiotracers. The generative adversarial network with shared parameters (ShareGAN) model was trained and tested with 1009 Aβ PET and paired MRI images to generate 3D synthetic Aβ PET images from structural MRI. Using only MRI and readily available diagnostic data with less cost and exposure to ionizing radiation, Petrella et al. utilized CNN-based deep learning to predict PET-determined amyloid-tau-neurodegeneration biomarker status. Numerous ML studies have predicted PET status using PET and MRI images, achieving satisfactory performance. − However, they share several critical challenges in the ML prediction process. First, data heterogeneity stemming from varying techniques and scanning protocols severely limits image comparability and the ML training workflow. Second, the resolution gap between PET and MRI is considerable, which compromises precise, voxel-level spatiotemporal registration, especially when disrupted by physiological motion. Third, even though deep learning exhibits exceptional performance in some clinical diagnoses, it still greatly suffers from poor interpretability, causing a lack of clear biological mechanisms or pathways necessary for actual clinical implementation. In clinical applications, a strict distinction must be drawn between ML models that predict amyloid-biomarker positivity (e.g., amyloid PET status) and those that diagnose the clinical syndrome of Alzheimer’s disease, as amyloid burden alone is not strictly equivalent to clinical dementia. Furthermore, while ML models attempting MRI-to-PET synthesis show promise, they face a significant risk of exploiting cohort demographics, scanner-specific artifacts, or general brain atrophy correlations rather than true amyloid-specific biology. The field must move beyond single-cohort studies. The clinical utility of these models will ultimately depend on robust external multicenter validation, demonstrating generalizability across different MRI scanners and PET radiotracers, alongside rigorous uncertainty quantification to guide physician decision-making.

Current Challenges and Future Orientations

In general, data quality and database establishment are still some of the biggest challenges in the field of ML-based amyloidogenic prediction. Early ML trainings were realized on relatively small data sets consisting of several hundred peptides, which limited their generalizability. The subsequent development of comprehensive databases, such as WALTZ-DB 2.0 and the Cross-β database, gathering experimentally characterized amyloid peptide information from the published literature, has enabled more robust model training. But challenges persist: experimental characterization of amyloidogenicity applies different assays under different conditions, which results in inconsistent ground truth labels. For example, a peptide defined as amyloidogenic by thioflavin T fluorescence may not form fibrils under the conditions examined by electron microscopy. Standardizing experimental protocols and consensus annotations will significantly improve the training data for future ML models. Furthermore, establishing a community-organized amyloid prediction assessment, similar to the Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction (CASP) for protein structures, would accelerate progress in this field. Additionally, data imbalance is another problem. Amyloidogenic sequences are far rarer than nonamyloidogenic ones in natural sequence databases, creating a class imbalance that can bias ML models toward predicting the majority class. To solve this database bias problem, several class imbalance learning strategies can be employed: SMOTE generates artificial positive examples by interpolating between existing amyloidogenic sequences; cost-sensitive learning , assigns higher penalties to misclassification of the minority class; and one-class classification models the distribution of nonamyloidogenic sequences and flags deviations. Note that the choice of an imbalance handling strategy significantly affects model performance, particularly for identifying rare amyloidogenic sequences in genome-wide screens. In addition, active learning could potentially correct database bias and accelerate high-throughput discovery by iteratively proposing underrepresented sequences for the next round of testing. While much of the previous amyloid research focused on mature fibril structures in the ML area, the transient, structurally heterogeneous oligomeric intermediates also act as driving forces in amyloid pathology because they are highly toxic to pancreatic β-cells. These oligomers could impair cellular homeostasis via many mechanisms: , (1) most notably, oligomers permeabilize lipid membranes and form ion-conducting pores. They actively disrupt lipid membranes and bind to cellular receptors (such as the Receptor for Advanced Glycation Endproducts (RAGE)) to activate pathogenic cell death signaling pathways; , (2) activating endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response. This process further triggers dysfunction of the ubiquitin–proteasome system and autophagy; (3) perturbing mitochondrial function and disturbing cellular redox balance. Following cellular uptake, oligomers induce inflammation and the formation of reactive oxygen species upon cellular internalization; (4) compromising endosomal–lysosomal function, axonal and synaptic trafficking; and (5) triggering stress-kinase signaling cascades and apoptotic pathways. Apart from primary nucleation and elongation, seeding and cross-seeding mechanisms also play an important role in neurodegenerative and systemic pathologies. The aggregates of one protein could guide and template their abnormal structures to either the same precursor proteins (seeding) or other protein monomers (cross-seeding). These processes were found in human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP, or also known as amylin), α-synuclein, and semen-derived enhancer of viral infection (SEVI) formed between the bacterial curli. However, investigating these complex phenomena requires careful consideration of potential experimental limitations. For instance, while standard Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence remains widely utilized for tracking fibrillogenesis, these extrinsic probes can produce misleading results by competitively binding with small-molecule inhibitors like Epigallocatechin-3-gallate for specific sites on amyloid fibrils. Thus, orthogonal techniques such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and electron microscopy are needed for further validation. Ultimately, overcoming these detection and mechanistic hurdles is essential for therapeutic development. Due to the low abundance, structural heterogeneity, and the transient nature of oligomeric species and cross-seeding events, coupled with limitations in available experimental techniques, experimental data sets remain scarce, and ML approaches are underexplored in this field. Future ML frameworks also need to extend to multifaceted properties, including orthogonal assay outcomes, cytotoxicity, polymorph specificity, ADME profiles, and blood–brain barrier penetration. For example, recent advances in high-throughput small-molecule screening targeting the membrane interface, the site of key early misfolding and oligomerization events, have yielded tool compounds that interact with and stabilize distinct, nontoxic off-pathway Aβ species. These compounds counter amyloid-β-mediated pathogenic cascades, providing promising strategies for the rational design of disease-modifying therapeutics. These high-throughput experimental data also open up opportunities for database construction, ML model training, and extended chemical space exploration. Challenges in model validation and data leakage are potential problems in amyloid ML studies. Flawed validation practices frequently compromise the reliability of ML models in predicting amyloid behavior, yet this issue is often overlooked in many existing studies. Random splitting of data sets containing overlapping peptides or homologous proteins inevitably leads to severe information leakage, resulting in artificially inflated performance metrics. , Robust model evaluation requires rigorous data-splitting strategies, such as CD-HIT sequence clustering at low identity thresholds (e.g., <30%), or leave-protein-family out cross-validation, to ensure the model generalizes to unseen sequence spaces. Furthermore, because amyloidogenic sequences are vastly outnumbered by nonamyloidogenic ones in nature, the data sets are highly imbalanced. In such scenarios, relying on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve or overall accuracy is misleading. Future studies must prioritize the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and rigorous model calibration. Ultimately, the true utility of any predictive model rests on its domain of applicability and successful prospective experimental validation, rather than retrospective computational metrics. Synergizing ML with MD represents a promising frontier. High-throughput MD could generate extensive, cost-effective training data to train the ML. Meanwhile, ML can reciprocally refine more accurate interatomic potentials (e.g., DeepMD) or optimize the free energy computing algorithm, accelerating sampling efficiency. , These benefits could effectively build a more accurate and amyloid-specific force field and bridge the time scale gap between in silico simulations and empirical experiments. Emerging ML frameworks, such as the combination of PLMs, like ESM-2 and ProtBERT, with some deep-learning models, are transforming amyloidogenic prediction. These PLMs could alleviate the problem of experimental data scarcity by being pretrained on more extensive protein databases. By integrating structure-aware attention mechanisms and equivariant networks, these PLMs comprehensively extract intricate conformational patterns and latent biological knowledge from other proteins. By integrating deep-learning models on top of PLMs, specific fine-tuning can be carried out to enhance amyloidogenic prediction. Some other new ML paradigms are also advancing the design and mechanistic study of amyloid proteins. Interpretable ML can elucidate underlying disease mechanisms by isolating causal relationships from statistical noise, and could complement the poor interpretability or “black box” problem of many deep-learning models. Algorithms, including SHAP and LIME, can identify which amino acids among the sequences and physicochemical properties contribute most to predictions. Additionally, generative AI, such as GANs, diffusion models, and autoregressive transformers, drives closed-loop “data-ML-analysis-design” cycles to efficiently discover and formulate novel biomaterials and amyloid inhibitors. Therefore, a critical near-term milestone lies in the development of a single multitask, end-to-end framework capable of simultaneously predicting amyloid propensity, fibril structure, and aggregation kinetics, as well as designing functional inhibitors. This progress can be enhanced by combining the strengths of multiple emerging algorithms: PLMs responsible for extracting comprehensive biological information and building embedding matrices, deep learning aimed at amyloidogenic fine-tuning, SHAP workflows for interpretable mechanistic analysis, and generative AI for novel molecular design. Each of these methods offers different functional and complementary advantages, and their combination could potentially allow unprecedented research progress that is difficult to achieve by any individual model. Note that while posthoc interpretability methods like SHAP and LIME are frequently employed to explain model predictions, it is crucial to recognize that they report feature attributions and statistical correlations, not true biophysical or causal mechanisms. Similarly, the deployment of generative AI for de novo peptide design must be scrutinized beyond simple structural generation. Generative models must be strictly evaluated for training-set memorization, structural novelty, diversity, and chemical synthesizability. Designing effective therapeutics requires navigating multiobjective trade-offs encompassing binding potency, solubility, toxicity, and pharmacokinetics. Therefore, rather than pursuing a monolithic “end-to-end” predictive framework, the future of the field relies on developing uncertainty-aware, task-specific ML modules that are intimately linked by predefined, orthogonal experimental validation steps at every stage of discovery.

Methods

To comparatively evaluate the ML applications in amyloidogenic polypeptide studies, literature searches were carried out using Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed, covering articles published up to 2026. The search string consists of combinations of terms such as (“machine learning” OR “deep learning” OR “artificial intelligence”) AND (“amyloid” OR “amyloidogenic polypeptides” OR “drug discovery” OR “clinical diagnosis”). Preprint papers from bioRxiv and arXiv were discussed only if they exhibit highly novel architectures or heavily utilized community-standard data sets, and they are regarded as nonpeer-reviewed in our report. It is crucial to note that direct performance comparisons between these ML models are often precluded by the use of different training data sets and specific definitions of amyloidogenicity.

Conclusions

ML has become integral to amyloidogenic polypeptide research, evolving from simple hexapeptide classifiers to advanced architectures capable of predicting fibril structures, designing inhibitors, and assisting clinical diagnostics. For some applications, classical ML methodologies rely on a standardized pipeline: data set curation (e.g., Waltz, Amy, and Cross-β), physicochemical feature extraction (e.g., hydrophobicity, β-sheet propensity, charge distribution), model training via traditional algorithms (e.g., Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, SVM), and empirical evaluation using interpretability frameworks like SHAP and LIME. In contrast, deep neural networks facilitate end-to-end structural prediction and rational inhibitor design, with many advanced applications increasingly integrating both classical and deep learning paradigms to leverage their respective strengths. Thus, continued advancements in data and modeling are expected to foster the cross-domain integration necessary to overcome the multifaceted challenges of amyloid research.
