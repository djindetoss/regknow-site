Abstract

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into pharmaceutical care represents a paradigm shift in healthcare delivery, offering unprecedented opportunities to revolutionize medication management, accelerate drug development, and enhance patient outcomes. This comprehensive review synthesized evidence from global literature (2021-2025) examining AI applications across drug discovery and development, clinical trials optimization, medication therapy management, and pharmacy operations. The AI demonstrated prediction accuracies of 86-98% in drug discovery, 80% improvement in clinical trial recruitment efficiency, and 32.7% increase in medication adherence over standard care. The global AI drug discovery market, valued at $1.5 billion (2023), is projected to reach $11.8 billion by 2030, reflecting substantial industry investment. However, implementation challenges persist including data quality concerns (30% accuracy in some datasets), regulatory compliance issues, and algorithmic bias (8-12% performance variations across demographics). Successful implementation requires coordinated efforts across technological development, regulatory frameworks, healthcare professional training, and continuous validation protocols addressing technical, organizational, and ethical dimensions simultaneously.

Introduction

The healthcare industry is undergoing a technological shift, with artificial intelligence (AI) serving as a transformative force in pharmaceutical sciences and clinical practice. The integration of advanced computational capabilities, high-quality healthcare data, and sophisticated analytical algorithms has generated considerable opportunities for innovation in drug development and patient-care delivery [1]. The pharmaceutical sector, known for its prolonged development cycles and substantial financial investments, is undergoing transformation due to the integration of AI throughout its value chain. The AI integration encompasses various computational methods aimed at replicating human cognitive functions, such as learning, reasoning, and problem solving [1]. In pharmaceutical applications, these technologies include machine-learning algorithms for identifying novel drug targets and natural-language processing systems for extracting insights from clinical literature. The ability to analyze large datasets, recognize intricate patterns, and produce predictive models has established AI as a crucial instrument for addressing enduring issues in drug discovery, development, and therapeutic delivery [2,3]. The economic significance of the influence of AI on pharmaceutical care is considerable and growing swiftly. Market analyses indicate substantial growth patterns, with global AI in the drug discovery market demonstrating annual compound growth rates surpassing 25% in recent years. This growth indicates technological advancements and a greater understanding of AI's capacity to tackle important healthcare issues, such as enhancing the efficiency of drug development, personalizing therapeutic strategies, and improving patient outcomes [4]. Pharmaceutical development typically follows a linear sequence that encompasses basic research, preclinical studies, clinical trials, regulatory approval, and post-market surveillance. The process generally requires 10–15 years and investments of over $1 billion for each drug that receives approval, with failure rates of approximately 90% during the clinical development stages [5,6]. The AI technologies have the potential to transform this paradigm by enhancing efficiency, improving prediction accuracy, and facilitating informed decision-making at each stage of development. The range of AI applications in pharmaceutical care includes drug discovery and clinical practice, which involves medication therapy management, adverse event prediction, dosage optimization, and patient monitoring [4]. These applications utilize various data sources, such as electronic health records, genomic data, real-world evidence, and patient-reported outcomes, to produce actionable insights for healthcare providers and patients. Modern healthcare delivery systems are increasingly focusing on personalized medical strategies that customize therapeutic interventions based on individual patient characteristics. The AI technologies effectively support this paradigm shift by integrating multiple data streams, identifying patient-specific risk factors, and predicting treatment responses. This capability is consistent with overarching trends in healthcare, specifically in precision medicine and value-based care delivery models [1]. The incorporation of AI into pharmaceutical care mirrors changing regulatory frameworks and professional practice models. Globally, regulatory agencies are formulating frameworks to assess AI-enabled technologies, whereas healthcare professionals are adjusting to new tools and workflows that integrate intelligent decision-support systems. These developments present opportunities and challenges for pharmaceutical care delivery. Despite growing evidence of AI applications in individual pharmaceutical domains, significant gaps persist in the literature. Prior reviews have focused on narrow technological capabilities without adequately addressing real-world implementation challenges, including data quality barriers, algorithm drift, and health equity implications. Furthermore, integrated frameworks spanning the complete pharmaceutical care continuum from drug discovery through clinical deployment and pharmacy operations remain limited. Regional adaptation requirements for diverse healthcare systems, particularly in emerging markets pursuing digital health transformation, are underexplored. This comprehensive review addresses these gaps by synthesizing current evidence across all pharmaceutical care domains, examining implementation frameworks including technological, regulatory, and workforce dimensions, analyzing regional case studies, and providing evidence-based recommendations for successful integration that balance innovation with safety, efficacy, and equity considerations. Introduction › Study design and data sources

This comprehensive review used a twofold methodology: i) a global systematic analysis of AI applications in pharmaceutical care, ii) and a focused regional case study examining AI deployment in Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) concerning Vision 2030 and Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) healthcare efforts. A thorough literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar, covering publications from January 2018 to October 2025. Consolidate search phrases related to AI technology, pharmaceutical applications, and healthcare concepts such as precision medicine, clinical decision support, and medication adherence. For the regional component of KSA, further focused searches included the Saudi Digital Library, Saudi Pharmaceutical Journal, Vision 2030 material, Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) publications, and Ministry of Health (MOH) reports. Additional sources, such as regulatory recommendations from the Food and Drug Administration, European Medicines Agency, World Health Organization (WHO), and SFDA, together with corroborative industry papers and conference proceedings, were also examined. Reference lists were analyzed, and forward citation analysis was performed on essential components.

Introduction › Study selection and quality assessment

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they constituted peer-reviewed original research, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, clinical case studies, technical validation studies, or implementation reports that outlined AI/machine learning (ML) applications pertinent to pharmaceutical care domains, including drug discovery, clinical trials, personalized medicine, medication therapy management, or pharmacy operations. Eligible studies were required to report explicit quantitative performance metrics such as accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), receiver operating characteristic (ROC), sensitivity, or specificity, or demonstrate clinical outcomes including patient safety indicators, treatment efficacy measures, or adherence rates. Methodological rigor was assessed based on sufficient sample sizes, rigorous statistical analysis, and validation using independent datasets or prospective studies. Publications from January 2018 to October 2025 in the English language were considered for inclusion. For the regional KSA case study component, additional sources were accepted beyond peer-reviewed literature, including Saudi government publications and vision 2030 documentation, SFDA regulatory guidance, MOH strategic plans, verified implementation reports from regional healthcare facilities, and studies addressing cultural and regulatory considerations affecting pharmaceutical AI implementation in the GCC region. These supplemental materials provided essential context for understanding region-specific implementation frameworks and challenges. Studies were excluded if they addressed general healthcare technology without specific AI or ML applications, presented theoretical frameworks lacking validation data or empirical evidence, or lacked clear methodology and statistical analysis. Duplicate reports of the same study, investigations centered on non-pharmaceutical healthcare AI applications, publications from predatory journals, and conference abstracts without accompanying full papers were systematically excluded, with the exception of significant commercial implementations with verified outcomes. Unverifiable news reports without supporting documentation were also omitted from consideration. The initial literature search yielded 3,847 records across all databases, with PubMed/MEDLINE contributing 1,256 records, Scopus 1,089 records, Web of Science 892 records, IEEE Xplore 387 records, and Google Scholar 223 records. After removing 1,124 duplicate entries, 2,723 titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to the review objectives. Full-text assessment was performed on 312 articles, ultimately resulting in 67 studies meeting inclusion criteria for the global analysis. An additional 23 supplementary sources were included specifically for the KSA regional component, comprising governmental reports, regulatory documents, and verified implementation data from regional healthcare initiatives. The final sample of 67 studies was determined by thematic saturation across the 5 pharmaceutical care domains rather than predetermined sample size targets, consistent with established comprehensive review methodology. This approach ensured adequate representation of AI applications spanning drug discovery and development, clinical trial optimization, personalized medicine and precision therapeutics, medication therapy management, and pharmacy operations. The temporal scope spanning 2018 to 2025 ensured capture of both foundational AI applications established in earlier years and recent technological innovations reflecting current state-of-the-art implementations. Study quality was systematically assessed using domain-specific instruments appropriate to each study type. Clinical research studies were evaluated using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) standards, while diagnostic and predictive studies were assessed using Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) criteria. Implementation studies were evaluated using adapted frameworks examining feasibility, fidelity, and impact assessment, whereas technical reports were assessed for validation methodology rigor, sample representativeness, and statistical robustness. Two reviewers independently assessed study quality using these standardized instruments, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus. Studies identified as having high risk of bias were noted in the quality assessment but not excluded from the synthesis, as comprehensive reviews aim to capture the breadth of available evidence while acknowledging methodological limitations that may influence interpretation of findings. As a comprehensive review synthesizing published literature, publicly available reports, and regulatory documents, this study did not require institutional ethics committee approval. All included primary research studies involving human participants reported appropriate ethical approval and informed consent procedures from their respective institutions. For KSA regional case study component, only publicly accessible governmental reports, regulatory guidance documents, and published implementation data were utilized, with no direct patient-level data accessed or analyzed. The use of AI in drug discovery represents a substantial advancement in pharmaceutical research, fundamentally changing the process of identifying, optimizing, and developing new therapeutic compounds. Conventional drug discovery methods depend predominantly on empirical screening techniques, which, although effective, require considerable time and resources [7]. The AI technologies have the potential to enhance these processes using computational methods that analyze molecular structures, predict biological activities, and identify promising therapeutic targets with remarkable efficiency [8]. Machine-learning algorithms have shown substantial effectiveness in predicting molecular properties such that researchers can evaluate drug likeness, potential toxicity, and pharmacokinetic characteristics before synthesis [9]. These predictive capabilities markedly decrease the number of compounds required for physical testing, thus expediting the initial phases of drug development and lowering related costs. Deep-learning methods, especially those utilizing neural network frameworks, have demonstrated considerable efficacy in forecasting intricate molecular interactions and discovering new drug–target associations [9]. The incorporation of AI in drug discovery encompasses target identification and validation processes. Advanced algorithms can analyze genomic data, protein structures, and biological pathway information to identify potential therapeutic targets and evaluate drug ability. This computational method for target identification has resulted in the discovery of new therapeutic opportunities in previously unexamined biological areas, thereby broadening the potential for addressing gaps in medical care [8]. Drug repurposing is an important area of application in which AI technologies have shown considerable value. The AI systems can identify new therapeutic applications for approved medications by analyzing existing drug profiles, molecular structures, and biological mechanisms [10]. This method presents important benefits regarding development duration and expenses because repurposed medications possess known safety profiles and can bypass initial clinical development obligations. The optimization of clinical trials using AI technologies addresses several critical challenges in pharmaceutical development, including patient recruitment difficulties, protocol design complexities, and data management inefficiencies [11]. Historically, these challenges have contributed to extended development timelines and increased costs, making AI-driven solutions particularly valuable for pharmaceutical companies seeking to improve their development efficiency. Patient recruitment and retention represent persistent challenges in clinical trials, with studies indicating that recruitment difficulties contribute to delays in approximately 80% of the clinical trials [12]. The AI technologies offer solutions through sophisticated patient-matching algorithms that can analyze electronic health records, genomic data, and other clinical parameters to identify suitable trial participants. These systems can process vast amounts of patient data to identify individuals who meet specific inclusion criteria, while accounting for potential confounding factors that might affect trial outcomes [13]. Natural-language processing (NLP) applications have proven particularly valuable in clinical trial settings, enabling the automated extraction of relevant information from unstructured clinical documents [13]. These capabilities support more efficient screening processes and identify potential participants who might otherwise have been overlooked by traditional recruitment methods. In addition, NLP technologies can monitor ongoing trials for safety signals and protocol deviations, thereby enhancing trial oversight and participant safety [14]. Predictive analytical applications in clinical trials facilitate informed decision-making regarding trial design, endpoint selection, and statistical planning. The AI algorithms can analyze historical trial data to predict likely outcomes, optimal patient populations, and potential challenges, thereby enabling a more efficient protocol design and reducing the likelihood of trial failure [15]. These predictive capabilities are particularly valuable in rare disease research, where patient populations are limited, and trial failures can have a substantial impact on development programs. The transition to personalized medicine signifies a crucial change in therapeutic methodologies, shifting from population-centric treatment strategies to individualized interventions designed for specific patient characteristics. AI technology plays a crucial role in facilitating this transition by offering analytical capabilities that integrate various data sources and produce personalized treatment recommendations. Pharmacogenomics applications constitute a highly developed domain of AI-driven personalized medicine. Through the analysis of genetic variants that influence drug metabolism, transport, and target interactions, AI systems can forecast individual patient responses to particular medications and suggest suitable dosing strategies. For example, AI algorithms analyzing CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genetic variants have successfully optimized warfarin dosing with clinical outcomes similar to physician-guided approaches while reducing bleeding complications [16]. Similarly, AI-guided clopidogrel prescription based on CYP2C19 variants improves cardiovascular outcomes, whereas DPYD gene analysis prevents severe toxicity in patients receiving 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy [17]. These applications are especially beneficial for medications with narrow therapeutic windows or considerable interindividual variability. The combination of real-world evidence and AI analytics facilitates ongoing learning and enhancement of personalized treatment strategies. The AI systems analyze patient outcome data and electronic health records to discern patterns that enhance treatment optimization. For instance, International Business Machines (IBM) Watson for Oncology integrates patient data with treatment guidelines to recommend personalized cancer therapies, whereas AI platforms such as Tempus analyze tumor genetics with clinical data to identify optimal immunotherapy candidates [18,19]. The AI technologies have shown considerable value in biomarker discovery and validation. Advanced machine-learning algorithms analyze complex biological datasets to identify novel biomarkers that can predict treatment responses or disease progression [20]. Notable examples include AI identification of PD-L1 expression patterns for immunotherapy selection and the discovery of genetic signatures that predict chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer, guiding the creation of companion diagnostics for more accurate patient selection [21]. The AI in medication therapy management addresses issues in contemporary pharmacy practice, such as medication safety, adherence monitoring, and therapeutic optimization. These applications combine electronic health records, prescription databases, and patient-reported outcomes to provide insights to healthcare providers and patients. Drug interaction screening is an important progression in which AI outperforms traditional rule-based systems. Advanced algorithms evaluate intricate interaction patterns, considering pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics mechanisms, as well as individual patient risk factors [22]. The AI systems, such as Warfa-Check's clinical decision support, can identify interactions between warfarin and cranberry juice that traditional tools may overlook, whereas IBM's Watson detects rare drug–gene interactions in patients with multiple genetic variants that influence metabolism [13]. The AI has revolutionized medication adherence monitoring through the analysis of prescription filling patterns and patient data, facilitating the identification of challenges and implementation of targeted interventions. The Consumer Value Stores (CVS) Health's AI platform uses ML techniques built in Python 3.8 (Python Software Foundation, Delaware, United States of America [USA]) and scikit-learn version 0.24.2 for predictive modeling. The method predicts medicine abandonment with 85% accuracy using patient demographics and prescription information. Statistical validation was carried out using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), the caret package (version 6.0–88) for model performance assessment, and the pROC package (version 1.17.0.1) for ROC curve analysis and AUC calculations [23]. In addition, applications such as AiCure use computer vision to ensure that patients adhere to their medication regimens, resulting in adherence rate improvements of at least 80%. The AI-based adherence prediction models were tested in 235 individuals with schizophrenia (NCT03351244), 60.4% of recruited patients showed adherence of at least 80%. The 14-day observation window at the 0.6 threshold resulted in optimum predictive performance (AUC: 0.87), with trial-end forecasts being the most accurate (AUC: 0.92). A stratified study revealed that BI 409306 decreased recurrence risk in adherent completers (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.485) and predicted adherent patients (HR = 0.510) [24]. Intelligent clinical decision-support systems signify advanced developments in therapeutic management. The Epic Sepsis Model evaluates patient data in real time to suggest modifications in antibiotic therapy, [25] Cerner's HealtheIntent technology (Oracle Health, Kansas City, MO, USA) uses predictive analytics to identify patients who are at high risk for adverse medication events before they occur in the clinic. These systems are particularly useful in handling complicated clinical situations with numerous comorbidities and concurrent treatment regimens that exceed 5 medicines [26]. Dosing optimization applications use AI to recommend personalized strategies based on patient characteristics and pharmacokinetic modeling. Bayesian-guided precision dosing with DoseMeRx significantly improved vancomycin treatment optimization. Among 243 hospitalized patients, DoseMeRx-guided dosage reached therapeutic AUC24 objectives in 68.3%, compared to 41.7% with traditional protocols (relative improvement: 64%, p < 0.005). The platform significantly increased median duration in therapeutic range (57.1% versus (vs) 30.0%, p < 0.00001) and reduced missed doses by 78% (0.23 vs 1.04 doses per course, p < 0.00001), indicating better drug management without affecting clinical outcomes [27]. The incorporation of AI technologies into pharmacy operations mitigates various operational challenges and improves service quality and patient safety. These applications encompass inventory management, prescription processing, clinical consultations, and patient education, illustrating the extensive applicability of AI in the pharmacy practice domain. The AI-enhanced automated dispensing systems provide greater accuracy and efficiency for the preparation and distribution of medications. These systems integrate barcode verification, image recognition, and predictive analytics to reduce dispensing errors and enhance workflow efficiency [28]. Advanced systems can predict maintenance requirements and identify potential mechanical issues before they affect operations. Inventory management applications use AI algorithms to optimize stock levels, forecast demand patterns, and reduce waste from expired medications. These systems analyze historical dispensing data, seasonal patterns, and local health trends to produce precise demand forecasts and automated ordering recommendations [28]. The incorporation of supply chain analytics facilitates enhanced inventory management, which can adjust to fluctuations in demand and supply interruptions. The integration of clinical decision support within pharmacy information systems offers real-time alerts and recommendations for prescription handling and clinical consultation [29]. These systems can identify potential issues related to drug therapy, propose alternative therapeutic options, and offer evidence-based recommendations for patient counseling. The incorporation of AI facilitates advanced clinical assessments that consider various patient factors and treatment objectives. Patient educational applications use AI to produce individualized counseling suggestions based on individual patient characteristics, health literacy levels, and drug complexity. Beyond content development, these systems detect high-risk patients who need more help and offer tailored instructional resources that suit individual learning requirements. Implementation studies show that AI-based treatments have a significant clinical effect, with medication adherence increasing by 6.7% overactive intervention controls and 32.7% compared to standard care practices [30]. The successful integration of AI in pharmaceutical care necessitates sophisticated machine-learning frameworks capable of managing various data types and generating interpretable clinical predictions. These architectures must consider the unique characteristics of pharmaceutical data, including its high dimensionality, regulatory constraints, and the necessity for explainable outcomes. The use of machine learning architectures in antiviral drug discovery is highlighted by the Ebola virus (EBOV) therapeutic research, where Bayesian models have shown higher prediction accuracy. A landmark study trained Bayesian, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and recursive partitioning forest algorithms on 868 compounds using 5-fold cross-validation. The compounds were classed as active (IC50 < 50 μM) or inactive [31]. The Bayesian approach achieved optimum discrimination (ROC = 0.86), identifying 3 new anti-EBOV candidates from a library of 2,320 compounds. Tilorone, quinacrine, and pyronaridine tetraphosphate had EC50 values of 350 nanometer (nM), 420 nM, and 230 nM, which were much lower than chloroquine (4.0 μM) [32]. Contemporary techniques have evolved to include ensemble methods that include random forest, artificial neural networks, and logistic regression, resulting in Pearson coefficients of 0.83–0.98. These models have been implemented as web servers (such as EBOLApred, which has an accuracy of 0.89), making AI-driven drug prediction capabilities more accessible [31]. Advanced deep learning architectures offer greater pattern recognition skills, which are critical for pharmaceutical applications. The Machine Learning-based Functional Group-Aware Graph Convolutional Network (MFAGCN) predicts antimicrobial effectiveness by combining 3 molecular fingerprint modalities Molecular Access System (MACCS), PubChem, and Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints with graph convolutional representations. The MFAGCN outperformed baseline models across 2 public datasets, using attention processes to weight surrounding node information differently, while also discovering new drugs active against multidrug-resistant infections such as Acinetobacter baumannii [33]. The design includes structural similarity analysis to avoid the rediscovery of existing antibiotics, allowing for quick screening of antimicrobial candidates while providing mechanistic insights into functional group contributions [28]. In addition, Google DeepMind's AlphaFold3 (AF3), created in collaboration with Isomorphic Labs, accurately predicts 3 dimensional structures and intermolecular interactions for over 200 million proteins. The capabilities of AF3 extend across all biomolecular classes including proteins, ligands, nucleic acids, and ions resulting in reducing structure determination timelines from years to hours, revolutionizing target identification, rational drug design, and precision therapeutics development [34]. Unsupervised learning approaches allow for the discovery of clinically different sub-phenotypes among heterogeneous disease populations, evidenced in septic shock stratification. A 2-step cluster analysis of 10,462 critical care patients with septic shock revealed 2 subtypes with distinct biochemical severity profiles. Cluster 1 (n = 5,355) had significantly higher lactate (2.40 vs 2.20 mmol/L, p < 0.001), creatinine (1.39 vs 1.20 mg/dL, p < 0.001), and sequential organ failure assessment scores (7.05 vs 6.76, p < 0.001) than cluster 2 (n = 5,107), necessitating more frequent mechanical ventilation (46.1% vs 42.2%, p < 0.001) and renal replacement therapy (6.6% vs 5.2%, p = 0.005). Paradoxically, both clusters had similar death rates (15.4% vs 15.8%, p = 0.615) and hospital stays, indicating phenotypic complexity that defies traditional severity rating and supports phenotype-guided therapy methods [35]. Reinforcement learning methods help to optimize consecutive treatment choices by iteratively refining therapeutic strategies. A multicenter randomized controlled study (NCT04642378) assessed an AI-based insulin clinical decision support system (iNCDSS) in 149 hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes (mean age 64.2 ± 12.0 years, 56.4% male). The AI system outperformed senior endocrinology physicians in glycemic control, with a mean time in target glucose range (70–180 mg/dL) of 76.4% ± 16.4% compared to 73.6% ± 16.8% with physician-directed therapy over 5 days (difference: 2.7%, 95% CI: -2.7% to 8%, meeting prespecified noninferiority margin of 6 percentage points). Adverse event rates, including hypoglycemia episodes, were similar between groups. Clinician satisfaction surveys revealed that the system offered clear, time-efficient, and safe decision assistance [36]. Successful AI implementation in pharmaceutical care necessitates advanced data integration capabilities that synchronize various sources, while ensuring quality and adherence to regulatory standards. Organizations engage with various types of data, including structured clinical data, unstructured documents, imaging data, and real-world evidence, each of which poses distinct integration challenges. Effective data integration techniques are critical for pharmaceutical AI applications that use electronic health records. Patient portal systems, such as Epic's MyChart system, which was examined in a community hospital environment (n = 5,651 participants), highlight substantial data management problems that affect AI system development. Key barriers include incomplete user adoption (59% citing a lack of awareness), registration complexities (32% of non-users), and limited data accessibility, with users complaining about insufficient medical history and test result access. These integration limitations jeopardize the data completeness and representativeness necessary for effective AI algorithm training. Successful data management strategies must address user education (54% of non-users are willing to adopt with improved information), extend data accessible features, and assure full longitudinal data acquisition to enable pharmaceutical decision support systems [37]. In real-world evidence, integration from electronic health records necessitates comprehensive data quality methods to assure compliance with pharmaceutical research and regulatory applications. Flatiron Health's oncology platform demonstrates comprehensive quality assurance in 4 key dimensions: i) relevance (availability, sufficiency, and representativeness), ii) reliability (accuracy validated through external benchmarking, iii) completeness assessed against source documentation), provenance (auditable transformation metadata), iv) and timeliness. The platform collects longitudinal data from academic and community cancer clinics around the USA and validates it using methodologies such as conformance checks, consistency verification, and plausibility evaluations. These systematic curation procedures translate heterogeneous EHR data into research-grade real-world evidence that can be used to make regulatory decisions and conduct comparative effectiveness studies [38]. Management strategies involve the application of federated learning methods that facilitate model training on distributed datasets without the need for centralized data sharing. The TriNetX network facilitates collaboration among over 120 healthcare organizations to train AI models while ensuring that data privacy is upheld [39]. Pharmaceutical companies, such as Novartis, use differential privacy techniques to disseminate insights while safeguarding patient information, thereby ensuring regulatory compliance in over 15 countries. The findings of this comprehensive review both align with and extend previous assessments of AI in pharmaceutical care. Consistent with earlier systematic reviews, our analysis confirms machine learning's superiority over traditional computational methods in drug discovery, with current evidence showing prediction accuracies of 86–98% (ROC = 0.86–0.98) [7,8]. However, this review reveals that real-world implementation faces more substantial barriers than previously recognized in technology-focused literature from 2019–2020. Three critical gaps emerge when comparing current evidence to earlier reviews. First, while Álvarez-Machancoses and Fernández-Martínez [47] emphasized AI's potential to accelerate drug discovery timelines, they did not quantify implementation obstacles. Our analysis identifies that data quality challenges with accuracy as low as 30% in some healthcare datasets and algorithm drift affecting 30–50% of deployed systems within 12–24 months represent more formidable barriers than technical algorithm development [39,48]. Second, the health equity dimension was largely absent from pre-2021 pharmaceutical AI literature. Recent evidence synthesized in this review reveals systematic algorithmic bias, with performance variations of 8–12% across racial and ethnic groups and Black patients receiving lower risk scores compared to equally ill White patients in algorithms used by over 200 million individuals [42]. These findings indicate that implementation success requires proactive fairness auditing rather than assuming algorithmic neutrality. Third, whereas earlier reviews focused on isolated applications within single pharmaceutical care domains, our comprehensive analysis reveals the importance of integrated implementation frameworks. The KSA vision 2030 case study demonstrates that coordinated strategies combining infrastructure investment ($64 billion), National Platform for Health Information Exchange Services (NPHIES), workforce development, and regulatory alignment achieve broader adoption than fragmented initiatives reported in other regions. These comparisons highlight the maturation of pharmaceutical AI research from purely technical assessments to implementation science addressing sociotechnical challenges, regulatory requirements, and health equity implications. Pharmaceutical AI systems require ongoing validation beyond initial implementation, as algorithm drift, a progressive decline in predictive precision influences 30–50% of medication-related AI systems within 12–24 months due to temporal variations in prescribing patterns, formulary modifications, and changing patient demographics [40]. Healthcare institutions estimate allocating 0.5–1.0 full-time equivalent staff to continual monitoring of deployed algorithms, including prediction accuracy, alarm override frequencies, and clinical outcomes [41]. However, there are no defined revalidation triggers, with institutions using 10% performance degradation thresholds or absolute metric changes such as area under the curve decreases greater than 0.05 [42]. Importantly, algorithmic fairness and the consequences for health equality are not well captured by aggregate performance indicators. Systematic subgroup analyses indicate that medication adherence prediction algorithms exhibit AUC variations ranging from 0.08 to 0.12 across different racial and ethnic groups, showing poorer performance in minority groups [43,44]. A significant study examining an algorithm utilized by over 200 million patients revealed that Black patients received lower risk scores compared to equally ill White patients. This variance arose from the use of healthcare costs as an indication for health needs, failing to consider systemic inequities in utilization [43,44]. Socioeconomic factors such as insurance status, neighborhood deprivation indices, and health literacy levels are correlated with prediction errors, which may exacerbate variations through biased recommendations [44]. Advanced validation protocols that include adversarial testing expose vulnerabilities not identified by traditional methods. A systematic review identified 15 medications with race-based guidelines for initiation and monitoring, along with 42 risk calculators using race as a predictor variable, despite concerns that incorporating race in clinical algorithms may exacerbate health disparities and promote inappropriate race-based medicine [45]. An evaluation framework for assessing algorithmic fairness in hospital readmission models identified trade-offs between bias reduction and overall performance as risk thresholds were modified. The Hemoglobin, Oncology service, Sodium level, Procedure, Index admission, number of admission in the previous Twelve months, Admission to the hospital and Length of stay (HOSPITAL) score and retrained Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services measure exhibited the lowest racial bias among the models tested, while the default thresholds of all models were deemed reasonable for achieving a balance between accuracy and fairness [46]. Engaging stakeholders from affected communities during the validation design phase ensures that fairness assessments reflect patient values in addition to technical metrics [47]. This approach fosters the development of comprehensive frameworks that address performance degradation and equity implications throughout the lifecycles of AI systems. The implementation of AI in pharmaceutical care involves technical, organizational, and regulatory challenges that necessitate comprehensive strategies addressing data quality, system integration, workforce development, and change management. Challenges related to data quality significantly affect the performance of healthcare AI systems. Missing values and data inconsistencies are common in healthcare datasets and can negatively impact model performance [48]. Some healthcare datasets have been reported to have data quality as low as 30% accuracy due to invalid codes and erroneous laboratory data [49]. The implementation of data quality improvement strategies, such as imputation methods and anomaly detection, has yielded measurable enhancements in model reliability. One study indicated that data completeness increased from approximately 91% to nearly 100% after preprocessing interventions [48]. Healthcare organizations that adopt automated data cleansing, real-time validation systems, and standardized data formats demonstrate enhanced data accuracy and reliability, thereby minimizing human error and promoting patient safety [50]. System integration presents marked infrastructure challenges. Legacy pharmacy systems frequently exhibit deficiencies in application programming interface capabilities essential for AI integration. The CVS Health's $2 billion technology modernization facilitated real-time AI medication screening in over 9,000 locations [51]. Phased rollouts are essential. Johns Hopkins effectively implemented the Targeted Real-time Early Warning System (TREWS) for AI sepsis detection, initially deployed in 2018, achieving an area AUC of 0.97 and significantly lowering mortality rates by identifying sepsis symptoms hours earlier than conventional methods [52]. Workforce development necessitates the integration of technical and clinical training. Training must emphasize the constraints of artificial intelligence. Untrained clinicians utilize AI recommendations 60% more frequently than trained clinicians, leading to inappropriate medication adjustments. The Cleveland Clinic implemented an AI-powered medication therapy management system in 2021, developed in collaboration with IBM Watson Health. Successful implementation requires interdisciplinary teams and strong governance. The AI healthcare initiatives generally involve teams comprising clinicians, data scientists, information technology specialists, legal advisors, regulatory authorities, and quality assurance experts, supported by dedicated project management to facilitate collaboration among stakeholders. Introduction › Ethical considerations

The AI systems may exacerbate healthcare disparities due to biased training data and insufficient representation of diverse populations. Effective mitigation requires the use of various datasets, algorithms that account for bias and validation across different population subgroups. Equity considerations include geographic access, language barriers, and economic factors influencing the availability of AI [61]. The black-box AI systems are incompatible with clinical decision-making requirements explainable AI methods offer visualizations of algorithmic reasoning and generate natural-language explanations [62]. Regulatory requirements highlight the necessity of interpretability in safety-critical applications, whereas patient communication requires appropriate technical detail. Conventional consent frameworks are insufficient for AI applications. Dynamic consent facilitates detailed permission for individual applications and permits adjustments to preferences. Variations in international privacy regulations necessitate the navigation of General Data Protection Regulation, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, and other emerging laws across different jurisdictions [63,64]. Federated Learning facilitates collaborative model training without the need for centralized data sharing, effectively addressing privacy concerns, while allowing for the aggregation of diverse datasets [65]. This method enhances the pace of research in rare diseases and pharmacovigilance, letting smaller organizations leverage advancements in AI. Genomic integration is a substantial advancement in which AI evaluates intricate genomic data to develop personalized treatment approaches. Pharmacogenomics applications predict individual drug responses by analyzing genetic variants, whereas multi-omics strategies integrate proteomics and metabolomics to obtain comprehensive molecular profiles [66]. Global standard harmonization mitigates regulatory fragmentation via initiatives from the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) and the WHO. Technical standardization efforts emphasize data interoperability and algorithmic validation with the aim of balancing regulatory consistency with the promotion of innovation [67]. Key recommendations for successful implementation include: i) establishing interdisciplinary teams combining clinical, technical, and ethical expertise; ii) adopting explainable AI frameworks to ensure clinical decision transparency; iii) implementing continuous monitoring for algorithm performance and fairness; engaging in international standardization efforts; iv) and maintaining systematic attention to health equity throughout the AI lifecycle. The evidence synthesized in this review demonstrates that AI integration in pharmaceutical care is achievable through coordinated efforts addressing technological, organizational, regulatory, and ethical dimensions simultaneously. In conclusion, the integration of AI in pharmaceutical care demonstrates transformative potential across drug discovery, clinical trials, personalized medicine, and medication therapy management. This comprehensive review of evidence from 2018–2025 reveals measurable improvements: drug discovery prediction accuracies of 86–98%, clinical trial recruitment efficiency improvements of 80%, medication adherence increases of 32.7% over standard care, and diagnostic accuracies exceeding 85% in multiple domains. The global AI drug discovery market trajectory from $1.5 billion (2023) to projected $11.8 billion (2030) reflects substantial industry investment and confidence. However, successful implementation requires addressing 5 critical challenges identified in this review: i) data quality concerns, with accuracy as low as 30% in some healthcare datasets; ii) system integration barriers with legacy infrastructure requiring substantial investment; iii) workforce development needs, with skills gaps affecting initial adoption; iv) regulatory compliance challenges across jurisdictions; and v) ethical considerations, particularly algorithmic bias showing 8–12% performance variations across demographic groups. Algorithm drift affecting 30–50% of deployed systems within 12–24 months necessitates continuous monitoring protocols, with institutions allocating 0.5–1.0 full-time equivalent staff for validation. The KSA's vision 2030 initiative demonstrates that coordinated national strategies combining $64 billion infrastructure investment, NPHIES platform integration, and culturally aligned implementation frameworks can accelerate AI adoption while maintaining ethical standards and addressing regional healthcare needs. Future directions emphasize federated learning for privacy-preserving multi-institutional collaboration, genomic integration through multi-omics approaches for precision therapeutics, and global regulatory harmonization through WHO and ICH initiatives. Organizations that proactively address implementation challenges, particularly establishing continuous validation protocols and implementing fairness audits across demographic subgroups, will be optimally positioned to leverage AI's transformative potential.
