Abstract

Digital health technologies hold significant potential for reducing global healthcare disparities. Large language models (LLMs) offer new opportunities to enhance access to culturally specific healthcare, including traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). This study evaluated the diagnostic and treatment performance of seven publicly available LLMs using a real-world acupuncture case, comparing their outputs with three professional acupuncturists across five domains: Western diagnosis, TCM diagnosis, acupoint selection, needling technique, and herbal medicine. Twenty-eight expert evaluators from China, South Korea, and the United States assessed the responses using a multilingual survey. LLMs performed comparably to acupuncturists in Western diagnosis and showed variable performance in TCM-specific tasks. GPT-4o, Qwen 2.5 Max, and Doubao 1.5 Pro demonstrated the highest alignment with expert evaluations, particularly in TCM diagnosis and acupoint selection. These findings highlight the potential of general-purpose LLMs to support culturally grounded medical decision-making and reduce access barriers in TCM care systems.

Introduction

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) offers therapeutic approaches through herbal medicine, acupuncture, and Qigong to support neurological disorders1,2, mental health3,4, pain relief5,6, women’s health and fertility7,8, cancer care9,10, chronic conditions11,12, disease prevention13,14, and overall wellness15,16. These approaches emphasize personalized treatment based on individual patient assessment, syndrome differentiation17, and many other disorders/conditions. However, global adoption of TCM faces significant barriers. Its philosophical foundations, such as Yin-Yang balance18, five elements, and Qi flow19, are deeply rooted in Chinese philosophy, making them difficult to translate into global and modern scientific biomedical models20. Additionally, the diagnostic approach in TCM also differs significantly from modern Western medicine; while Western protocols typically follow standardized pathways, TCM adopts a more personalized and nonlinear diagnosis approach21. These philosophical and methodological differences create substantial and fundamental cultural and systemic barriers to global reach and adoption of TCM, affecting broader health equity. Populations unfamiliar with the cultural context of TCM struggle to fully understand and trust its practices, limiting their access to its potential benefits. Addressing these cultural disparities is essential for transforming TCM into a globally inclusive healthcare option. Recent advances in digital health technologies, particularly large language models (LLMs), present an innovative opportunity for making TCM more globally accessible22–25. LLMs have proven valuable in clinical decision support, diagnostics, and medical documentation26–29, while also playing an expanding role in medical education30–32. Furthermore, their multilingual capabilities and adaptability enable more inclusive digital health solutions, ensuring accessibility for diverse populations through culturally and linguistically tailored applications33,34. However, the use of LLMs for TCM presents unique challenges due to its complex philosophical and linguistic nuances. For example, adapting LLMs to TCM-specific tasks such as syndrome differentiation, acupuncture point selection, and herbal medicine recommendations, require LLMs to bridge significant cultural and theoretical gaps35. Thus, successfully integrating LLMs into TCM practice demands not only technological advancements but also a deeper understanding of cross-cultural medical translation capabilities. This study aims to evaluate the performance of LLMs in diagnosing and recommending treatments for a complex real-world acupuncture case study by comparing the performance of seven general-purpose LLMs with professional acupuncturists. Specifically, we test three hypotheses: (1) can LLMs provide Western and TCM diagnoses consistent with the assessments from professional acupuncturists, (2) can LLMs recommend appropriate acupuncture points and techniques, and (3) can LLMs suggest suitable herbal medicine combinations. As a pilot benchmarking effort, this study provides foundational insights into the capabilities and limitations of LLMs within culturally embedded medical systems such as TCM. By exploring how scalable AI tools can bridge linguistic, cultural, and geographic gaps in medical knowledge delivery, our findings underscore the potential of LLMs to promote digital health equity and expand global access to culturally rooted care.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a case-based evaluation of seven publicly accessible LLMs and compared their diagnostic and treatment recommendations with those of experienced acupuncturists using a real-world TCM case. We found that (1) certain LLMs, including GPT-4o, Qwen 2.5 Max, and Doubao 1.5 Pro, demonstrated performance comparable to licensed acupuncturists in specific domains such as TCM diagnosis, acupoint selection, and herbal treatment planning, and (2) performance varied substantially across models and tasks, indicating inconsistency in current capabilities. These findings suggest that general-purpose LLMs possess foundational potential to support culturally embedded healthcare systems like TCM. With targeted refinement, they may serve as scalable and cost-effective tools to expand access to high-quality, culturally relevant care, particularly in settings where linguistic, geographic, or infrastructural barriers limit the availability of trained practitioners. Our findings demonstrated exceptional performance in the Western medical diagnostic capabilities of LLMs. In addition, we found that three models, GPT, Qwen, and Doubao, achieved performance comparable to human acupuncturists in TCM diagnosis and acupoint selection, while showing more variability in needling techniques and herbal formulation. This finding is consistent with broader evaluations where LLMs have shown high accuracy in structured and rule-based assessments, such as medical board examinations36–38. These models exhibit a robust understanding of standardized protocols and diagnostic criteria, enabling them to deliver promising results that match or even surpass human expertise in some scenarios. Additionally, the multilingual processing capabilities of LLMs also present another significant advantage in global healthcare applications. LLMs demonstrated proficiency in aligning with clinical guidelines across linguistic barriers, making accurate recommendations in various languages39–41. This capacity for multilingual processing promotes equitable access to medical knowledge and improves global healthcare accessibility. While LLMs like GPT, Qwen, and Doubao are comparable to professional acupuncturists in multiple tasks in several key domains, their performance was not uniform across all assessment criteria. Although GPT, a Western-developed model, demonstrated strong performance, Chinese-developed LLMs, like Qwen and Doubao, outperformed many other models in handling culturally sensitive TCM tasks. This suggests that both model architecture and culturally tailored training datasets can enhance performance in areas requiring deep cultural adaptability. Our results have important implications for addressing longstanding barriers in globalization of TCM. TCM has historically faced challenges in globalization due to its complex theoretical foundations, cultural nuances, and differences in educational systems worldwide42,43. These barriers make it difficult for international practitioners and students to access and integrate TCM knowledge into their practices. LLMs provide innovative potential to address these challenges and foster health equity through several processes. First, the ability of LLMs to process TCM concepts and provide recommendations across multiple languages addresses linguistic barriers and enables broader engagement with TCM practices33,35,44. The strong performance of culturally adapted models like GPT, Qwen, and Doubao demonstrates the potential for maintaining Asian cultural authenticity while increasing accessibility of TCM. Furthermore, LLMs’ scalability and cost-effectiveness, and digital nature make them powerful tools for democratizing TCM education and distribution, particularly in regions where access to trained acupuncturists is limited42,45,46. Nevertheless, LLMs still face significant challenges in handling culturally sensitive and complex tasks within TCM. Our analysis revealed significant performance disparities among LLMs in TCM-specific tasks. Notably, Gemini, LLaMA, Claude, and ERNIE were associated with lower performance compared to human acupuncturists in specialized areas such as acupoint selection and needling techniques. Additionally, Gemini and Claude underperformed relative to professional acupuncturists in herbal formulation tasks. In contrast, Qwen and Doubao, along with GPT, consistently achieved performance levels comparable to acupuncturists in culturally nuanced tasks, particularly in TCM diagnosis and acupoint selection, while still showing variability in other domains such as needling techniques and herbal formulation. These models demonstrated better performance than other models, likely due to their training with more regionally and culturally relevant data. These performance variations may be primarily influenced by model architecture47, training data48, fine-tuning49, domain specialization50, and reasoning ability51. The comparable performance of GPT demonstrates the impact of its advanced architecture, large-scale parameters, and reasoning capabilities, allowing it to achieve high diagnostic accuracy even without explicit cultural optimization52–55. The stronger performance of Chinese-developed models (Qwen and Doubao) indicates that they might benefit from targeted optimization for Chinese medical corpora, improving text comprehension and diagnostic reasoning56. Their integration of Chinese clinical guidelines and traditional decoction provides a distinct advantage in culturally sensitive tasks57, with reasoning patterns that more closely align with those of Chinese physicians. Our findings highlight fundamental challenges in applying LLMs to TCM practices, as the diagnostic framework relies heavily on complex subjective and culturally embedded methods such as pulse analysis, tongue observation, and syndrome differentiation17,56,58. These holistic and dynamic approaches and elements pose additional challenges for LLMs that are primarily trained on Western medical datasets57,59. Furthermore, the lack of large, standardized datasets specific to TCM exacerbates these issues, leaving many LLMs unable to fully replicate the expertise required for culturally sensitive TCM practices56. Thus, some LLMs failed to match the performance of human practitioners in tasks that require cultural and contextual sensitivity, particularly in TCM diagnosis and treatment. Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the evaluation was based on a single reported clinical case involving amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). While this allowed for a controlled comparison across multiple domains, it may limit the generalizability of the findings. Broader validation using diverse case types and disease categories is needed to determine LLM adaptability in more varied clinical settings. Second, all LLM responses were generated from structured case texts without real-time interaction. This non-interactive format does not replicate the real-world process in which a practitioner interprets a patient’s natural expressions and then translates them into TCM terms. In clinical settings, TCM diagnosis often depends on spontaneous patient language, behavioral cues, and subtle contextual factors that are difficult to capture in standardized written formats. In addition, while this study evaluated acupoint selection and needling techniques, it did not assess treatment implementation or patient response, both of which are critical to therapeutic outcomes in acupuncture. Future research should incorporate more interactive methodologies, such as clinical simulations or real patient interviews using AI-Agent, to better approximate real-world diagnostic workflows in TCM and evaluate how LLMs perform under more naturalistic and context-rich conditions. Third, while we used a five-domain scoring rubric developed through expert consensus, the framework has not been externally validated. Although predefined criteria were applied, some subjectivity in evaluator judgments may remain. Formal assessment of inter-rater reliability and the development of standardized evaluation tools tailored to TCM practice are important next steps. Fourth, the models examined were general-purpose LLMs that had not been fine-tuned on medical or TCM-specific corpora. The results should therefore be interpreted as a baseline assessment of current capabilities. The emergence of newer models after our study, such as DeepSeek and Gork, highlights the need for continuous evaluation as the field rapidly evolves. It is expected that future domain-specialized models trained on curated TCM datasets will show improved contextual understanding and clinical accuracy. To address these limitations and support the responsible integration of AI into TCM practice, future research should prioritize the development of high-quality, domain-specific corpora. These should include classical TCM texts, modern clinical guidelines, trial data, and real-world patient records to align model outputs with TCM theory, language, and diagnostic reasoning. Expanding datasets to include diverse cases and longitudinal, multi-case evaluations will be essential for testing performance across varied clinical settings. Furthermore, incorporating AI agents and multimodal diagnostic inputs, such as tongue images or pulse waveforms, could allow the AI to communicate with the patient directly to mimic real clinical settings, enhancing the realism and clinical utility of LLMs. Also, expert-in-the-loop learning approaches would allow continuous refinement based on practitioner feedback, while explainable AI tools can support transparency and clinician trust. From a system-level perspective, cross-cultural validation and prospective clinical trials will be necessary to determine whether LLM-informed TCM care improves diagnostic accuracy, healthcare access, and patient outcomes, particularly in underserved or resource-constrained environments. These efforts will help define the role of LLMs within global digital health ecosystems. Ultimately, advancing the use of LLMs in TCM requires close collaboration among practitioners, AI developers, and policy experts. By fostering culturally sensitive, inclusive, and evidence-based innovation, LLMs can contribute meaningfully to the mission of digital health equity and support more accessible, high-quality care for all. In summary, this study establishes an initial benchmark for evaluating the diagnostic and treatment capabilities of LLMs in TCM. Using a standardized clinical case, we found that while general-purpose LLMs demonstrated performance comparable to licensed acupuncturists in Western medical diagnosis, their capabilities varied considerably across TCM-specific domains. Notably, GPT-4o, Qwen 2.5 Max, and Doubao 1.5 Pro exhibited the highest alignment with licensed acupuncturists in TCM diagnosis, acupoint selection, and needling techniques. These findings highlight the potential of LLMs as accessible and scalable tools to support TCM care, particularly in settings with limited healthcare resources. Future research should prioritize domain-specific fine-tuning, AI-agent, multimodal input integration, and real-world clinical validation to enhance both performance and reliability. As LLMs continue to evolve, their thoughtful integration into TCM practice holds significant potential to bridge traditional medical knowledge with modern healthcare systems, contributing to a more equitable and culturally inclusive digital health future.

Methods

A clinically complex ALS case integrating Western and TCM diagnostic elements was used. Seven LLMs and three licensed acupuncturists responded to three standardized prompts. Responses were evaluated across five domains: Western diagnosis, TCM diagnosis, acupoint selection, needling technique, and herbal medicine. Ratings were provided by 28 blinded TCM experts using a 5-point Likert scale. The LLMs included GPT-4o (OpenAI), Gemini 1.5 Flash (Google), LLaMA 3.2 (Meta), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic), ERNIE 3.5 (Baidu), Qwen 2.5 Max (Alibaba), and Doubao 1.5 Pro (ByteDance). Methods › Study design

This survey-based study evaluated the diagnostic and treatment recommendations of seven LLMs and three acupuncturists, assessed independently by 28 professional acupuncturists. A clinically complex and educationally representative case of ALS was selected from a peer-reviewed TCM case report60. ALS was chosen for its neurological complexity, integration of Western and TCM diagnostic elements, and relevance to acupuncture education and practice in China61. These characteristics made it a suitable case example for benchmarking LLM performance across multiple diagnostic and treatment domains. The case was reformatted to include detailed patient history, physical examination findings, and TCM diagnosis to provide uniform inputs (Supplementary Table 1). Each LLM and acupuncturist was treated as an independent “model” and assessed across five domains: diagnostic accuracy (Western and TCM), acupoint selection, needling technique, and herbal prescription appropriateness. Blinding and bias mitigation strategies were implemented to enhance the validity. A detailed overview of the study design is provided in Fig. 2. Methods › Recommendations by LLMs

Given the rapidly expanding landscape of LLMs, selecting models for evaluation requires balancing representation across leading international and domestic developers with the practical need to limit the time and cognitive demands on expert evaluators. Seven generative LLMs were selected for evaluation based on the following criteria: (1) representation of leading international or Chinese developers, including models designed for English and Chinese language tasks; (2) public availability through free or low-cost user interfaces to ensure accessibility and reproducibility; and (3) general-purpose design without fine-tuning in medicine or TCM, allowing for an unbiased evaluation of baseline performance in TCM diagnostic and treatment recommendations. The models included in this study were GPT-4o (OpenAI), Gemini 1.5 Flash (Google), LLaMA 3.2 (Meta), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic), ERNIE 3.5 (Baidu), Qwen 2.5 Max (Alibaba), and Doubao 1.5 Pro (ByteDance). Detailed specifications are provided in Supplementary Table 2. All models were accessed through publicly available user interfaces without any external plugins or real-time internet access. Therefore, all generated responses were based solely on the models’ pre-trained knowledge available at the time of data collection. None of the models had been specifically trained or fine-tuned on medical or TCM data. Methods › Recommendations by licensed acupuncturists

To establish a benchmark for evaluating LLMs, experienced TCM acupuncturists were recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) possession of a valid license as a TCM or acupuncture practitioner; (2) a minimum of eight years of clinical acupuncture experience; (3) active clinical practice in a hospital or an academic acupuncture department. Two acupuncturists met these criteria: Acupuncturist 1, MSc, an attending TCM physician at a hospital in Guangzhou, China, with about ten years of clinical experience, and Acupuncturist 2, PhD, a full professor in the Department of Acupuncture, chief physician, and senior clinician at a hospital in Chengdu, China, with more than ten years of clinical experience. The third set of clinical responses was extracted from the original peer-reviewed case report60, in which the author was identified as a clinician (with a Master’s degree) affiliated with Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with about 8 years of clinical experience. The participating acupuncturists were provided with standardized case information, identical to what was given to the LLMs through three prompts, including detailed clinical history, examination findings, and preliminary diagnoses. They were not aware of the study’s aims before completing their responses to minimize potential bias. Each acupuncturist was instructed to independently provide a written response, which was submitted via email. All participants were encouraged to complete their recommendations independently (see Supplementary Table 3).

Methods › Evaluation criteria

The recommendations were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale across five criteria: (1) accuracy of Western medical diagnoses, (2) accuracy of TCM diagnoses, (3) appropriateness of acupuncture point selection, (4) practicability of needling techniques, and (5) suitability of herbal medicine. Scoring definitions and thresholds were standardized to ensure consistency across evaluations (Table 5). Methods › Evaluator selection and survey implementation

Professional acupuncturists from China, South Korea, and the United States participated as independent evaluators to assess the diagnostic and treatment responses generated by both LLMs and licensed acupuncturists. Evaluators were selected based on the following criteria: (1) active involvement in either clinical care or academic activities (e.g., teaching or research) in acupuncture or TCM; and (2) affiliation with a TCM hospital/clinic or an accredited university/institution. Recruitment was conducted through email invitations distributed through professional networks, institutional collaborations of all co-authors, and academic referrals. As a pilot study designed to provide preliminary insights, a broad pool of qualified professionals was invited. Of those invited, 28 evaluators completed the full assessment within the designated timeframe and were included in the final analysis. To reduce bias, evaluators were not informed that some responses were generated by LLMs. They were told that the study aimed to examine variation in diagnostic and treatment recommendations from different sources. All evaluators were blinded to the study hypothesis, evaluation criteria, and the identities of the respondents. Each evaluator received a standardized survey translated into Chinese, Korean, or English through a forward-backward translation process to ensure accuracy and cultural equivalence. Evaluators independently reviewed anonymized diagnostic and treatment responses and rated them using predefined criteria. All responses were submitted anonymously through the secure online survey platform “Wenjuanxing”62 (www.wjx.cn) between November 1 and November 30, 2024.

Methods › Bias mitigation

To minimize bias, a standardized evaluation guide with clear scoring definitions and examples was provided to all evaluators. The order of responses was randomized, and all identifying labels (e.g., “AI” or “acupuncturist”) were removed to ensure impartial assessments. Independent evaluations were conducted under blinded conditions to prevent influence by prior responses.

Methods › Ethical approval

This study was reviewed and determined to be exempt from full ethical review by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (Protocol #: 2025P001014) under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2). The research involved no identifiable private information or direct interaction with human participants, and relied solely on a publicly available, de-identified clinical case and anonymous expert survey data. Informed consent was waived in accordance with relevant policy63,64. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable guidelines65.

Methods › Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.1.066. Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and as counts (n) with percentages for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons when applicable. Chi-square tests were conducted for categorical variables across multiple groups. All analyses were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Data visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.
