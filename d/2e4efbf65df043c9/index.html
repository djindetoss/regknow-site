Abstract

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer death in women, and toxicity, tumor heterogeneity and acquired resistance sustain the search for multi-targeted agents of natural origin. Aegle marmelos (L.) Correa is a traditional medicinal species whose molecular targets in breast cancer have not been defined systematically. Filtering 138 reported phytochemicals on drug-likeness, oral bioavailability and predicted toxicity left six leads, whose 252 predicted targets yielded ten hub genes by protein-protein interaction degree. Three classifiers trained on GSE42568 (104 tumor, 17 normal) under nested tenfold cross-validation reached pooled areas under the curve of 0.939-0.974, and their marker sets intersected in PPARG and ERBB2; a composite score adding differential expression, network degree and out-of-fold SHAP importance ranked BCL2 a clear second, with EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 clustered within 0.02 of one another for third, too close to call. All compounds were docked with six targets; marmeline was the strongest lead at four of the six receptors. Molecular dynamics and Hartree-Fock-level quantum-chemical characterization, completed for marmeline at PPARG against its own co-crystallized reference ligand, rosiglitazone, found both complexes stable over 200 ns (backbone RMSD 2.4-2.6 Å) with MM/PBSA binding free energies of -26.5 and -29.6 kcal mol-1, and confirmed marmeline's optimized geometry as a genuine minimum (zero imaginary frequencies; HOMO-LUMO gap 9.97 eV). Together, these findings identify PPARG as the best-supported target by almost every measure and marmeline as a structurally supported lead for experimental validation.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women and one of the principal causes of cancer-related death worldwide.1 Early detection, endocrine therapy and HER2-directed agents have improved outcomes, but a considerable proportion of patients still relapse, and treatment remains constrained by systemic toxicity, by the molecular heterogeneity between luminal, HER2-enriched and triple-negative subtypes, and by acquired resistance. These constraints motivate the search for agents that act on several disease-relevant nodes at once rather than on a single protein. Medicinal plants have supplied a substantial fraction of clinically used anticancer molecules, and their chemical diversity makes them well suited to multi-target intervention. Aegle marmelos (L.) Correa, commonly known as bael, is a member of the Rutaceae used extensively in Ayurveda and other traditional South Asian systems of medicine. Its fruit, leaves and bark contain coumarins, alkaloid amides, terpenoids, phenylethyl cinnamamides and carotenoids, and its extracts show antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in preclinical models.1 The molecular basis of these effects in breast cancer, however, remains poorly resolved, and the constituents responsible have not been matched systematically to the proteins they are likely to engage.1,2 Network pharmacology was developed to address exactly this problem. By assembling compound-target and disease-target relationships into a single network and analyzing its topology, it moves beyond the one-drug-one-target paradigm and exposes the multi-component,3 multi-target logic characteristic of herbal medicine. Its principal weakness is that hub selection is purely topological: a gene ranks highly because it is well connected in an interaction database, not because it distinguishes diseased from healthy tissue, and those databases are themselves biased towards intensively studied proteins.4 Supervised machine learning applied to independent transcriptomic data provides a natural corrective. If a topological hub is genuinely relevant to the disease, its expression should carry information that separates tumor from normal tissue, and that claim is directly testable.4 Regularized regression, ensemble tree methods and support-vector machines with recursive feature elimination make different assumptions about how features relate to the outcome,5 so agreement among them is stronger evidence than any single model; coupling them to nested cross-validation, permutation testing and Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values makes the resulting shortlist both statistically defensible and mechanistically interpretable.6 Here that combined strategy was applied to the reported phytochemicals of bael in breast cancer. Compounds were screened for drug-likeness, oral bioavailability and predicted toxicity; targets of the surviving leads were predicted and intersected with breast-cancer associated genes; hub genes were identified from the resulting protein–protein interaction network and functionally annotated; and those hubs were tested as classification features on an independent expression cohort using three machine-learning algorithms under nested cross-validation, with SHAP analysis establishing which genes drive the predictions. The consensus panel defines the receptors taken forward to molecular docking, molecular dynamics with molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) analysis, and Hartree–Fock-level quantum chemical characterization.

Methods › Phytochemical data and pharmacokinetic screening

Information on the phytochemical composition of Aegle marmelos (L.) Correa was obtained from IMPPAT (Indian Medicinal Plants, Phytochemistry and Therapeutics),7 a curated database of Indian medicinal plant constituents. One hundred and thirty-eight phytochemicals with IMPPAT identifiers and canonical SMILES strings were compiled, and the SMILES strings used for all subsequent steps. The drug-likeness (DL) score was obtained from the MolSoft molecular-property server (https://molsoft.com/mprop/),8,9 and the oral bioavailability (OB) score is the Abbott bioavailability score returned by SwissADME (https://www.swissadme.ch/).10 Across the 135 compounds carrying both scores, DL ranged from −1.96 to 0.95 and OB from 0.17 to 0.85; compounds with DL ≥ 0.30 and OB ≥ 0.30 were carried forward.

Methods › Toxicity prediction

Compounds surviving the DL and OB filters were submitted to the ProTox 3.0 server (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/)8 for prediction of carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity and cytotoxicity. Only compounds predicted inactive at all four endpoints were designated lead compounds and carried forward.

Methods › Target prediction and network construction

Putative human protein targets of the lead compounds were predicted from their canonical SMILES using SwissTargetPrediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/)11 with the organism restricted to Homo sapiens. Predicted targets were pooled and de-duplicated. Breast-cancer associated genes were retrieved from GeneCards12 and MalaCards13 using “breast cancer” as the query term, and the intersection with the pooled predicted targets was taken as the putative Aegle marmelos-breast cancer targets. Both databases were accessed on 1 June 2026. GeneCards returned 22 510 scored gene matches for the query, each carrying a relevance score; genes at score ≥ 20 (12 566 genes) were used for the reported intersection (Fig. 1A), leaving 229 of the 252 predicted targets. MalaCards returned 1703 scored gene-disease associations (assoc. score). No relevance threshold was applied to MalaCards: its 1703 genes were combined with the GeneCards ≥ 20 set by union, and this union was intersected with the 252-compound target pool to give the 229 retained targets. For this particular compound set MalaCards contributed no gene to the intersection beyond what GeneCards ≥ 20 already supplied the 229 targets are recovered from GeneCards alone so MalaCards' role here is to confirm rather than extend the GeneCards-derived list. These genes formed the node set of a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network built in STRING v12.0 (https://string-db.org/cgi/), with the organism set to Homo sapiens and the minimum interaction score at 0.4. The network was imported into Cytoscape 3.10 (ref. 14) and scored with the cytoHubba15 plug-in on node degree, the ten top-ranked genes being retained as hub genes. A merged compound-target-pathway network was assembled in the same environment and node degree computed with NetworkAnalyzer to rank compounds by targets engaged.

Methods › Gene ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment

The pathway-level roles of the hub genes were examined by over-representation analysis against Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Enrichment was run in R with clusterProfiler16 against the org.Hs.e.g.db annotation, covering the three GO ontologies as well as KEGG pathways. Raw p-values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure,17 and a term was called significant at an adjusted p-value below 0.05. The ten most significant terms in each category were drawn as bubble charts, with fold enrichment on the horizontal axis, bubble area proportional to the number of hub genes annotated, and color encoding −log10 of the adjusted p-value.

Methods › Machine learning on hub genes

The GSE42568 series16 was downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus. It holds 104 breast-tumor and 17 normal breast tissue samples profiled on the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 plus 2.0 array (GPL570). Robust multi-array average normalized log2 intensities were filtered to retain probes expressed above the overall median in at least 20% of samples and with variance above the 25th percentile. Because the series comprises microarray intensities rather than counts, differential expression was assessed with the empirical-Bayes moderated t-test in limma17 rather than with limma-voom; genes with a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1 were called differentially expressed. Hub genes were used as the candidate feature set for classification, with probes mapped through the platform annotation and multi-probe genes collapsed by the max-mean rule. For the genome-wide differential-expression analysis, genes represented by multiple probes were instead resolved by retaining the probe with the lowest adjusted p-value, since that analysis ranks probes by statistical significance rather than by absolute expression level. Three supervised classifiers were trained under nested cross-validation: L1-penalised (LASSO) logistic regression in glmnet, a 1500-tree random forest in randomForest, and a linear-kernel support-vector machine with recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) in e1071, the last following Liaw et al.19 An outer loop of ten stratified folds supplied the generalization estimate; inside each training partition a fivefold inner loop tuned the one free parameter of each model on AUC, repeated three times for SVM-RFE because the cohort holds only 17 normal samples. Stratification kept each fold close to the 104 : 17 tumour : normal ratio (folds 1–4 : 11 tumor/2 normal; folds 5–7 : 10/2; folds 8–10 : 10/1), and every preprocessing, feature-selection and tuning step was performed strictly within the training partition of each fold. Class imbalance was handled by balanced class weights and by balanced per-tree sampling in the random forest. Selection stability was quantified by bootstrap resampling over 100 replicates for SVM-RFE and, for all three models, by the proportion of outer folds in which a gene was retained, with 70% taken as the consensus threshold. A marker set was additionally defined for each model, independently of that threshold: for LASSO, every gene with a non-zero coefficient at λ*; for the random forest and for SVM-RFE, the five highest-ranked genes by mean decrease in Gini impurity and by bootstrap selection frequency respectively. The consensus panel is the intersection of these three marker sets. Random-forest importance was assessed by two complementary measures: Gini importance, the impurity reduction attributable to each gene summed across tree splits, which defines this model's marker set and is plotted in Fig. 5E; and permutation importance, the mean decrease in predictive accuracy when each gene's values are shuffled, evaluated here using out-of-bag observations excluded from fitting each tree rather than training-set predictions, because training-set evaluation substantially overstates the discriminative contribution of every predictor in a sufficiently deep, low-bias ensemble such as this 1500-tree forest. Gini importance does not depend on this choice and is reported here from the same out-of-bag-fitted forest for consistency. Discrimination was summarized by AUC, with accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 computed at a probability cut-off of 0.5 for LASSO and the random forest, and at the zero decision-margin threshold (predicted class given by the sign of the decision function, since this implementation does not produce calibrated class probabilities) for SVM-RFE, with bootstrap confidence intervals obtained on the pooled out-of-fold predictions. All analyses were run in R 4.6.1 with a fixed seed of 123, using limma for differential expression, glmnet18 for the penalized regression, randomForest19 for the ensemble, e1071 for the support-vector machine (recursive feature elimination following Guyon et al.20), caret21 for the resampling scaffolding and pROC22 for the ROC analysis; figures were drawn with ggplot2.23

Methods › Post hoc explainability of ML models

Each classifier was additionally subjected to a label-permutation test with N = 1000 permutations, in which the labels were shuffled, the fold structure left untouched, and the entire pipeline re-run; for tractability the random-forest null used 500 trees per forest. The empirical p-value was taken as (b + 1)/(N + 1), so that the smallest attainable value at N = 1000 is 9.99 × 10−4, and α was set at 0.05. Uncertainty in the performance estimates and selection frequencies was assessed by stratified bootstrap (1000 replicates, bias-corrected and accelerated intervals24), every replicate preserving the tumor-to-normal ratio. Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) 24 values were derived separately for each model, with every selection and tuning step confined to the inner folds so that no test-fold information could leak into the estimate. For the two linear models (LASSO, SVM-RFE) this is the closed-form Shapley value of a linear predictor, coefficient times mean-centred feature value; no closed-form solution exists for the random forest, so its attribution is instead approximated by restoring each feature in turn from the population-mean baseline to its observed value and taking the resulting change in predicted probability, a single-order approximation rather than an exact Shapley value, and it is reported and interpreted. Global importance was reported as the mean absolute SHAP value over all samples, and per-sample explanations were built from out-of-fold predictions. Because the three models return SHAP values on different absolute scales, the ranking was re-derived with each model normalized to its own maximum before averaging; the ordering of the four leading genes is unchanged.25

Methods › Integrated target prioritization

Four independent lines of evidence were combined into a single ranking of the hub genes: differential-expression evidence (the product of −log10 adjusted p-value and absolute log2 fold change), STRING PPI degree, mean feature stability across the three classifiers, and overall mean absolute SHAP value. Each component was min-max normalized within its column and the composite score taken as their unweighted mean. Because the differential-expression component is unbounded, that scaling compresses the remaining genes into its lower range wherever one gene is an extreme outlier, so the ordering below the top-ranked gene rests mainly on the other three components. The highest-ranking genes were nominated as the receptors for structure-based analysis.

Methods › Molecular docking

The protein targets prioritized by machine learning were assessed by molecular docking. Crystal structures of PPARG (PDB ID: 1ZGY), ESR1 (PDB ID: 3ERT), BCL2 (PDB ID: 6O0K), EGFR (PDB ID: 1M17), ERBB2 (PDB ID: 3RCD) and EP300 (PDB ID: 6V8K) were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), each chosen for its resolution and for carrying a co-crystallized ligand that defines the binding site and supplies a validated positive control. Co-crystallized ligands, crystallographic waters and heteroatoms were removed and the receptors were converted to PDBQT with MGLTools 1.5.7. Conformers of the six lead compounds were retrieved from PubChem, relaxed with the MMFF94 force field in RDKit and converted to PDBQT; the six reference ligands were taken directly from their receptors so that their crystallographic coordinates are preserved unaltered. For each receptor, the docking grid box was centered on the position of its own co-crystallized reference ligand and sized to enclose it entirely, with every box edge between 22 and 25 Å so that the longest lead compound could rotate freely. Grid centers and box dimensions for all six receptors are listed in Table S6. Docking was performed in AutoDock Vina 1.1.2,26 retaining nine best poses per ligand, at an exhaustiveness of 32 and with a randomly generated seed for every run. The protocol was validated by re-docking each co-crystallized ligand into its own receptor, requiring top-ranked pose to reproduce crystallographic geometry approximately 2.0 Å as a symmetry-corrected root-mean-square deviation computed without superposition. Interactions of best-scoring pose were profiled in BIOVIA discovery studio visualizer.

Methods › Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

To assess the stability of the docked pose under solvated conditions, all-atom MD simulations were performed using GROMACS version 2025.3 on marmeline bound to PPARG, its highest-affinity complex,27 built from its top-ranked docked pose, alongside PPARG bound to its own co-crystallized reference ligand, rosiglitazone, simulated under the same protocol. The protein is described by the CHARMM36 force field28 and ligand topology generated through the CGenFF server.29 Each complex was solvated with TIP3P water in a cubic box with a minimum 1.0 nm margin, neutralized with counter-ions and brought to 0.15 M salt. Each system was energy-minimized by steepest descent, then equilibrated for 100 ps under NVT at 310 K with the V-rescale thermostat and 100 ps under NPT at 1 bar with the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, both with position restraints on the solute. Production runs of 200 ns used a 2 fs time step, LINCS constraints on bonds involving hydrogen and particle-mesh Ewald electrostatics with a 1.2 nm real-space cut-off, writing coordinates every 10 ps to give 20 000 frames. Trajectory analysis included protein backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), Cα root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), the per-frame count of ligand–receptor hydrogen bonds, principal component analysis (PCA) based on Cα atoms, with the free energy landscape (FEL) constructed over the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) and a dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) of Cα atomic displacements. Ligand pose stability was assessed separately using 21 snapshots per trajectory, extracted from 0 to 200 ns at 10 ns intervals and superimposed on the starting frame, recording the ligand heavy-atom RMSD, its center-of-mass displacement and its contact shell.

Methods › MM/PBSA binding free energy

Binding free energy was estimated from the equilibrated portion of each trajectory by the molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area method using gmx_MMPBSA version 1.6.3,30 with the internal Poisson–Boltzmann solver of sander and an ionic strength of 0.150 M. A total of 100 frames were extracted from the final 50 ns of each trajectory at 500 ps intervals over which the stability metrics remained relatively stable, and the binding free energy was decomposed into van der Waals, electrostatic,31 polar solvation and non-polar solvation contributions, reported as means with sample standard deviations.

Methods › Quantum chemical calculations

The electronic structure of the lead compound, marmeline, was characterized in Gaussian 16. The geometry was optimized in the gas phase at the Hartree–Fock level with the 6-311G(d,p) basis set and tight SCF convergence. The optimized geometry was confirmed as a genuine minimum by an analytic harmonic vibrational-frequency calculation at the same level, which returned no imaginary frequencies and supplied thermochemical corrections at 298.15 K and 1 atm. Frontier orbital energies were read from the converged wave function and the global reactivity descriptors derived from them through Koopmans' theorem, and the molecular electrostatic potential was mapped onto a total electron density isosurface in GaussView. The formatted checkpoint file was then analyzed in Multiwfn.32 The topology of the electron density was characterized by Bader's quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM), locating every critical point and evaluating the density, its Laplacian and the local energy densities at each, with the energy of each closed-shell contact estimated by the Espinosa relation. Intramolecular non-covalent interactions were mapped by the reduced density gradient method and rendered in VMD.33 The electron localization function and the localized orbital locator were evaluated on a plane through the molecule, and density of states convoluted from the orbital eigenvalues in GaussSum.34 All quantities reported below describe the isolated molecule in the gas phase.

Results › Screening of Aegle marmelos phytochemicals

The constituents of Aegle marmelos were assessed for pharmacokinetic suitability. Of the 138 phytochemicals retrieved from IMPPAT, 135 carried both a drug-likeness and a bioavailability score; DL ranged from −1.96 to 0.95 and OB from 0.17 to 0.85. Twelve compounds met both thresholds and went forward to toxicity prediction, of which six were excluded: alpha-carotene and beta-carotene as predicted mutagens, and beta-sitosterol, beta-sitosterol-beta-D-glucoside, clionasterol and daucosterol as predicted immunotoxins (Table S2). The remaining six compounds were predicted inactive at all four endpoints and were taken forward as lead compounds for this study (Tables 1 and S1).

Results › Target prediction and compound-target network construction

Target prediction returned 94 to 98 human targets per lead compound, giving 252 unique targets after pooling and de-duplication. Intersection with the scored, thresholded breast-cancer gene set (GeneCards score ≥ 20) returned 229 of these 252 targets (Fig. 1A). These genes were carried forward as the putative Aegle marmelos-breast cancer targets. To examine the molecular basis of reported effects of A. marmelos, a protein–protein interaction network of these targets was constructed in STRING and analyzed in Cytoscape (Fig. 2). Ranking the nodes by degree identified ten hub genes (Fig. 1B and Table S3): STAT3 was the most connected at 88, followed by EGFR (83), BCL2 and CASP3 tied at 82, ESR1 (78), PPARG (76), PTGS2 (66), EP300 (61), GSK3B (60) and ERBB2 (57). These ten were selected as candidate target set for machine-learning validation. Re-querying STRING with the ten hub genes alone returned a completely connected sub-network, all 45 possible pairs being joined (Fig. 1C), so the ten behave as a single interaction module and degree within it carries no information, which is why the ranking in Fig. 1B is taken from the full 229-node disease-filtered network and why topology alone was not used to select the final targets. Within GSE42568 itself the ten genes are strongly inter-correlated, which is why minimal-subset selectors retain different redundant partners.

Results › Gene ontology and KEGG pathway analyses

The possible biological activities and molecular mechanisms of Aegle marmelos targets were examined using GO and KEGG pathway analyses. Enrichment of the ten hub genes yielded 663 biological processes, 89 molecular functions, 26 cellular components and 76 KEGG pathways at an adjusted p-value below 0.05. The strongest GO signal came from regulation of miRNA transcription (four of ten hub genes, adjusted p = 2.92 × 10−5), and leading molecular functions were DNA-binding transcription factor binding, RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding and nuclear receptor binding. On the KEGG side the hub genes were enriched in microRNAs in cancer (seven of ten genes, adjusted p = 6.96 × 10−7), EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, prostate cancer and HIF-1 signalling (Tables 2, S5) and Fig. 3 plots the leading terms in each category. These annotations suggest that A. marmelos constituents could act on microRNA-regulatory, growth-factor-resistance and hypoxic signalling in breast cancer, a hypothesis that requires experimental testing.

Results › Compound-target-pathway network

To relate the lead compounds of Aegle marmelos to enriched pathways, a merged network was constructed joining each lead compound to its hub targets and each hub target to pathways it belongs to (Fig. 4). The network comprised 77 edges, 27 connecting six lead compounds to eight hub targets and 50 connecting those targets to the ten enriched KEGG pathways. Node degree was highest for marmeline, N-[2-hydroxy-2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethyl]cinnamamide and the prenyloxyphenyl-oxazolylpyridine, each engaging six targets, ahead of aegeline with five (Table S7); the busiest targets were ESR1 and PPARG, each engaged by five of the six compounds. Two of the three highest-degree compounds share a phenylethyl cinnamamide scaffold, as does aegeline, and the third carries the same prenyloxyphenyl group as marmeline, so a small set of structurally related constituents accounts for most of the network connectivity.

Results › Differential expression analysis

Of 54 675 probes on the array, 28 980 passed the expression and variance filters, and 6254 were differentially expressed 3410 up-regulated and 2844 down-regulated in tumor tissue at an adjusted p-value below 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change above 1 (Fig. S5), the empirical-Bayes prior degrees of freedom being 3.84 (Fig. S7). Among the ten hub genes (Table S4 and Fig. S6), four satisfied both criteria: PPARG showed by far the largest effect (log2 FC = −4.74, adjusted p = 1.3 × 10−25), followed by ERBB2 (+3.12, 1.5 × 10−8, the only up-regulated gene of the four), EGFR (−2.39, 3.3 × 10−8) and PTGS2 (−2.26, 6.7 × 10−9). BCL2, ESR1, EP300, GSK3B and CASP3 were significant by adjusted p-value but below the |log2 FC| > 1 fold-change threshold (CASP3 adjusted p = 0.041, log2 FC = 0.37), while STAT3 alone was not significant by either criterion. PPARG and BCL2, taken forward for structure-based analysis together with ESR1 as a previously characterized secondary target, were re-examined in GSE15852, an independent cohort of 43 tumor and 43 paired normal breast tissues on a different Affymetrix platform (GPL96, HG-U133A; GSE42568 used GPL570, HG-U133 Plus 2.0, whose probe content is a superset of GPL96's, so each hub gene's GSE42568-selected probe ID maps unambiguously onto GSE15852 without independent re-selection), not used in differential expression, feature selection, model development or target ranking; ERBB2, one of three genes tied for the remaining panel position (see Integrated target prioritization), was included in this replication check as the only one of the three carried over from the previous round, while EP300 and EGFR were not re-examined in GSE15852. Because each tumor sample in GSE15852 has a matched normal sample from the same patient, replication was assessed by a paired moderated t-test with patient as a blocking factor, rather than the unpaired design used for the discovery cohort. Using the same representative probe as GSE42568 for each gene, all four replicated in direction: PPARG (208510_s_at) log2 FC −1.99 (paired t = −10.20, adjusted p = 4.9 × 10−11) against −4.74 in the discovery cohort; ERBB2 (216836_s_at) +0.70 (t = 3.96, adjusted p = 3.5 × 10−3) against +3.12; ESR1 (215552_s_at) +0.51 (t = 3.49, adjusted p = 0.011) against +0.92; BCL2 (203684_s_at) −0.23 (t = −2.38, adjusted p = 0.086, not significant after correction) against −0.33. BCL2's weaker external signal mirrors its status as the smallest-effect gene of the four in GSE42568 itself, rather than indicating a discrepancy between cohorts; ERBB2, by contrast, replicates cleanly and significantly. This is a direction-of-effect replication in a paired design, not a re-evaluation of the frozen classifier panel, and GSE15852 is not treated as statistically independent evidence beyond that check.

Results › Machine learning

All three machine-learning methods discriminated tumor from normal samples on the ten hub genes under nested cross-validation (Fig. 5A–C and S8). The random forest was the strongest, with a mean AUC of 0.995 ± 0.014 across the ten outer folds and an accuracy of 0.950, followed by LASSO and SVM-RFE, tied at a mean AUC of 0.972 ± 0.063 (accuracy 0.959 and 0.942 respectively). Pooling the out-of-fold predictions gave AUCs of 0.974 (95% BCa CI 0.903–0.997) for the random forest, 0.970 (0.893–0.999) for SVM-RFE and 0.939 (0.785–1.000) for LASSO, with sensitivities of 0.971, 0.952 and 0.971 and specificities of 0.824, 0.882 and 0.882 respectively. The specificity estimates rest on only 17 normal samples, so the pooled figures and their bootstrap intervals are the more trustworthy summaries; all three are given in Table 3. The three algorithms retained different subsets. Under LASSO genes surviving in at least 70% of outer folds were PPARG, GSK3B, ERBB2 and EP300 (Fig. 5D); the random forest kept EGFR, PPARG, PTGS2, ERBB2 and ESR1 above the same threshold (Fig. 5E); and bootstrap resampling left SVM-RFE with PPARG, EP300, BCL2, GSK3B and ERBB2 (Fig. 5F). These per-fold stability lists are narrower than the model-specific marker sets, and the two are reported separately in Table 4. The model-specific marker sets themselves were: LASSO, every gene with a non-zero coefficient at λ* (BCL2, EP300, ERBB2, ESR1, GSK3B, PPARG); random forest, the five highest-ranked genes by Gini importance (EGFR, ERBB2, ESR1, PPARG, PTGS2); and SVM-RFE, the five highest-ranked by bootstrap selection frequency (BCL2, EP300, ERBB2, GSK3B, PPARG). Set against one another, the three marker sets share exactly two genes, PPARG and ERBB2 (Fig. 5G–I, S9 and S10); BCL2, EP300, ESR1 and GSK3B are each selected by two of the three. Because the ten hub genes are strongly correlated, and PPARG alone separates the classes at an AUC of 0.93, the consensus was built by intersecting the three model-specific marker sets rather than the near-empty per-fold stability sets. For marker sets of six, five and five genes drawn from ten, an overlap of two or more arises by chance with probability 0.49 (expected overlap 1.5 genes), so this two-gene marker-set intersection is weak evidence on its own; the composite score below, which also folds in differential expression, PPI degree and SHAP influence, is what the final target panel is actually based on, not the marker-set intersection in isolation.

Results › Post hoc explainability and validation

Permutation testing with 1000 randomized iterations confirmed that the discriminative performance of all three models was not attributable to chance: for every model the null distribution of AUC under shuffled labels was centered near 0.49 and no permuted value reached the observed AUC, giving an empirical p-value of 9.99 × 10−4 in each case, the smallest value attainable, so the discrimination is not an artefact of the 104 : 17 class imbalance. Stratified bootstrap resampling confirmed the stability of the estimates, with 95% BCa intervals of 0.785–1.000 for LASSO, 0.903–0.997 for the random forest and 0.893–0.999 for SVM-RFE. SHAP analysis quantified contribution of each hub gene to model predictions, using genuinely out-of-fold attributions so that every sample's explanation comes from a model fitted without that sample, correcting a full-data-model shortcut in an earlier round of analysis. Averaged across the three models, EP300 carried the highest mean absolute SHAP value of any hub gene (0.948), closely followed by PPARG (0.867) and then BCL2 (0.638); GSK3B (0.420) and ERBB2 (0.419) followed, essentially indistinguishable from one another, while ESR1 contributed comparatively little (0.284). PPARG and ERBB2 remain the two genes present in every classifier's own marker set, but SHAP importance computed correctly does not single out ERBB2 among the other candidates: EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 are not meaningfully separated by this measure. The direction of effect matched the differential-expression results: higher PPARG and BCL2 expression pushed predictions towards the normal class, higher EP300 and ERBB2 expression towards the tumor class. Taken together, the stability and SHAP results identify PPARG as the dominant single contributor and BCL2 as a consistent second; the remaining ranked positions are not resolved by this analysis alone (Fig. S1–S4).

Results › Integrated target prioritization

Combining differential expression, PPI degree, cross-model stability and out-of-fold SHAP influence into a single composite score produced the ranking shown in Table 4 (Fig. S11 and S12). PPARG ranked first by a wide margin (composite 0.881), followed by BCL2 (0.499), a clear second. Below that the ranking does not resolve a single third target: EP300 (0.401), EGFR (0.393) and ERBB2 (0.383) are separated by only 0.018 of composite score, well within the resolution of a four-component index built on a ten-gene panel, and we report this instability rather than nominate one of the three with false confidence. PPARG and ERBB2 are the two genes present in the marker set of every classifier; BCL2 is present in two of three (LASSO and SVM-RFE) and is the strongest of these by composite score. ESR1 ranks sixth (composite 0.332) and is not among the statistically top-ranked genes under the corrected panel. PPARG is among the statistically top-ranked genes and structural characterization was concentrated on PPARG, the dominant target by every measure. PPARG alone is therefore reported as a fully characterized target, extended here with molecular dynamics simulation of its own co-crystallized reference ligand, rosiglitazone; BCL2, ESR1, EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 are examined by docking only.

Results › Molecular docking

Three-dimensional structures of six machine-learning-prioritized receptors were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank: 1ZGY at a resolution of 1.80 Å (PPARG), 3ERT at 1.90 Å (ESR1), 600K at 1.62 Å (BCL2), 1M17 at 2.60 Å (EGFR), 3RCD at 3.21 Å (ERBB2) and 6V8K at 1.84 Å (EP300). Poses were ranked by predicted binding affinity and compared with the co-crystallized reference ligand of each receptor. Before any lead compound was interpreted, each receptor's own co-crystallized ligand was re-docked into it and the top-ranked pose compared with the crystallographic geometry, without superposition. Five of six receptors were validated by this criterion, all as the top-ranked pose of nine: rosiglitazone returned 1.992 Å at PPARG, 4-hydroxytamoxifen 1.855 Å at ESR1, venetoclax 0.696 Å at BCL2, erlotinib 1.568 Å at EGFR and TAK-285 1.721 Å at ERBB2. At EP300, the top-ranked pose of QS4 returned 7.048 Å, failing this criterion; EP300 results are reported for completeness but interpreted with lower confidence than the five validated receptors (see discussion). Predicted binding affinities of the six lead compounds at the six receptors are summarized in Fig. 6. Marmeline is the strongest lead at four of six receptors: PPARG (−8.9 kcal mol−1), BCL2 (−8.1), ERBB2 (−8.7) and EP300 (−8.8); at EGFR the hydroxyphenyl-cinnamamide is strongest instead (−8.1), with marmeline third (−7.6), and at ESR1 aegeline is strongest (−8.3), with marmeline second (−8.2). Ascorbic acid remains the weakest compound at every receptor, by a margin of at least 1.7 kcal mol−1. The comparison that carries interpretive weight is with the co-crystallized drug at each receptor rather than between compounds. Measured against each receptor's own reference ligand, marmeline outperformed it at PPARG (−8.9 vs. −8.6 kcal mol−1, a margin of 0.3) and at EP300 (−8.8 vs. −6.4, a margin of 2.4); the hydroxyphenyl-cinnamamide likewise outperformed the reference at EGFR (−8.1 vs. −7.0, a margin of 1.1). At the remaining three receptors no lead reached the reference drug: the best ESR1 lead fell 1.6 kcal mol−1 short of 4-hydroxytamoxifen, the best ERBB2 lead fell 1.3 kcal mol−1 short of TAK-285, and the best BCL2 lead fell 4.2 kcal mol−1 short of venetoclax. That asymmetry reflects the references themselves — venetoclax binds with extraordinary affinity by design — as much as it reflects the leads. Because the Vina scoring function is approximately additive over atoms, raw affinity rewards molecular size: venetoclax (61 heavy atoms) dominates the raw comparison at BCL2 at −12.3 kcal mol−1, while ascorbic acid, the weakest binder in absolute terms at every receptor, is also the smallest compound tested (12 heavy atoms), so part of the raw ordering is a statement about molecular weight rather than binding efficiency. Interactions of the best pose at each receptor are listed in Table 5. Contacts in these complexes are polar, non-polar, π-cation, π-anion, π-sulfur and π–π stacking, with alkyl and π-alkyl contacts accounting for most of the residues drawn in every panel. Every pose was checked against the position of the co-crystallized ligand, and affinities were interpreted only for poses at the reference ligand's own site. The same interactions are additionally shown as individual, higher-resolution panels in Table 6 (PPARG, ESR1 and BCL2) and Table 7 (EGFR, ERBB2 and EP300).

Results › Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics of marmeline bound to PPARG, its highest-affinity complex, was run for 200 ns on an external cluster, alongside PPARG bound to its own co-crystallized reference ligand, rosiglitazone, simulated. Backbone RMSD (Fig. 7A) stayed within a broadly stable regime at both complexes: 2.62 ± 0.39 Å for PPARG-marmeline and 2.41 ± 0.40 Å for PPARG-rosiglitazone, with neither complex drifting beyond 3.72 Å over the full 200 ns. The marmeline complex's final-100-ns mean differed from its whole-trajectory mean by only 0.17 Å, indicating it had equilibrated well within the 200 ns window; the reference complex's final-100-ns mean (2.71 Å) ran 0.30 Å above its whole-trajectory mean, a slower, still-settling approach to its stable regime rather than any loss of the bound pose. Radius of gyration (Fig. 7B) was likewise stable, averaging 1.958 ± 0.012 nm for PPARG-marmeline and 1.969 ± 0.017 nm for PPARG-rosiglitazone, indistinguishable in magnitude between the two complexes and indicating that the receptor neither unfolds nor expands with either ligand bound. Rg stayed within a narrow window throughout the run (1.923–2.007 nm for PPARG-marmeline and 1.906–2.034 nm for PPARG-rosiglitazone), a span of under 0.13 nm at each. Solvent-accessible surface area (Fig. 7C) averaged 147.1 ± 3.3 nm2 for PPARG-marmeline and 148.0 ± 3.9 nm2 for PPARG-rosiglitazone, closely matching between the two complexes and consistent with a comparably buried, well-packed binding site under either ligand. The full range spanned 136.5–159.4 nm2 for PPARG-marmeline and 137.3–164.7 nm2 for PPARG-rosiglitazone, with no directional trend over the course of either trajectory. Per-residue Cα fluctuation (Fig. 7D) averaged 1.09 Å for PPARG-marmeline and 1.23 Å for PPARG-rosiglitazone; the largest excursions in both systems were confined to solvent-exposed loop segments away from the binding pocket, with the reference complex fluctuating somewhat more at one such loop in particular (peak 6.6 Å vs. 5.6 Å for marmeline). The receptor–ligand hydrogen-bond count (Fig. 7E) averaged 0.75 per frame for PPARG-marmeline and 0.93 for PPARG-rosiglitazone; marmeline therefore engages the receptor with somewhat fewer persistent polar contacts than the co-crystallized drug, consistent with the predominantly hydrophobic contact shell identified by docking (Table 5). This reference complex's hydrogen-bonding behaviour much closer to marmeline's. Binding free energy was estimated by MM/PBSA over the final 50 ns of each trajectory (Table 8 and Fig. 8). The total binding free energy was −26.47 kcal mol−1 for PPARG-marmeline and −29.63 kcal mol−1 for PPARG-rosiglitazone, its own co-crystallized reference ligand simulated. The van der Waals term dominates both complexes, consistent with the hydrophobic binding mode seen in docking. The first two principal components of Cα motion accounted for 41.53% of total variance at PPARG-marmeline and 46.42% at PPARG-rosiglitazone (Fig. 9C and D), each free energy landscape (Fig. 9E and F) resolving to a single dominant basin rather than multiple competing conformational states, and the dynamic cross-correlation matrices (Fig. 9A and B) show no long-range anti-correlated motion that would indicate a large-scale conformational rearrangement of the receptor under either ligand. Snapshots taken every 50 ns and superimposed by receptor Cα least-squares fitting (Fig. 10A) show marmeline occupying the same pocket at 0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 ns, without leaving the site. At 200 ns, marmeline sits in a predominantly hydrophobic contact shell (Fig. 10B): the closest contacts are Glu259, Cys285, Met364, Leu330, Phe287 and Gly284 (1.9–2.5 Å), consistent with the docking contact profile in Table 5. A frame-by-frame analysis of all 21 snapshots (Fig. S13) shows ligand heavy-atom RMSD from the docked pose rising within the first 10–20 ns and then fluctuating around a plateau rather than continuing to increase: 3.3–7.0 Å (mean 5.6 Å), with centre-of-mass displacement of 1.1–3.0 Å. The ligand therefore relaxes from the docked pose into a nearby bound conformation and is found in contact with the site at every sampled time point; this establishes association at the sampled 10 ns intervals, not uninterrupted residence between them.

Results › Hartree–Fock analysis of the lead compound

Marmeline (C22H25NO3) was optimized at the Hartree–Fock level with the 6-311G(d,p) basis set (Fig. 11A). The harmonic-frequency calculation returned no imaginary frequencies (lowest real mode +7.0 cm−1), confirming the structure as a genuine minimum, and supplied thermochemical corrections at 298.15 K and 1 atm (zero-point correction 0.4536 hartree). The dipole moment is 4.04 D. Frontier orbital energies and the global reactivity descriptors derived from them through Koopmans' theorem are collected in Table 9: HOMO −8.09 eV, LUMO +1.88 eV, gap 9.97 eV. Spatially, the HOMO is delocalized across the conjugated cinnamide-aryl core of the molecule, whereas the LUMO is concentrated over the aliphatic prenyl/cyclohexenyl portion at the opposite end of the structure (Fig. 11C and D), so the two frontier orbitals sample distinct regions rather than overlapping. The molecular electrostatic potential (Fig. 11B) shows the expected polarity pattern for this scaffold: the carbonyl oxygen of the cinnamamide carries the most negative potential, and the amide N–H the most positive, with the aromatic rings and the aliphatic chain close to neutral. QTAIM analysis of the HF/6-311G(d,p) electron density located 108 critical points: 51 nuclear, 53 bond, 4 ring and no cage critical points (Fig. 12A). Of the 53 bond critical points, 51 fall in the covalent range (ρ 0.25–0.43 a.u.), and 2 are markedly weaker (ρ 0.0085 and 0.0110 a.u.), with Espinosa stabilization energies of 1.7 and 2.1 kcal mol−1 a weak intramolecular contact rather than a classical hydrogen bond. The Poincaré–Hopf sum for this critical-point set is 2 rather than the value of 1 expected for a fully resolved isolated-molecule topology, a minor completeness gap typical of dense, flexible structures and not evidence of a chemically significant missing bond path. The two non-covalent contacts located by this analysis are collected in Table 10. The density of states (Fig. 12B), convoluted from the HF/6-311G(d,p) orbital eigenvalues, shows a well-separated occupied band below −8 eV from the virtual band above +2 eV, consistent with the 9.97 eV HOMO–LUMO gap. The electron localization function and localized orbital locator (Fig. 12C and D) both show the expected maxima along the covalent framework, with no localization signature in the region of the two weak non-covalent contacts identified by QTAIM, consistent with their small stabilization energies. The reduced density gradient scatter (Fig. 13, 542 754 points) shows a small attractive population 96 points at RDG < 0.5 a.u. with sign(λ2)ρ < −0.01 a.u. (most negative −0.013 a.u.) alongside 120 van der Waals and 154 steric points in the same low-RDG range, corroborating the weak, non-hydrogen-bonding contact identified by QTAIM rather than a strong intramolecular hydrogen bond.

Discussion

This study set out to identify, and then to test, the protein targets through which Aegle marmelos phytochemicals may act in breast cancer. Two findings anchor it: PPARG is the best-supported target by almost every measure tried, and BCL2 is a robust second. The third position does not resolve cleanly EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 are not reliably separated by composite score once SHAP importance is computed correctly. That layered design proved discriminating: STAT3, the most connected node (degree 88), was not differentially expressed, retained by no model, and carried almost no SHAP weight ranking on connectivity alone would have put it near the top, which is the clearest sign the additional layers are doing real work. A sensitivity analysis of the composite ranking confirms this: a twelve-variant grid (three normalizations × four weighting schemes) places PPARG in the top three under all twelve variants and BCL2 under eleven of twelve, while EGFR, ERBB2 and EP300 are not consistently favored by any particular normalization or weighting, matching the three-way tie already reported above (Table S8). A case-resampling test (300 stratified bootstrap resamples) and a matched label-permutation null (300 permutations) confirm that PPARG's dominance reflects real signal rather than an artifact of a hand-picked gene panel: PPARG lands in the top three in 99.3% of bootstrap resamples against a 31.7% permuted baseline, a clear separation. Both analyses use a single, fixed-hyperparameter fit and a full-data SHAP approximation per iteration rather than the complete nested-cross-validation, out-of-fold pipeline behind Table 4, since the latter cannot be re-run inside several hundred iterations in reasonable time; they therefore resample a simplified estimator of the composite score, not the complete composite itself, and the 99.3%/31.7% separation should be read accordingly. Nor does the label-permutation null, which operates within this fixed ten-gene panel, establish that the upstream panel selection (STRING degree and GeneCards filtering) was itself unbiased; it tests only whether the classifier distinguishes real signal from noise once the panel is fixed. The same resampling test was not tractable for BCL2 given the cost of nested cross-validation and genuinely out-of-fold SHAP even at this simplified level, so BCL2's robustness rests on the reweighting sensitivity grid rather than on case resampling. PPARG and BCL2 remain the most consistently supported part of this panel, and both are top-two by composite score regardless of which SHAP computation is used. PPARG and BCL2 form the most consistently supported part of this panel. PPARG dominated on almost every axis largest fold change, highest selection stability and the second-highest mean absolute SHAP value of the ten and, as a ligand-activated nuclear receptor promoting differentiation in breast epithelium, its down-regulation in tumor tissue is consistent with loss of a differentiation-promoting brake. BCL2 is a canonical anti-apoptotic regulator of the intrinsic pathway and an established, druggable target in this disease (venetoclax), and ranks a clear second by composite score. The gene completing the panel is less certain: EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 are too closely spaced in composite score to separate with confidence at this sample size, and are discussed as candidates rather than one being singled out. ESR1 encodes oestrogen receptor alpha, the central determinant of subtype and target of endocrine therapy in this disease, and was carried through docking (Tables 5 and 6) alongside the better-supported panel; molecular dynamics and Hartree–Fock-level quantum-chemical characterization were concentrated on PPARG instead, the dominant target by almost every measure, extended here to PPARG's own co-crystallized reference ligand, rosiglitazone. The chemistry points the same way: the three most connected compounds marmeline, N-[2-hydroxy-2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethyl]cinnamamide and the prenyloxyphenyl-oxazolylpyridine are each predicted to engage six targets, the first two sharing a phenylethyl cinnamamide scaffold with aegeline, indicating a scaffold-driven interaction rather than a scatter of unrelated hits. Trans-marmeline was isolated and characterized independently of the GC-MS-based phytochemical database entry used here, by Sharma, Rattan and Sharma,35 so marmeline's occurrence as a genuine Aegle marmelos phytochemical is independently supported; a difference between the modelled structure and a single current database entry does not by itself indicate the modelled isomer is wrong, and confirming the exact stereoisomer used here against primary isolation reports is noted as a limitation. Marmeline's predicted engagement of PPARG has also been reported independently by Ongtanasup et al.,36 in an Alzheimer's disease context distinct from the breast-cancer application examined here; no new isolation or characterization of marmeline was performed in this study. In docking, marmeline was the strongest lead at four of the six receptors: PPARG (−8.9 kcal mol−1, stronger than rosiglitazone at −8.6), BCL2 (−8.1, short of venetoclax at −12.3), ERBB2 (−8.7, short of TAK-285 at −10.0) and EP300 (−8.8, stronger than the QS4 inhibitor at −6.4). At EGFR the hydroxyphenyl-cinnamamide was strongest instead (−8.1, stronger than erlotinib at −7.0), with marmeline third (−7.6); at ESR1 aegeline was strongest (−8.3, short of 4-hydroxytamoxifen at −9.9), with marmeline second (−8.2). The receptor panel rests on the machine-learning consensus (Table 4), not on any single compound; marmeline was carried forward for molecular dynamics and Hartree–Fock-level quantum-chemical characterization on the strength of its docking result at PPARG. This is not the first computational study of Aegle marmelos in breast cancer: Rami and Bhatt screened leaf-extract fractions for MCF-7 cytotoxicity, identified constituents by GC-MS/LC-MS, and docked the hits against ESR1 (3ERT, the same structure used here) and EGFR. The present study differs on three points: an evidence-based pipeline nominates PPARG and BCL2 as clear candidates, absent from theirs, alongside a closely tied cluster (EP300, EGFR, ERBB2) for a third position their own panel does not consider; the compound set is IMPPAT-predicted rather than extract-confirmed; and their phenotypic assay (DPPH, MTT) has no counterpart here, so the two studies are complementary rather than competing.37 Docking supported this directly: every lead occupied the orthosteric site of each receptor tested (strongest: −8.9 kcal mol−1 at PPARG, −8.8 at ESR1), and the diagrams of Tables 6 and 7 show a consistent binding mode: the leads occupy the partial-agonism region of the pocket, distinct from where thiazolidinediones stabilize helix 12.38 At ESR1 the leads reproduce the hydrophobic half of the reference binding mode, occupying the same cage as 4-hydroxytamoxifen, whose polar contact repositions helix 12 and underlies its antagonism.39 Occupancy on this scale is consistent with competition at the oestradiol site. At BCL2, leads bind the hydrophobic subsites of the BH3-binding groove venetoclax occupies, which additionally reaches two hydrogen bonds near the P4 pocket. All three receptors recognize the same pockets as the established drugs through this hydrophobic component. The docking score comparisons above, and the binding-mode descriptions and discussion that follow, are computational predictions, not experimental measurements of affinity, agonism or antagonism, and should be read as testable hypotheses pending a direct assay. The EP300 redocking control did not meet the validation criterion (7.048 Å versus the 2.0 Å threshold used for the other five receptors); EP300 affinities and contacts are therefore reported with lower confidence than the five validated receptors, and this limitation applies to every EP300 comparison discussed above. Confidence in docking poses at the five validated receptors rests on redocking each receptor's own co-crystallized reference ligand within the 2.0 Å RMSD criterion described in methods, which is the physical basis for trusting the predicted binding modes at those sites. Molecular dynamics of marmeline and of rosiglitazone bound to PPARG over 200 ns found both complexes stable, with MM/PBSA totals of −26.47 kcal mol−1 (marmeline) and −29.63 kcal mol−1 (rosiglitazone). Binding free energy estimates for each complex were derived from a single production trajectory; reported uncertainties reflect frame-to-frame variation within that trajectory rather than variation between independent repeat simulations. Hartree–Fock-level calculations (HF/6-311G(d,p)) confirmed marmeline's optimized geometry as a genuine minimum with a 9.97 eV HOMO–LUMO gap and only a weak intramolecular contact; this is isolated-ligand characterization only, not evidence of receptor engagement. Taken together, network pharmacology, transcriptome-based machine learning and docking converge on PPARG and BCL2 as the best-supported targets and on marmeline as the lead compound; the third position remains genuinely open among EP300, EGFR and ERBB2. Molecular dynamics and quantum-chemical characterization, completed for marmeline at PPARG against its own co-crystallized reference ligand, support carrying marmeline forward for experimental testing; extending the same depth of characterization to BCL2, ESR1 and the remaining candidates is the logical next computational step, pending experimental validation.

Conclusions

By coupling network pharmacology to transcriptome-based machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence, this study nominates PPARG as the best-supported candidate target of Aegle marmelos phytochemicals in breast cancer and BCL2 as a robust second; a third target is not nominated with confidence, since EP300, EGFR and ERBB2 are not reliably separated by composite score once SHAP importance is computed correctly, and ESR1 is retained as a previously characterized target for practical rather than statistical reasons. Six lead compounds predicted inactive for the four modelled endpoints were prioritized from 138 phytochemicals, while a phenylethyl cinnamamide scaffold accounted for most of the network connectivity. All three classifiers separated tumor from normal tissue with pooled AUCs between 0.939 and 0.974 at an empirical permutation p-value of 9.99 × 10−4 and shared PPARG and ERBB2 as a two-gene marker-set core, with BCL2 added by composite ranking; PPARG was the dominant contributor throughout. Molecular docking of the six leads against all six receptors (PPARG, ESR1, BCL2, EGFR, ERBB2 and EP300) found marmeline the strongest lead at four of the six receptors: PPARG, BCL2, ERBB2 and EP300. Marmeline was carried forward for molecular dynamics and Hartree–Fock-level quantum-chemical characterization at PPARG, alongside a corrected molecular dynamics simulation of PPARG's own reference ligand, rosiglitazone, which together confirmed stable 200 ns trajectories (MM/PBSA −26.47 and −29.63 kcal mol−1 respectively) and a genuine-minimum optimized geometry for marmeline. Testing PPARG engagement directly in breast-cancer cell lines, resolving the identity of the third target with a larger or independent cohort are the logical next steps to test these computational predictions.
