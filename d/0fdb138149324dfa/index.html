Abstract

Tyrosinase, a copper-dependent oxidase, plays a critical role in melanin biosynthesis and is a target in skin-whitening cosmetics. Conventional inhibitors like arbutin and kojic acid are widely used but suffer from cytotoxicity, instability, and inconsistent efficacy, highlighting the need for safer, more effective alternatives. In this study, two ligand-based machine learning models were developed: one to predict the biological activity of compounds and the other to estimate specific pIC50 values. These models were employed to screen potential tyrosinase inhibitors from natural product libraries and FDA-approved drug databases. Subsequently, the molecules identified through machine learning screening were subjected to more precise multi tyrosinase-like structures molecular docking to refine the selection. We identified three top-ranking inhibitors, rhodanine-3-propionic acid, lodoxamide, and cytidine 5'-(dihydrogen phosphate), with strong binding affinities mediated by metal ion coordination and π-π interactions at the enzyme's active site. In vitro assays revealed that all three compounds exhibited higher inhibitory activity against mushroom tyrosinase compared to arbutin (IC50 = 38.37 mM), with rhodanine-3-propionic acid displaying the most potent inhibition (IC50 = 0.7349 mM). Furthermore, transdermal permeation experimental results confirmed that these compounds achieved markedly better skin permeability than commercial arbutin-based formulations, highlighting their potential as next-generation agents for inhibiting melanin production in cosmetic applications.

Introduction

Tyrosinase, a copper-dependent oxidase, plays a critical role in melanin biosynthesis by catalyzing the oxidation of tyrosine to l-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (l-DOPA) and subsequently to dopaquinone, which undergoes further reactions to form melanin. Melanin is an essential pigment responsible for the coloration of skin, hair, and eyes in humans, and it also provides photoprotection against ultraviolet radiation. However, abnormal tyrosinase activity has been implicated in various dermatological disorders, including melasma, postinflammatory hyperpigmentation, freckles, and age spots, as well as in diseases like vitiligo and albinism. Beyond its biological roles, tyrosinase is also involved in enzymatic browning reactions in fruits and vegetables, significantly impacting food quality and shelf life. Tyrosinase inhibitors, a class of compounds that suppress tyrosinase activity, have found extensive applications across various industries. These include the treatment of hyperpigmentation disorders, the development of skin-whitening cosmetics, and the prevention of food spoilage. Among these applications, the cosmetics industry has shown particular interest in tyrosinase inhibitors due to their ability to reduce melanin production, making them key active ingredients in skin-whitening and antipigmentation products. For example, popular tyrosinase inhibitors, such as kojic acid, arbutin, and hydroquinone, have been successfully used in cosmetic formulations. However, many of these inhibitors have limitations such as potential cytotoxicity, skin irritation, instability under light or heat, and variable efficacy across different skin types. These drawbacks highlight the demand for safer, more effective, and stable alternatives. Natural product libraries represent an ideal source for discovering next-generation tyrosinase inhibitors due to their unparalleled chemical diversity and structural complexity. Many natural products have evolved to interact with biological targets, making them inherently suited for high-affinity binding. Notably, more than 50% of small-molecule drugs approved since 1981 are either derived from natural products or based on natural-product scaffolds. Numerous plant-, microbial-, and marine-derived molecules, including kojic acid, arbutin, aloesin, and resveratrol, already serve as benchmark tyrosinase inhibitors. Some of them are valued for their favorable safety profiles. These attributes highlight the critical role of natural products, particularly in the discovery of enzyme inhibitors such as tyrosinase. Furthermore, the inclusion of FDA-approved drug libraries in screening ensures the identification of compounds with established safety and pharmacokinetic profiles, thereby accelerating the development pipeline for efficient and safe tyrosinase inhibitors. Recent advancements in machine learning (ML) have revolutionized the drug discovery process by enabling rapid and efficient virtual screening of compounds. ML models, trained on large-scale chemical data sets, demonstrate exceptional capability in predicting critical properties of molecules, including biological activity, toxicity, and physicochemical characteristics, with remarkable accuracy. These predictive capabilities allow for the efficient prioritisation of candidate compounds for downstream analysis, substantially reducing the time and cost associated with traditional experimental workflows. Complementing ML, structure-based drug design techniques, such as molecular docking, provide detailed insights into the binding interactions and affinities between small molecules and target proteins. Molecular docking has been widely applied in the discovery and exploration of tyrosinase inhibitors. Due to the absence of a resolved Homo sapiens tyrosinase (HsTYR) crystal structure, docking simulations are typically conducted using the crystal structure of Agaricus bisporus tyrosinase (AbTYR), − H. sapiens tyrosinase-related protein 1 (HsTYRP1), , or homology-modeled HsTYR based on AbTYR or HsTYRP1. , For example, Choi et al. utilized mushroom tyrosinase as the 3D-structure for docking simulations to investigate whether the synthesized (Z)-5-(substituted benzylidene)-3-phenyl-2-thioxooxazolidin-4-ones, which demonstrated in vitro tyrosinase inhibitory activity, could bind directly to the active site of tyrosinase. Similarly, Xu et al. employed molecular docking simulations to demonstrate that maculosin could bind to AbTYR, HsTYRP1, and Bacillus megaterium tyrosinase. Lee et al. constructed a 3D structure of HsTYR based on HsTYRP1 and performed high-throughput virtual screening, identifying gallacetophenone as a novel antimelanogenesis compound with potential tyrosinase inhibitory activity. In this study, we aim to identify potent and safe tyrosinase inhibitors by integrating ML-based screening and molecular docking. First, natural product libraries and FDA-approved drug databases were rapidly and efficiently screened using ML models for activity prediction and IC50 estimation. Next, candidate selection was carefully refined through molecular docking against HsTYR-like experimental structures derived from AbTYR and HsTYRP1. Finally, the inhibitory activity and skin permeability of top-ranking candidates were experimentally validated, revealing that three compounds demonstrated higher mushroom tyrosinase inhibitory activity and permeability compared to the reference inhibitor, arbutin. These findings highlight their potential as whitening agents or topical tyrosinase inhibitors for cosmetic and pharmaceutical applications. Furthermore, this integrative screening approach demonstrates its potential as an efficient strategy for discovering novel enzyme inhibitors with favorable properties.

Materials and Methods

The screening process for identifying potent tyrosinase inhibitors involved four stages (Figure ). First, a library of 517,903 compounds was compiled from diverse databases, ensuring a broad pool of candidates relevant to tyrosinase inhibition. Next, two ligand-based ML models were applied: one predicted biological activity, and the other estimated specific pIC50 values. Compounds predicted to be active with a pIC50 ≥ 4.00 were shortlisted, narrowing the data set to 100,846 compounds. Subsequently, molecular docking was performed to evaluate binding affinities to the tyrosinase active site, selecting candidates with docking scores ≤−8.5, which reduced the pool to 2372 compounds. Finally, in vitro validation was conducted to experimentally confirm tyrosinase inhibitory activity (IC50) and transdermal penetration, identifying three highly effective and safe inhibitors. Stepwise workflow for screening tyrosinase inhibitors. The process includes database compilation, ML-based activity prediction, molecular docking, and in vitro validation, identifying three promising tyrosinase inhibitors.

Materials and Methods › ML Screening

Two ligand-based ML models were developed to predict tyrosinase inhibitory activity. The first model classified compounds as potential active or nonactive inhibitors, while the second estimated their specific pIC50 values.

Materials and Methods › ML Screening › Data Sets

Tyrosinase inhibitors with l-DOPA substrate and IC50 ranging from 18 nM to 5 mM were collected from BindingDB, including SMILESs and IC50 values. Molecular descriptors (including 208 features) were calculated using RDKit. However, 208 molecular descriptors posed challenges such as high dimensionality, increased computational cost, and the risk of overfitting. To address this issue, descriptors with low variance or redundancy were filtered out, resulting in a refined set of 137 descriptors. Compounds with IC50 values below the positive control threshold were labeled as potential inhibitors (491 compounds, labeled “1”). In contrast, those above the threshold were labeled noninhibitors (506 compounds, labeled “0”), resulting in 997 samples for activity classification. For the regression task, IC50 values were converted to pIC50 values (IC50 = −lg IC50), producing a total of 1153 data for specific IC50 values prediction. The overall distribution of pIC50 values is depicted in Figure S1. The data exhibit a roughly normal distribution, with the majority of pIC50 values concentrated between 2 and 8, indicating a reasonably balanced data set.

Materials and Methods › ML Screening › Descriptors Processing

Molecular descriptors quantify molecules’ properties, including integer and floating-point variables. Remove molecules with missing descriptors. Integer features constant in >90% of samples were excluded. Continuous features with a standard deviation ≤0.05 were eliminated. After filtering, 137 descriptor features remained. To reduce redundancy and prevent overfitting, feature importance was assessed using multiple algorithms (Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest) via Scikit-learn. Model performance was evaluated with 10-fold cross-validation. Feature importance included Gini, permutation, and SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP). Models were then rebuilt using top-ranked features and re-evaluated.

Materials and Methods › ML Screening › Prediction Models Construction

Optimal feature sets were selected to build classification and regression models. Hyperparameters were tuned using random search, grid search, and Bayesian optimization, with final evaluation via 10-fold cross-validation. The best-performing models were used for database screening. Compound libraries were sourced from ZINC15 (Natural Products & FDA), COCONUT, TCMSP, TCMBank, FooDB, and FocusDB. Molecular descriptors were computed using RDKit, and the models were applied to predict activity. Compounds predicted as potential inhibitors (label = “1”) and with pIC50 ≥ 4 (equivalent to IC50 ≤ 100 μM) were selected for molecular docking. Notably, the standard tyrosinase inhibitor kojic acid exhibits inhibitory activity at approximately 100 μM under standard l-DOPA assay conditions. Furthermore, the distribution of pIC50 values peaks around 4, indicating that this range represents the most frequently observed inhibitory potency in the data set (Figure S1).

Materials and Methods › Multi HsTYR-Like Structures Docking Screening

Molecular docking was performed using Schrödinger Maestro 2018-4 to identify potential tyrosinase inhibitors. Two AbTYR (PDB IDs: 2Y9W, 2Y9X) and nine HsTYRP1 (PDB IDs: 5M8L, 5M8M, 5M8N, 5M8O, 5M8P, 5M8Q, 5M8R, 5M8S, 5M8T) were selected. A conformation enrichment analysis was conducted to select suitable receptor conformations for docking. This analysis used 30 commonly known HsTYR active compounds (Table S1) and 1600 decoys from the DUD-E data set to evaluate the receptors’ ability to distinguish active compounds from inactive ones. Enrichment curves were used to identify the receptor conformations that best distinguished active molecules from inactive molecules. The selected receptor structures underwent a series of standard preprocessing steps to ensure accuracy and reliability. This included adding hydrogens, removing water molecules, optimizing hydrogen bonding networks, and performing energy minimization to refine the protein structures. A 20 Å grid box was defined around the ligand-binding site, based on the positions of ligands in the experimental crystal structures. The ligands obtained from ML screening were prepared using LigPrep with the OPLS 2005 force field, ensuring proper geometry and energy minimization. Ionization states were generated using Epik at pH 7.0 ± 2.0 to account for physiological conditions. The refined ligands were then docked into the active site of the selected receptor structures using the Glide module with the standard precision (SP) algorithm. Docking simulations generated up to 20 poses per ligand, carefully considering the metal coordination states within the tyrosinase active site to ensure realistic binding interactions. Docking scores for each compound were averaged across all receptor structures to provide a comprehensive binding affinity estimate. Compounds with average docking scores better than −8.0 kcal/mol were shortlisted for further analysis, as these scores indicated strong potential for tyrosinase inhibition.

Materials and Methods › In Vitro Tyrosinase Inhibitory Activity › Assay Materials

Tyrosinase lyophilized powder (from mushroom) and l-DOPA were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. Ltd. (USA). Rhodanine-3-propionic acid, cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), kojic acid, hydroquinone, and arbutin (all analytical grade) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Lodoxamide (purity ≥ 98%) was supplied by Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 6.8) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were used to prepare all solutions. Test compounds were dissolved in DMSO and then diluted with PBS to obtain five working concentrations appropriate for IC50 determination, maintaining a final DMSO concentration of 1% in the reaction system. Tyrosinase was dissolved in PBS to prepare an enzyme solution at 1000 U/mL, and l-DOPA was prepared as a 1 mM substrate solution in PBS (pH 6.8).

Materials and Methods › In Vitro Tyrosinase Inhibitory Activity › Experimental Procedure

The in vitro tyrosinase activity assay was performed following a previously reported spectrophotometric method with modifications. In a 96-well microplate, 50 μL of PBS, 40 μL of compound solution, and 10 μL of tyrosinase enzyme solution (1000 U/mL) were sequentially added and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min to facilitate enzyme–inhibitor interaction. Subsequently, 100 μL of l-DOPA solution (1 mM) was added to initiate the enzymatic reaction, followed by incubation at 25 °C for another 10 min. The blank control group received 40 μL of PBS buffer instead of the compound solution.

Materials and Methods › In Vitro Tyrosinase Inhibitory Activity › Measurement and Data Analysis

The optical density (OD) at 490 nm was recorded at both 0 and 20 min using a Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). Kojic acid, hydroquinone, and arbutin were used as positive controls. Materials and Methods › Transdermal Penetration Study › Materials

A Franz diffusion cell system was used, featuring a donor chamber with an internal diameter of 1 cm, an effective diffusion area of approximately 0.785 cm2, and a receptor chamber volume of 15 mL. Excised skin samples from Bama miniature pigs (thickness: 0.8–1.0 mm; area: 20 cm2 × 30 cm2; batch number: TPSY-11) were supplied by Guangquan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Subcutaneous fat was removed, and the skin was washed repeatedly with physiological saline until transparent. Rhodanine-3-propionic acid, cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), and lodoxamide were dissolved in sterile physiological saline containing 30% ethanol to prepare 10 mM solutions. The receptor medium consisted of sterile physiological saline with 30% ethanol.

Materials and Methods › Transdermal Penetration Study › Experimental Procedure

Diffusion cells were maintained at 32 °C in the water bath and stirred at 600 rpm. A 400 μL test solution was added to the donor chamber. At time points (30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 24 h), 500 μL of receptor solution was collected and replaced with an equal fresh receptor medium. Standard calibration solutions were prepared for each compound at 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 mM. Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was used to determine the maximum absorbance wavelengths (λmax), identified as 260 nm for rhodanine-3-propionic acid and cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), and 240 nm for lodoxamide. The absorbance of each receptor solution sample was measured at the corresponding λmax, and the concentration was calculated using the respective standard curves. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and average values were used for data analysis.

Results › ML Models for Tyrosinase Inhibitor Prediction

To accelerate the identification of potential tyrosinase inhibitors, ML models were developed for both activity classification and specific pIC50 value prediction. Five ML algorithms, Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, XGBoost, and Random Forest, were evaluated as baseline models. Each model was trained using default parameters and subjected to 10-fold cross-validation to assess performance in both classification and regression tasks. Among the five algorithms, Random Forest demonstrated the best performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.7927 for activity classification and an R 2 value of 0.6260 for pIC50 prediction (Figure ). The superior performance of Random Forest can be attributed to its ensemble learning approach, which combines multiple decision trees to improve predictive accuracy and robustness. This method effectively handles high-dimensional data and reduces model variance through bootstrap aggregation, making it particularly well-suited for bioactivity modeling, where data sets are often moderately sized and heterogeneous. To enhance model interpretability and reduce redundancy, feature importance was assessed using three distinct strategies: Gini, permutation, and SHAP. Gini importance ranks features based on their ability to reduce node impurity in decision trees. Permutation importance captures the sensitivity of model performance to feature shuffling. SHAP values provide a unified framework to quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to individual predictions. Based on these feature importance metrics, models were reconstructed using subsets of the top-ranked features, ranging from 10 to 50 features. As shown in Table , the optimal performance for activity classification was achieved with 20 Gini-ranked features, resulting in an accuracy of 0.8104 ± 0.0195. For pIC50 regression, the best performance (R 2 = 0.6349 ± 0.0723) was observed when using 40 SHAP-ranked features. Reducing the dimensionality of the feature set improved the models’ generalization ability and decreased computational demands, demonstrating the importance of effective feature selection in ML-based bioactivity modeling. Hyperparameter optimization was subsequently performed to further enhance the performance of the Random Forest model. Three optimization strategies, random search, grid search, and Bayesian optimization, were employed. Among these, Bayesian optimization yielded the best results, improving the accuracy of the activity classification model to 0.8115 and the R 2 value of the pIC50 regression model to 0.6763 (Table S2). The accuracy of activity classification models and R 2 values of pIC50 numeric prediction models reconstructed using the top 10–50 features selected by Gini, permutation, and SHAP. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, and results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Additionally, feature analysis revealed that descriptors such as fr_NH0 (number of tertiary amines) and SMR_VSA3 (van der Waals surface area within a specified molar refractivity range) consistently ranked among the top contributors across both classification and regression models (Table S3). These structural motifs are chemically relevant: tertiary amines can form hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with polar residues in the tyrosinase active site, while a large surface area facilitates van der Waals contacts, enhancing binding affinity. Descriptors reflecting charge distribution, such as MinPartialCharge and MaxAbsPartialCharge, also showed high importance. This aligns with the known characteristics of the tyrosinase catalytic site, which includes a pair of copper ions coordinated by histidine residues and favors interactions with polar ligands. BCUT2D_MRHI, representing molecular refractivity, was also important. Its relevance may lie in its correlation with electron polarization capacity, influencing how ligands interact with metal centers in the active site. The combination of descriptor filtering, importance-guided feature selection, and hyperparameter optimization enabled the construction of robust and interpretable predictive models. These models provided a foundation for downstream virtual screening, effectively prioritising candidates for molecular docking and experimental validation. The final Random-Forest models achieved high accuracy and R 2 while using only 20–40 interpretable descriptors, demonstrating that strategic feature pruning can slightly enhance performance and improve speed. Importantly, top descriptors match known tyrosinase pharmacophore elements (tertiary amines, polar surface, charge separation), suggesting the models capture genuine structure–activity relationships rather than artifacts. Results › Multi HsTYR-Like Structures Docking Screening

Due to the lack of experimental structures for HsTYR, accurate molecular docking and structure-based drug design targeting tyrosinase inhibitors remain challenging. Homology modeling and AlphaFold predictions are potential alternatives for generating HsTYR structures. , However, homology models often fail to accurately represent the geometry of metal-coordinated active sites, which is critical for docking simulations involving tyrosinase inhibitors. Similarly, AlphaFold-generated structures, especially AlphaFold3, are capable of producing complexes with Cu2+ ions but typically represent apo forms without ligands. This can lead to inaccuracies in the active site geometry and side-chain orientations. Previous studies − found that while AlphaFold models provide high-quality predictions of backbone conformations, fine structural details, such as side-chain positioning and metal coordination, limit the direct application of AlphaFold models as reliable docking targets without further refinement. Considering these limitations, we utilized available tyrosinase-related experimental structures for molecular docking. AbTYR was selected as a docking target due to its highly conserved dinuclear copper center, which shares significant structural similarity with the active site of HsTYR. While differences exist between AbTYR and HsTYR, particularly in the secondary coordination sphere, the conservation of the active site geometry and key residues makes AbTYR a reliable surrogate for virtual screening. − , In addition, HsTYRP1 was included to complement AbTYR. HsTYRP1 shares ∼40% sequence identity with HsTYR and contains a dinuclear zinc center with geometry resembling the copper center of tyrosinas. Molecules containing carboxyl (−COOH), phosphate (−PO4 3–), or hydroxyl (−OH) groups are known to chelate both Cu2+ and Zn2+ ions, enabling certain inhibitors, such as kojic acid and tropolone, , to bind both HsTYRP1 and HsTYR. Furthermore, the hydrophobic residues surrounding the active sites of HsTYR and HsTYRP1, such as phenylalanine and tyrosine, suggest that hydrophobic ligands may simultaneously bind these proteins. Given these shared features, molecules capable of binding to both AbTYR and HsTYRP1 could potentially exhibit activity against HsTYR, suggesting they may be worthwhile candidates for further investigation. Two crystal structures of AbTYR and nine human-related tyrosinase family structures, including HsTYRP1, were collected from the PDB. To ensure the reliability of the docking approach for tyrosinase-related proteins and small molecules, redocking experiments were first conducted using the Schrödinger Glide module. As shown in Figure S2, redocking was performed on 2Y9X (AbTYR) and 5M8O (HsTYRP1), yielding root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) values of 0.809 Å and 0.351 Å, respectively, between the redocked and crystal conformations of the small molecules. RMSD values below 2 Å are widely accepted as a hallmark of accurate pose reproduction, and values below 1 Å are considered outstanding. , These results support the applicability of this docking method for studying interactions between tyrosinase-like proteins and small molecules. To identify the most suitable conformations of AbTYR and HsTYRP1 for molecular docking while ensuring diversity in binding pocket characteristics, retrospective enrichment analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory power of each structure. This approach allowed the selection of receptor conformations with the highest enrichment capacity, ensuring robust virtual screening results. As shown in Figure and Table S4, the ROC enrichment curves demonstrate variability in the ability of different conformations to distinguish active compounds from decoys. Among the AbTYR structures, 2Y9X achieved superior enrichment (ROC = 0.70, AUC = 0.69, RIE = 2.83), outperforming 2Y9W. In the set of HsTYRP1, 5M8O exhibited the best overall enrichment (ROC = 0.76, AUC = 0.76, RIE = 2.25), followed by 5M8R (ROC = 0.70, AUC = 0.70, RIE = 2.63), which demonstrated early retrieval of actives. Based on the enrichment results, the receptor structures 2Y9X (AbTYR), 5M8O (HsTYRP1), and 5M8R (HsTYRP1) were selected for docking. 2Y9X serves as a well-characterized surrogate with high enrichment capacity, providing a benchmark for HsTYR activity. The two HsTYRP1 structures capture conformational variability within human tyrosinase family members. Structural conservation across these proteins, particularly in their metal-coordinated centers and hydrophobic environments, supports the potential cross-target activity of ligands. Candidate compounds identified through ML were docked individually into these three receptor conformations. The final docking score for each compound was determined as the average of the docking scores obtained across the three selected structures, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of their binding potential. The top-scoring ligands were further analyzed for binding conformation and interaction mode. Representative docking poses of three promising compounds are shown in Figure (3D interactions), with corresponding 2D interaction diagrams in Figure S3. The binding poses revealed that most ligands penetrate deeply into the catalytic site and coordinate with the dinuclear ions essential for tyrosinase activity. At physiological pH, carboxylic acid groups (−COOH) deprotonate to form carboxylates (−COO–), which can chelate metal ions. Lodoxamide, and rhodanine-3-propionic acid all showed metal-coordination interactions with distances ranging from 2.0 to 2.5 Å. Similarly, the phosphate group of cytidine 5′-monophosphate lost two protons to form a dianionic phosphate, coordinating with copper ions within 2.0–3.3 Å. In 2Y9X, key active site residues included HIS-61, HIS-85, HIS-259, HIS-263, PHE-264, MET-280, SER-282, VAL-283, and ALA-286. In 5M8O and 5M8R, the binding pockets involved HIS-215, HIS-377, HIS-381, ASN-378, GLU-216, ASP-212, and SER-394. Aromatic moieties of ligands frequently engaged in π–π stacking with histidine residues (e.g., HIS-263 in 2Y9X and HIS-215 in 5M8R), while hydroxyl and phosphate groups contributed to hydrogen bonding with polar residues such as SER, ASN, and MET. Lodoxamide formed symmetrical hydrogen bonds through its terminal carboxylic acids and coordinated with copper ions, forming a stable and well-anchored pose. Rhodanine-3-propionic acid, featuring a thiazolidine core, occupied the hydrophilic pocket region and exhibited metal chelation potential alongside hydrogen bonding. The selected compounds show strong binding potential and favorable interaction patterns, warranting further biochemical validation. All three docking candidates chelate the dicopper or dizinc catalytic center through bidentate phosphate or carboxylate donors at 2.0–2.5 Å distances consistent with optimal Cu–O coordination reported for high-affinity blockers. Aromatic rings further stabilize the complexes via π–π stacking with His 263 (fungal) or His 215 (human). These findings validated the ML models’ predictive power and provided mechanistic insights into the molecular interactions of the selected compounds. Results › Tyrosinase Inhibition Assay and IC50 Evaluation

To validate the inhibitory potency of the screened compounds, a spectrophotometric tyrosinase inhibition assay was performed using l-DOPA as the substrate. Kojic acid, hydroquinone, and arbutin were used as positive controls. The results, summarized in Table and Figure S4, showed that while all tested compounds exhibited lower inhibitory activity than the classical tyrosinase inhibitor kojic acid (IC50 = 0.1129 mM), they demonstrated significantly stronger inhibition compared to arbutin (IC50 = 38.37 mM) and hydroquinone (IC50 = 10.15 mM), both of which are widely used in cosmetic formulations as skin-whitening agents. Among the tested candidates, rhodanine-3-propionic acid displayed the highest potency (IC50 = 0.7349 mM), followed by lodoxamide (IC50 = 3.715 mM) and cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate) (IC50 = 6.289 mM). These findings highlight the superior inhibitory potential of the tested compounds compared to arbutin, a common ingredient derived from Arctostaphylos uva-ursi and other plant extracts, which is frequently used in commercial skin-whitening products. The in vitro assay confirms that all three ML-selected hits are more potent than the benchmark cosmetic actives, hydroquinone and arbutin. Although they are less potent than kojic acid, their food-grade (rhodanine-3-propionic acid and cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate)) or FDA-approved (lodoxamide) origins suggest a more favorable safety profile, potentially avoiding the irritation and photostability concerns associated with kojic acid. Moreover, their observed IC50 ranking mirrors the docking score order, supporting the validity of the multiconformation docking step. Because the binding modes are conserved between fungal (assay) and human tyrosinase models, these compounds are expected to retain activity against the human enzyme. They are therefore promising candidates for further cosmetic or therapeutic development.

Results › In Vitro Permeation Test for Dermal Delivery Potential

The ability of active compounds to permeate the skin and remain within targeted layers is critical for the efficacy of topical formulations. It is particularly important for tyrosinase inhibitors used in skincare products, as their effectiveness hinges on reaching the melanocytes in the basal layer of the epidermis, where melanin synthesis occurs. Insufficient skin penetration can limit the bioavailability of these inhibitors at their site of action, reducing their ability to effectively inhibit tyrosinase activity and control pigmentation disorders. An in vitro permeation test (IVPT) assessed dermal delivery potential using Franz diffusion cells and excised pig skin. Over 24 h, the permeation flux and cumulative permeation of rhodanine-3-propionic acid, cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), and lodoxamide were monitored. The cumulative permeation and flux profiles are illustrated in Figure , with detailed metrics summarized in Table S5. Cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate) showed the highest cumulative permeation (45.34% ± 4.50%) and flux (0.473 μmol/cm2·h), followed by rhodanine-3-propionic acid (29.92%) and lodoxamide (29.92%). Clinical efficacy of depigmenting agents hinges on reaching melanocytes in the basal epidermis. Using the calculation recommended by Aung et al. for pig-skin microneedle studies, we derived a 24 h cumulative permeation of 12.7% for a 5% α-arbutin cream. All three compounds demonstrated significantly higher skin permeation and retention compared to α-arbutin. Specifically, cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate) achieved a 3.58-fold higher permeation, and rhodanine-3-propionic acid a 2.36-fold higher rate, underscoring their superior skin penetration profiles. In terms of skin retention, lodoxamide demonstrated the highest retention rate (32.79%), indicating potential for prolonged local activity. Rhodanine-3-propionic acid and cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate) showed a favorable balance between permeation and retention, aligning with desired properties for topical skin-lightning agents. These candidates deliver markedly higher permeation without chemical enhancers, an encouraging property for consumer formulations that must reach basal-layer melanocytes. Cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate) favors rapid flux, suiting daytime lotions where systemic exposure should remain low, whereas lodoxamide’s greater retention suggests a night-patch or microneedle format that sustains local levels. Rhodanine-3-propionic acid offers an intermediate profile and, together with its food origin, represents a balanced candidate for routine serums. Overall, the IVPT data complement the enzyme results and reinforce the view that these compounds combine acceptable potency with advantageous skin-delivery characteristics. Conclusion and Discussion

We presented an integrated pipeline that combines ligand-based ML, multi tyrosinase-like structures docking, and two in vitro assays to identify novel tyrosinase inhibitors that are both potent and skin-permeable. From a large initial library of over 500,000 natural compounds and FDA drugs, three promising candidates, rhodanine-3-propionic acid, lodoxamide, and cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), were identified. All three compounds exhibited superior inhibitory activity against mushroom tyrosinase and enhanced skin permeability compared to the widely used cosmetic agent α-arbutin. Their favorable safety profiles, derived from their food-based or FDA-approved origins, suggest their potential as safer and effective alternatives for consideration in skin-lightning formulations. A significant advantage of this methodology is the use of computational tools to efficiently prioritize candidates from extensive chemical libraries. The ML models, trained on tyrosinase inhibition data, effectively predicted biological activity and pIC50 values, significantly reducing the number of compounds for downstream analysis. The subsequent docking simulations, performed using multiple tyrosinase-like experimental structures, provided detailed insights into binding interactions, ensuring the selection of candidates with strong affinity for the active site. This integrative strategy highlights the potential of combining ML and structure-based drug design to accelerate the discovery of enzyme inhibitors. Despite the promising results, several limitations should be noted. Due to the lack of high-resolution crystal structures for HsTYR, molecular docking relied on surrogate models, including AbTYR and HsTYRP1. These models are widely used due to their conserved active site geometry and metal coordination, which closely resemble HsTYR. Although AbTYR and HsTYRP1 share the same six-histidine-coordinated dinuclear metal center found in HsTYR, the secondary coordination sphere and the arrangement of surface hydrophobic residues around that center still differ from those of HsTYR. It may lead to discrepancies in binding predictions. , To further evaluate whether the screened compounds could bind to HsTYR, we performed homology modeling using SwissModel. The model was based on HsTYRP1 (PDB ID: 5M8R), which has the highest sequence identity with HsTYR (43%) and showed robust evaluation metrics (GMQE 0.67, QMEAN 0.74). Docking simulations of the three hit compounds, cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate), lodoxamide, and rhodanine-3-propionic acid, were conducted using this model. The results showed that all three compounds reproduced similar bidentate Cu-chelating poses and achieved respectable Glide scores of −8.96, −8.72, and −8.38 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure S5). Furthermore, the binding modes of these compounds were consistent with key interactions reported in previous studies of HsTYR inhibitors, including metal coordination (ligand-Cu distances of ∼2.0–2.5 Å), π–π stacking interactions with His and other aromatic residues, hydrogen bonding with polar side chains such as Ser, Glu, and Asn, and hydrophobic interactions with surrounding aliphatic residues. It is worth noting that the Cu–Cu spacing in the homology model was derived from the Zn–Zn spacing in HsTYRP1, resulting in a Cu–Cu distance of 3.55 Å. This is shorter than the experimentally observed Cu–Cu distance in AbTYR crystal structures (e.g., 4.36 Å for 2Y9X). Such deviations in the geometry of the metal center could affect docking accuracy, particularly in metal coordination interactions. Moreover, the global RMSD between the homology-modeled HsTYR structure and the template structure (PDB ID: 5M8R) was only 0.148 Å, indicating that the two structures are highly similar (Figure S6). In the binding pocket, we also found the orientations of amino acid side chains were also largely consistent between the two models. Notably, the key active site residues in HsTYR (His180, His202, His211, His363, His367, and His390) correspond well to those in HsTYRP1 (His192, His215, His224, His377, His381, and His404). This suggests that the binding pocket environments in the two structures are nearly identical, and ligand interactions at the active site are likely comparable. However, the replacement of Zn2+ with Cu2+ in the homology model introduces inaccuracies in the Cu–Cu positioning, which may lead to systematic errors in docking results, particularly in predicting metal coordination interactions and binding affinities. We also used AlphaFold3 to generate an HsTYR structure for comparison. As AlphaFold predicts apo structures without bound ligands, the resulting structure lacks the experimentally observed conformational details of the active site, particularly the precise geometry of the dicopper catalytic center. Docking simulations of the three hit compounds with the AlphaFold-predicted structure yielded Glide scores of −5.835 kcal/mol (cytidine 5′-(dihydrogen phosphate)), −5.31 kcal/mol (lodoxamide), and −6.050 kcal/mol (rhodanine-3-propionic acid), respectively, which were notably higher (indicating weaker binding) than the scores obtained using HsTYR-like experimental structures and the SwissModel homology model. This result suggests that our compounds may not bind as effectively to the AlphaFold model. However, it should be emphasized that AlphaFold3 was not specifically trained to model protein–ligand interactions or structures containing metal ions. Consequently, its predictions often lack the geometric precision required for accurately representing ligand-binding pockets , and the coordination environment of metal-active sites. Although metal ions can be manually incorporated into AlphaFold-generated structures, the resulting coordination information is qualitative and does not fully capture the precise structural and energetic characteristics needed for rigorous docking simulations. Additionally, the use of mushroom tyrosinase for in vitro activity assays, while cost-effective and widely accessible, does not fully replicate the biochemical properties of HsTYR. Previous studies ,,− have reported that some inhibitors effective against mushroom tyrosinase may exhibit reduced or no activity against human tyrosinase. For example, hydroquinone, kojic acid, and arbutin have IC50 values for HsTYR that are several-fold higher than their corresponding values for AbTYR. In contrast, Thiamidol (isobutylamido thiazolyl resorcinol) inhibits HsTYR with an IC50 of roughly 1 μM, but only weakly inhibits AbTYR with an IC50 of about 108 μM. It shows that a hit identified against the mushroom enzyme does not necessarily translate into activity against humans. Nevertheless, the observation of inhibitory activity against mushroom tyrosinase provides an important first layer of evidence, reducing the likelihood of identifying compounds that are completely inactive against tyrosinase family enzymes. Future studies should include direct testing of the identified compounds against HsTYR to confirm their inhibitory activity and determine their binding modes. Additionally, evaluating their effects on melanogenesis in human melanocyte cultures or 3D skin models will provide a more comprehensive understanding of their efficacy and safety in biologically relevant systems. These steps will be crucial for translating these compounds into practical applications.
