Abstract

Deep learning (DL) algorithms have increasingly been applied to predict protein-ligand binding affinity, a critical step in drug design. Yet, many models still struggle to generalize to unseen data, and when coupled with the absence of confidence estimates for predictions, they hinder effective decision-making. To address these challenges, we thoroughly compare five uncertainty quantification methods: Deep Ensemble, Monte Carlo Dropout, Laplace approximation, Bayes by Backprop, and Evidential Neural Networks. Notably, Bayes by Backprop-applied for the first time in this study area-offers a novel and promising approach to uncertainty quantification. To ensure unbiased training and validation, we leverage the Leak-Proof PDBBind dataset and rigorously evaluate performance across multiple external test sets. Our results reveal that a feed-forward neural network (FFNN) using extended connectivity interaction features (ECIF) as a protein-ligand representation, paired with the Bayes by Backprop method, achieves superior predictive performance and highly reliable uncertainty quantification. Notably, Bayes by Backprop demonstrated balanced performance across multiple evaluation metrics, particularly excelling in calibration without needing additional recalibration. Our findings not only advance the state of uncertainty quantification in deep learning models for binding affinity prediction but also open avenues for more reliable, calibrated, and reproducible applications in drug discovery and active learning-driven model development.

Introduction

Deep learning (DL) algorithms empower protein-ligand binding affinity predictions—a critical step in computer-aided drug discovery—yet they often yield inconsistent results across different trials, rendering them neither practical nor reliable1–4. This shortcoming stems from two intertwined key issues: (i) the generalization of proposed models and (ii) uncertainty in predicted values. Given limited access to all possible complexes for training a deep learning model, it is particularly necessary to assess the model generalization. Generalization of the model—its ability to transfer to unseen data—is commonly assessed using a test set that does not have any common members with the training set5. However, eliminating duplicate instances, called “hard overlap”, does not ensure an unbiased dataset meaning train and test sets with minimum similarities. This is because an unbiased dataset should also be devoid of any instances with similar characteristics or patterns, often known as “soft overlap”. The presence of soft overlap between the training and test sets might result in overly optimistic performance reports and undermine effective comparisons between different models6. For example, the commonly used PDBBind database train-test split, the most popular dataset for structure-based binding affinity prediction7, suffers from soft overlap issue5. In the common approach, the core set, which has the highest quality like high complex resolution, is reserved for testing, while the general set, a set with the least satisfied criteria, is used for training. This splitting leads to data leakage between the training and test sets, an issue that has been recently identified and spurred efforts to develop effective solutions8–10. In this regard, various solutions have been proposed, such as calculating structural, sequential, or ligand similarities to construct training, validation, and test sets based on different similarity thresholds5,11–14. Approaches like leave-cluster-out and clustered cross-validation effectively separate training and test sets for protein families15–18. Temporal splitting, another approach, simulates realistic drug discovery scenarios by establishing blind tests with members released only after a specific year9,19,20. Together, these approaches demonstrate that adjusting for “soft overlap” significantly changes the performance of deep learning-based models21,22. Even with unbiased train and test sets available, a trained model cannot consistently predict the binding affinity since each model has an inherent domain of applicability23,24. This challenge brings us to the second critical issue: the credibility of predicted values generated by a trained model. A common approach to investigate the applicability domain of a model is uncertainty quantification, which incorporates a confidence value or associated uncertainty with each predicted label25. Uncertainty, defined as a lack of knowledge, falls neatly into two classes: aleatoric and epistemic26. Aleatoric uncertainty is caused by intrinsic noise in measurements and can not be reduced. In contrast, epistemic uncertainty originates from the model framework, training processes, hyperparameters, and data scarcity and can often be reduced by improving model architecture or increasing dataset size26,27. Aleatoric uncertainty, unlike epistemic one, is beyond our control, yet we can delineate the contributions of each type of uncertainty within our model. Quantifying uncertainties and identifying their types are crucial for decision-making and assessing the credibility of a prediction24. To address the aforementioned uncertainties, various machine learning and deep learning-based binding affinity predictors have been developed in recent years. For example, Libouban et al.22 introduced a model utilizing an ensemble approach to quantify uncertainty in predicted values. ENS-Score employs a similar ensembling, but it uses conformal prediction to report confidence28. In another recent development in binding affinity prediction, the Geometric Interaction Graph Neural Network (GIGN)11, resorts to the Monte Carlo Dropout technique to quantify uncertainty. Despite the rise of diverse methods to quantify uncertainty, their application in deep learning models for protein-ligand binding affinity has remained sparse. As such, no systematic study has yet compared these approaches. In this study, we present the comparison of five uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods (see Fig. 1): Deep Ensemble29, Monte Carlo Dropout30, Laplace approximation31, Bayes by Backprop32, and Evidential Neural Networks (ENNs)33. We use Leak-Proof PDBBind21, a newly curated unbiased subset of the PDBBind dataset, for training and validation, alongside multiple external test sets for further evaluation. We evaluate several deep learning-based binding affinity predictors previously reported in the literature: (i) Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN) with Extended Connectivity Interaction Features (ECIF) for protein-ligand representation34, (ii) Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with the Multi-Shell Occurrence of Interatomic Contact (MS-OIC) scheme or OnionNet-235, (iii) DeepDTA36, and (iv) InteractionGraphNet (IGN)37. Our analyses reveal that the FFNN-ECIF model demonstrates the best performance in binding affinity prediction. We then evaluate the five UQ methods on FFNN-ECIF to determine the most effective technique. Methods › Binding affinity predictor › Model

Here, we construct four DL-based binding affinity predictors based on distinct neural network models. The first model incorporates feature generation method, including Extended Connectivity Interaction Feature (ECIF)34, and Feed-Forward Neural Networks (FFNNs). For the second model, we choose OnionNet-235, which utilizes the Multi-Shell Occurrence of Interatomic Contact (MS-OIC) scheme alongside Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for binding affinity prediction. Owing to the recent popularity of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)38, InteractionGraphNet (IGN)37 is also selected as the third model. For the last model, DeepDTA36 is selected, which only applies SMILES notation of the ligand and the primary protein structure, i. e., amino acids sequence, for predicting a protein-ligand binding affinity. Detailed information about models is available in the Model, and SMILES and the Protein Sequence sections of the SI and Tables S13 and S14.

Methods › Binding affinity predictor › Implementation

All models are implemented using the PyTorch package (v2.3.1)39. Hyperparameters are fine-tuned over 200 trials with the Optuna package (v3.6.1)40, a widely used tool for hyperparameter optimization—retaining default values for all other Optuna parameters. The optimized hyperparameters for each model are listed in Tables S9–S12. Training continues for 300 epochs, where L2 regularization, dropout, and early stopping (with a patience of 20 epochs) are applied to prevent overfitting. Model weights, except IGN, are initialized by the Kaiming Normal method in the “fan_in” mode, while the initial bias weights are set to 0.01. Given the random nature of neural network training, the reported results are based on an average and standard deviation calculated from five independently trained models. We quantify the results by Mean Square Error (MSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {R}_{\textrm{P}}$$\end{document}), and Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {R}_{\textrm{S}}$$\end{document}). Details of metrics are gathered in the Regression Metrics subsection within SI.

Methods › Uncertainty prediction › Uncertainty predictor model

UQ approaches, generally, fall into two categories: data-based and model-based methods41. Data-based methods define uncertainty in terms of a model’s applicability domain, using feature space, training set, and metrics like distance to assign uncertainty values42. Model-based methods, which are the focus of this manuscript, apply various ML or DL-based methods to estimate uncertainty26. We further divide DL-based methods into four subcategories: single deterministic, Bayesian, ensemble, and test-time augmentation methods26. In the single deterministic approach, both the prediction and its uncertainty are obtained through a single, non-random model evaluation, commonly referred to as a single deterministic forward pass. We selected Amini et al.’s extension of the evidential neural network (ENN) for regression as a candidate method43. Bayesian approaches, which estimate uncertainty by approximating intractable posterior distributions, include Monte Carlo (MC)-Dropout30, Laplace approximation31, and Bayes by Backprop32. Finally, For the ensemble category, we adopt the Deep Ensemble method29, which aggregates predictions from multiple independently trained models. We prioritize these methods for their ease of implementation and low inference cost. Among them, MC-Dropout, Deep Ensemble, and Laplace approximation are model-agnostic techniques and can be applied to pre-trained models. In contrast, the ENN requires a modified loss function for training, while Bayes by Backprop demands adjustments both to the model architecture and the loss function. Here, we excluded the test-time augmentation method as they are incompatible with our datasets. It is worth noting that this study focuses on overall uncertainty estimation and leaves the explicit distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty to future work. In what follows, we briefly describe each selected method, highlighting their assumptions, implementation details, and relevance to uncertainty estimation. Deep ensemble This technique offers a straightforward yet effective approach for estimating predictive uncertainty in neural networks (NNs). Deep Ensembles achieve this by training a diverse set of NNs while maintaining their independence through random weight initialization and data shuffling26. In this context, dropout effectively integrates over the models’ weights. In practice, we pass a data instance multiple (K) times, randomly dropping different neurons in each layer during each pass. The final result is the average of these K stochastic forward passes. Methods › Uncertainty prediction › Uncertainty evaluation

To evaluate and compare the quality of predictive uncertainty estimates, we employ a comprehensive set of metrics, including ranking-based, calibration-based, and proper scoring rules. We use Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient to assess how well the predicted uncertainties correlate with actual errors. For the calibration-based method, we adopt the Miscalibration Area (MCA), Root Mean Squared Calibration Error (RMSCE), and Mean Absolute Calibration Error (MACE), which quantify the alignment between predicted confidence and observed accuracy. The Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) serves as a proper scoring rule to estimate the deviation from the true probability distribution. In addition, Sharpness is used to evaluate the narrowness of the predictive distributions, regardless of whether the predictions are accurate or not. Here, predictive uncertainty refers to the variance of model predictions during inference. All metrics are computed using the open-source Uncertainty Toolbox (v0.1.1)46. A detailed description of each metric is provided in the Uncertainty Quantification Metrics subsection of the SI.

Results and discussion › Model performance and selection

The performance of binding affinity estimators—assuming sufficient data—depends on factors such as feature generation methods and the choice of ML or DL algorithms. To explore this diversity, we trained and tested four models: FFNN-ECIF, CNN-MS-OIC, DeepDTA, and IGN. The first two models, FFNN-ECIF and CNN-MS-OIC, belong to feature engineering methods and use FFNN and CNN algorithms, respectively. In contrast, DeepDTA and IGN are based on end-to-end representation learning, directly extracting relevant features from the raw representations of a protein-ligand complex. To determine an appropriate statistical test, we first examine the normality of the true and predicted labels, by calculating skewness and kurtosis47. Skewness, the third standardized moment, measures distribution symmetry: A value of zero indicates symmetry, while negative and positive values reflect left and right skew, respectively. Kurtosis, the fourth standardized moment, measures the tailedness of distribution or the frequency of outliers28. A kurtosis of zero marks a normal distribution, while negative values show leptokurtic behavior (heavier tails, more extreme outliers) and positive values signal platykurtic behavior (lighter tails, fewer extreme outliers)28. This test assumes the null hypothesis that both error delta distributions share the same population. A two-sided p-value below 0.05 indicates statistical significance. Results and discussion › Uncertainty quantification › Predictive intervals

Predictive intervals plots visualize how well model predictions align with true values46. Predictive intervals, defined here as the standard deviation of predictions (i.e., uncertainty values), provide insights into the variability or confidence in the model’s outputs. When true values frequently fall within the intervals, i.e., standard deviation, the model’s uncertainty is well-calibrated. Conversely, true values outside the intervals suggest an underestimation of uncertainty, while excessively wide intervals indicate overestimation. To explore these patterns and biases, we plot predictions and predictive intervals against ordered true values, sampling 100 instances from large datasets (LP-Test, BDB2020+, and Peptide-Holdout) for clarity. Fig. 5 shows the predictive intervals for Bayes by Backprop (see Figs. S11–S14 for other methods). This confirms our earlier conclusion that Bayes by Backprop is a well-calibrated model, as true values generally fall within predicted intervals even without recalibration.

These strengths make Bayes by Backprop particularly valuable for predictions in ODD scenarios and anomaly detection tasks, where reliable uncertainty estimates are crucial for identifying when models encounter unfamiliar data51,52.

Conclusions

In this study, we present the comparison of five UQ methods for binding affinity prediction. To achieve this, we used LP-PDBBind, an unbiased curation of the PDBBind dataset, along with external datasets (EGFR, M\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\textrm{pro}}$$\end{document}, BDB2020+, and Peptide-Holdout) for training, validation, and testing. We trained four deep learning models—FFNN-ECIF, CNN-MS-OIC, DeepDTA, and IGN—on the LP-Train dataset. FFNN-ECIF emerged as the best model when evaluated on the LP-Val dataset. Subsequently, we applied two popular UQ techniques, Deep Ensemble, and MC-Dropout, as well as three less commonly used approaches: Laplace approximation, Bayes by Backprop, and evidential neural networks. Our evaluation employed diverse ranking-based and calibration-based metrics alongside proper scoring rules. To complement these analyses, we examined calibration curves and predictive interval plots, which clearly established Bayes by Backprop as the top-performing UQ method. Bayes by Backprop showed accurate ranking-based, proper scoring rules, and sharpness metrics. This approach not only achieved strong results across ranking-based and sharpness metrics but also demonstrated superior calibration without requiring recalibration, making it particularly valuable for out-of-distribution predictions. In the near future, we will apply our findings to active learning algorithms, where the model actively selects the most informative data points for labeling through a query strategy, rather than passively using a pre-labeled dataset53,54. One such query strategy is uncertainty sampling, where data with the least confidence are selected for further training and labeling. We believe that applying FFNN-ECIF, augmented with Bayes by Backprop for UQ, alongside active learning can enhance and accelerate protein-ligand binding affinity prediction.
