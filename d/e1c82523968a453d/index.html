Abstract

Drug absorption can be altered due to the consumption of food, impacting the efficacy and safety of the drug administered, and predicting food effects (FE) can be quite complex. Traditional methods, including in vitro and in vivo models, fail to predict the full range of food-drug interactions owing to the biological variability of the gastrointestinal system. This review evaluates the predictive ability and accuracy of machine learning (ML) in predicting FE in comparison to conventional methods. We consider how ML models use food dataset information and assist in enhancing the formulation and dosing of the drugs. We discussed recent trends in FE prediction, its mechanisms, and effects on drug bioavailability. Supervised and unsupervised learning, as well as reinforcement learning, are analyzed in the context of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination (ADME) forecasting and drug development. ML is certainly useful in addressing the issues posed by traditional methods; however, challenges about data quality, model generalizability, and integration into the drug development process are obstacles that must be overcome. This review explains how other emerging technologies, for example, PBPK modeling, can be combined with ML to enhance its prospects in the field of drug development. We examined prospects of deep learning, explainable artificial intelligence (AI), and ethical and legal aspects of applying ML in pharmacokinetics, as well as the interdisciplinary approaches that are required to improve patient care outcomes.

Introduction

One of the most widespread and convenient ways to deliver medication is orally, with about 60% of all dosage forms in the worldwide market being oral [58]. The pharmacokinetics of oral doses of drugs is often known to be influenced by food intake. This is important for the drugs that are taken both short- and long-term to understand how different food-induced biological responses impact the bioavailability of these drugs [26]. Some of the physiological changes cause food effect (FE), and these include alterations of pH, the rate of gastric emptying, fluid volumes, bile salt concentrations, lymphatic transport, and intestinal enzyme activity. Moreover, the interactions of food with transporters and its impact on metabolism can also have a direct effect on drug absorption [37]. It has been found that a large percentage of drugs approved in 2010–2017 contained a large FE, and, therefore, FE predictability on drug absorption is relevant to the existing drug development procedure [37]. It is important to understand FE to formulate dosage forms that do not interact with food and to ensure the correct dosing of drugs, especially with narrow therapeutic windows. Hence, bioequivalence studies under the fed state are essential for guiding global regulators and helping to avoid drug failures due to the differences in drug exposure between the fed and fasted states [4]. It is important to manage and predict FE when developing new drugs. Traditional methods to predict FE are in vitro, in vivo, and in silico. In vitro studies usually use dissolution tests in biorelevant media, and in vivo models use animals like dogs and pigs to help predict human drug behavior. Although these methods have improved, there is still no single method accepted universally to predict FE [148]. Because of the many factors involved, such as drug type, meal ingredients, and health conditions, all current models have their limits and do not offer a comprehensive solution. Human studies are still considered the gold standard to predict FE,however, they are not always possible due to ethical, safety, and practical issues [26]. Due to the challenges with in vitro and in vivo studies, in-silico methods are now the main tools used in predictive biopharmaceutics. Simple guidelines to predict FE using drug physicochemical properties are available in the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) and the Biopharmaceutics Drug Disposition Classification System (BDDCS) [21]. Nevertheless, the BCS and BDDCS will only provide a very rough conception of what could be a food-drug interaction, but they are not accurate. Motivations behind their development in this special usage have been the requirement to have more sophisticated mechanistic or data-driven in silico models of prediction of FE. Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models are more important in the area of modeling and simulation, in order to make more precise predictions of FE [100]. Such models include mathematical equations used to derive descriptive human physiology and anatomy, and the drug absorption process. PBPK models can predict pharmacokinetic parameters (PK) in fed and fasted physiology, even in different species in preclinical research [2]. PBPK models need detailed data on physiological, biochemical, and physicochemical processes. Within the last 30 years, numerous attempts have been made to establish the correlation between FE and drug properties. Linear relationships between Area Under the plasma concentration–time Curve (AUC) and changes of drug solubility in simulated intestinal fluids after a meal have been established by computing tools [110]. Qualitative computational models (logistic regression or decision tree analysis) were constructed to predict the presence of a positive, negative, or no food effect of a drug based on the AUC ratio between the fed and fasting states. The modeling and simulation methods to predict FE on drug absorption are being developed. [56]. Attempts have been made to model the relationship between drug properties and FE, but the application of ML classification algorithms for the purpose of predicting FE has not been extensively explored [60]. The application of Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) algorithms, which are increasingly being implemented in drug design and development to optimize the output of decision making, has been implemented in preclinical testing [94]. SVM is a pattern recognition algorithm in data mining that has found extensive application in data mining and finds an optimal hyperplane that divides the various classes in an optimal margin. The data may be projected to a higher-dimensional representation where the data in that space may be split linearly with the help of kernel functions [20]. ANN extracts the trends of data that are extremely complex in the case of nonlinear data, and this is similar to how the human brain behaves. ANN has an input layer, a sequence of hidden layers, and outlay layer, and information flows among connections (synapses) between the layers, and the strength of connections (synapses) is assigned. The ANN makes predictions through updating the weights of its neurons and activation functions. The architecture can provide detailed relationships, and this is why ANN can be applied in the prediction of the outcomes of food on drug absorption [7]. This review aims to discover how ML can be applied in the prediction of the effects of food on drugs. The key ML methods that will be employed to predict FE will be introduced, like supervised learning and new methods, including deep learning. This review will also mention the application of common methods of using ML models and other tools, e.g., the PBPK models, to enhance the accuracy and usefulness of FE predictions. By analyzing the recent studies, we will see how the strategies founded on the principles of ML can offer opportunities and challenges and can be applied to decrease reliance on regular testing, as well as accelerate drug development. Pharmacokinetic classification of food effects

Introduction › Food effects and current approaches in drug development

FE plays an important role in PK of drugs, which affects the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of the drug (ADME) [72]. Depending on the physicochemical properties of a drug, food consumed, and the time when the food is consumed in relation to drug intake, the influence of foods on the bioavailability of a drug can be very diverse [4]. These processes require research, and FE should be taken into account in the development of drugs [148]. In this section, the mechanisms of FE will be discussed, types, clinical aspects of food effects, and currently available methods of studying FE.

Introduction › Mechanisms of food effects

The consumption of food is one of the significant determinants of oral drug absorption, leading to numerous biological events [37]. The intake of food can also change the gastrointestinal (GI) system, which could affect the effectiveness of drugs regarding pH, motility, gastric emptying, intestinal transit time, secretion of bile, and blood flow [4]. As an example, to demonstrate this, the viscosity of the gastrointestinal tract is higher after a meal, which decreases the absorption of drugs by the epithelial absorptive surface and can decrease bioavailability [128]. Bioavailability of lenalidomide decreases after food intake, partially due to the physical barrier created by food [24]. Food intake also slows gastric emptying, which delays absorption, but the extent of drug absorption is usually not affected. For example, capecitabine and duloxetine have delayed Time to reach the maximum plasma concentration (Tmax) after meals, but their overall absorption is not affected [26]. Food also changes the pH of the stomach from 1.7 to about 5.0 after eating. The change in pH can increase the dissolution and absorption of weakly acidic or basic drugs by increasing the proportion of the nonionized form, which is more permeable to the intestinal mucosa [3]. However, for drugs sensitive to pH variations, changes in pH could either enhance or reduce absorption, depending on the drug’s stability in acidic or alkaline environments [50]. Food also increases splanchnic blood flow, which can affect drug metabolism, particularly for drugs with high hepatic extraction ratios [72].

Introduction › Types of food effects

When the 90% confidence intervals of the population geometric mean ratios, derived from log-transformed data, fall outside the 80–125% range for either the Area Under the Curve from time 0 to infinity (AUC₀ → ∞) or the Maximum Plasma Concentration (Cmax) compared with the fasted state, the result is classified as a food effect. The evaluation of FE relies on bioavailability parameters, which include Cmax, Tmax, and AUC [131]. These parameters assist in the identification of the FE on the absorption of drugs. Welling categorized food-drug interactions into five types [139]: (1) Reduced bioavailability, (2). Slower absorption rate (3). Increased bioavailability (4). Faster absorption rate, and (5). No impact. Of these, the most important is the impact on bioavailability, which can be defined as either a positive or a negative food effect (Fig. 1). Although some variability is allowed, significant differences in bioavailability between the fed and fasted states may be relevant for clinical practice. For this reason, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has provided recommendations for clinical trials in which testing must be done under both fasted and fed states. The fed state study requires the administration of a standardized high-fat FDA breakfast (800–1000 kcal, with approximately 50% of calories from fat) to increase the chances of observing a FE [77, 109]. Pharmacokinetic classification of food effects

Introduction › Types of food effects › Positive food effects

A significant consequence of positive food effects is the increased dissolution of lipophilic compounds, such as poorly water-soluble drugs (PWSDs), in the fed state. During digestion, bile salts and other solubilizers, like dietary fats and their digestion products, improve the ability of gastrointestinal fluids to solubilize food. For drugs where the dissolution is rate-limiting, a longer gastric residence time can also enhance bioavailability. Additionally, an elevated gastric pH can improve the bioavailability of certain drugs, particularly weakly acidic compounds, by enhancing their solubility and dissolution rate. These factors together complicate the task of isolating the specific impact of each on the solubility and dissolution of PWSDs [30, 31, 40]. The inhibition of intestinal transporters can also increase the bioavailability of certain drugs. In the case of BCS class II compounds, Wu and Benet have demonstrated that efflux transporters play a dominant role, and blocking these transporters may improve bioavailability [123]. In addition, the bioavailability can be enhanced in the fed state because first-pass metabolism is less when blood flow is altered, lymphatic uptake is increased, and enteric metabolism is reduced [149]. The splanchnic blood flow may be augmented by as much as 60% during food intake, depending on the meal size and its content. This results in more drugs being able to bypass the liver, and on the other hand, higher hepatic blood flow may decrease the first pass for drugs with low to moderate clearance [72]. For lipophilic drugs, the intake with food helps their absorption through the lipid absorption pathways, and for lipophilic drugs with high log P values (> 5), a significant amount of the drug may be transported via the lymphatics. This process increases whole-stomach availability by the amount of drug that is not trapped in the lumen of the stomach and also reduces the first-pass metabolism [22, 40, 76]. Another factor is the ability of some foods to suppress the enzyme Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), that results in a decrease in the enteric drug metabolism and increased bioavailability. For example, grapefruit juice is an antagonist of CYP3A4 and has been associated with changes in the pharmacokinetics of many drugs and side effects. In spite of the inhibitory effects of dietary monoglycerides and bile salts on enzymatic activity in vitro, it is postulated that a high-fat meal could cause clinically relevant enzyme inhibition. However, these interactions have not been well described in vivo [23].

Introduction › Types of food effects › Negative food effect

Negative food effects include a decrease in drug absorption and a delayed absorption rate. The Immediate-Release (IR) formulations in a fed state can exhibit an absorption lag with minimal or no effect on the total bioavailability. This lag is attributed to slowed gastric motility, increasing Tmax, and possibly or not reducing Cmax, or slowing the onset of action. These delays tend to be insignificant with drugs intended to be used chronically when the total exposure to the drug is of greater importance than the maximum plasma concentration [117]. Nevertheless, absorption can be lower in the fed condition, which may cause a decrease in AUC and Cmax, and probable failure of treatment. Direct interactions involving the drug or its excipients and food are the main factors that result in a decrease in bioavailability in the fed state [109]. Moreover, as the upper GI tract is viscously increased due to a meal, the drug may not disintegrate, or there may be less surface area to absorb it. This effect is especially undesirable for low-permeability drugs with narrow absorption windows because the elevated viscosity in the upper GI tract may hinder the absorption of the drug before it gets to the distal gut, where the absorption is minimal [108, 142]. A further interaction is the adsorption of drugs to the food components, a process that is most frequently connected with polyvalent cations contained in the dairy products [95]. Physiological factors also contribute to adverse food effects, particularly for drugs that are unstable or acid-labile in the GI tract. While food intake increases gastric pH, which may decrease the degradation of acid-labile compounds, these drugs may still be degraded more rapidly because of prolonged exposure to the stomach. Moreover, food may affect drug absorption, enhancing passive and active transport. In the fed state, lipids and bile salts are increased, which leads to an increase in micelle formation and decreases the concentration of free drug. Poor solubility can reduce absorption, but it can be counteracted by high permeability. On the other hand, a low permeability with a high solubility may result in low absorption [19]. Negative food effects are due to the inhibition of uptake transporters. It is important to consider the FE for drugs with low permeability, which depend on the uptake transporter for their absorption. The overall reduction of the expression of intestinal transporters in the fed state can explain the lack of bioavailability of BCS class III drugs in the fed state. This is so provided that the drug in question depends on the uptake or efflux transporters, and the extent of inhibition of the transporters has the capacity to alter the bioavailability of the drug [33]. For example, fexofenadine (BCS class III) drug has negative food effects, which is caused by the Organic Anion Transporting Polypeptides (OATPs) and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) efflux transporters. The OATP inhibition is dominant during the fed state, and in particular, when it is mixed with fruit juices, which cause the absorption to decrease. OATP transporters have been demonstrated to be inhibited by fruit juices, resulting in poor AUC of drugs, i.e., aliskiren and celiprolol. In vigabatrin, although decreased absorption could be associated with the inhibition of Proton-Coupled Amino Acid Transporter 1 (PAT1), the major cause is delayed gastric emptying [41, 42, 44].

Introduction › Clinical implications

FE is important to consider during drug development and clinical practice [120, 132]. Oral medications with narrow therapeutic windows or susceptible to significant food–drug interactions can be significantly influenced by food. FE has multiple clinical implications on drug efficacy, safety, patient compliance, and overall treatment management [129].

Introduction › Clinical implications › Bioavailability variability

Food intake can impact the absorption rates and extents of many drugs, though the effects are not always clinically significant [37]. For example, the intake of abacavir sulfate with food causes the Tmax to be delayed, but not to change its efficacy, because it merely delays the onset of its anti‑HIV activity, which was a less critical issue with the long-term use of the drug [28]. Hence, unlike food, which decreased cilazapril absorption by about 14%, this small decrease has usually been considered clinically irrelevant [87]. In the case of roxithromycin [107], an antimicrobial agent where efficacy depends on both drug concentration and duration of exposure, food intake decreased overall plasma concentrations, however, levels remained above the minimum inhibitory concentration, thus preserving therapeutic effectiveness [37]. For drugs like tolterodine [97], food significantly increased bioavailability, yet the therapeutic effect remained unchanged because the active form is the metabolite, which is unaffected by food. However, for drugs where the metabolite is toxic, such as oral idarubicin, the impact of food on metabolism becomes clinically important and should be carefully considered [124].

Introduction › Clinical implications › Formulation design

In response to the challenges posed by FE on drug absorption, the pharmaceutical industry has developed formulations designed to ensure consistent drug uptake regardless of food intake [96]. Bio-enabling formulation strategies attempt to improve the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs and reduce food-induced variability in drug absorption [90]. Lipid-based formulations like self-emulsifying systems improve solubilization and reduce food effects [73] as seen with cyclosporin [46] and rivaroxaban [141]. Nanosized drug preparations increase surface area and improve dissolution rates and reduce food-related absorption variations [64], exemplified by fenofibrate [57] and megestrol acetate [39]. Formation of inclusion complexes on cyclodextrins decreases food dependency and improves drug solubility. [5], for example, by amiodarone [135] and ziprasidone [38]. Solid dispersions and amorphization [116] increase drug dissolution and solubility, as seen with anacetrapib [74], celecoxib [9], prodrugs such as fostemsavir [89], and ABBV‑167 [112] improve solubility and avoid food effects via enzymatic conversion. Each of these strategies increases drug efficacy, decreases FE variability in absorption, and enhances patient compliance.

Introduction › Clinical implications › Food-drug interaction management

Food-drug interactions have to be carefully managed in clinical practice to achieve the best therapeutic outcomes [129]. Healthcare professionals must know about the possible effects of food on drug absorption, metabolism, and excretion. Food-drug interactions are an important aspect of patient counseling [11]. For instance, patients should be informed which medications ought to be taken on an empty stomach, for example, levothyroxine, if the presence of food leads to reduced absorption [80]. Some specific drugs, for example, antibiotics like tetracycline or ciprofloxacin, form complexes with calcium, magnesium, and iron from food, inhibiting absorption and efficacy of the drug [140]. However, some medicines function best if one takes them along with food to aid their absorption, especially lipophilic medications like cyclosporine and ritonavir that are better absorbed if taken with food that contains fat because this facilitates increased bile secretion and lymphatic transport [109]. Therefore, healthcare providers need to convey to patients when to take their drugs in relation to eating to minimize or optimize food-drug interactions.

Introduction › Clinical implications › Regulatory guidelines

The FDA and European Medicines Agency (EMA) mandate that bioavailability and bioequivalence studies be conducted under both fed and fasting conditions. For the fed state, it is recommended to use a high-calorie meal, approximately 850–1000 kcal, consisting of around 150 kcal from protein, 250 kcal from carbohydrates, and 500–600 kcal from fats. This meal should be consumed 30 min before the administration of the test drug with 240 mL of water. The effect on drug absorption is evaluated by comparing the AUC and Cmax between the fasted and fed states. Bioequivalence is typically determined when the 90% confidence intervals of the ratios of these parameters fall between 80 and 125% [109].

Introduction › Current methods for studying food effects › In vitro methods

In vitro models have been developed to mimic the human intestinal lumen environment, allowing for the assessment of drug permeability and the identification of potential FE [47]. One commonly used model involves a monolayer of human colon epithelial cells (Caco-2 cells), which mimics the intestinal mucosa and serves as a useful tool for studying the transport of drug candidates from the intestinal lumen into systemic circulation [75]. Permeability studies conducted earlier used Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) as a buffered transport solution to ensure ionic balance and cell viability. Unfortunately, hydrophilic solutions are problematic because most new drug candidates are lipophilic, and a poor solubility in an aqueous medium means that their recovery is low, due to intracellular accumulation or adsorption to plastic surfaces [78]. Recently, there has been a tendency to use simulated intestinal fluids (SIFs) that are closer to the physiological conditions of the intestine. Bile salts (e.g., taurocholate), phospholipids (e.g., phosphatidylcholine and lecithin), and fatty acids are included in the fasted state simulated intestinal fluid (FaSSIF) and fed state simulated intestinal fluid (FeSSIF) [70]. Biorelevant media have eventually been developed to better mimic the human in vivo conditions [49, 62, 86]. A number of studies that have compared biorelevant media with the buffer solutions have found the biorelevant media provide a more predictive estimate of in vivo intestinal absorption [69]. Biorelevant media have been employed in a variety of in vitro studies to determine the dissolution and permeability of drugs. A transfer model is generally used to define drug solubility and precipitation in both a fed and fasted state with a scaled-down USP paddle apparatus [71]. It models the movement of dissolved and undissolved drugs from the stomach to the small intestine. An example of these is the dissolution of nelfinavir mesylate in FaSSIF-V2 and FeSSIF-V2 was studied in vivo by using the scaled-down paddle method to determine FE. In vitro, positive food effect was observed with the fed-to-fasted dissolution ratio increasing 11-fold. This had exaggerated the real fivefold increase in healthy human volunteers [122]. Dissolution was quicker in the fasted state simulated gastric fluid (FaSSGF) compared to FaSSIF-V2, leading to supersaturation and drug precipitation in the fasted small intestine. However, solubility in the fed state simulated gastric fluid (FeSSGF) and FeSSIF-V2 was similar, and no supersaturation or precipitation occurred in the fed state. These findings highlight the complexity of drug absorption and the challenges in accurately simulating the actions of both the stomach and small intestine in vitro [122]. While in vitro studies offer valuable insights, they do not reliably predict human outcomes because the transfer model omits absorption, which can significantly impact drug precipitation, especially for BCS Class II weak bases. Nevertheless, in vitro data can be incorporated into PBPK models to help characterize changes in drug pharmacokinetics under fasted and fed conditions [32].

Introduction › Current methods for studying food effects › In vivo methods

FE evaluations rely on canines as experimental animals due to their natural ability to eat solid formulations with food types matching human diet compositions. They present some comparable GI features to those observed in human bodies. FE studies conducted with canines may produce excessive results compared to human responses due to biological limitations of using such models [88]. The distinction emerges between species due to their different physical make-up and biological functions, which creates challenges when attempting direct human clinical trial applications. The digestive structures of dogs differ from those of humans because their small and large intestines are shorter in length, and their beagle dogs maintain higher fasting gastric pH levels [126]. To address this, pentagastrin has been utilized to lower the gastric pH in dogs, bringing it closer to human levels, thereby ensuring that study results are more representative of human outcomes [145]. The use of pentagastrin in dogs provides researchers with a way to match gastric acid conditions between animal and human subjects. Sjögren and colleagues reviewed the digestive systems of various animal models, noting that rats, mice, pigs, and dogs are commonly used to investigate FE [126]. Mathematical models can be used to describe the anatomy and physiology of animal models, and such models have been suggested to give more accurate predictions than the conventional allometric scaling methods [26]. The Advanced Compartmental Absorption and Transit Model (ACAT) used on species-specific parameters predicting the FE of theophylline (BCS Class I drug) could be used to predict a prediction that was similar both in dogs and humans [100]. However, this model could not be used to adequately predict the plasma concentration–time profile of aprepitant, which is a poorly soluble drug. The differences that arose, particularly in starved dogs, were explained by poor approximations of regional solubility, which led to overestimation of food-increased solubility effects [100]. The validation of PBPK models in the animal system requires preclinical in vivo studies of FE [26].

Introduction › Current methods for studying food effects › In silico methods

Initial mathematical models of the FE on pharmacokinetics utilized a one-, two-, and three-compartment distribution model [130, 143, 144]. Food in these models was simply considered as a covariate, categorical, and was not presented in relation to the physiological changes of the GI tract, which occur during the consumption of food. The first test of the sustained release of fenofibrate was conducted on an open one-compartment model,the time curve of plasma concentration was subsequently plotted with the help of data obtained on the patients. More commonly, PBPK and mechanism-based PK (MBPK) models are now being used to examine the effect of food on drug absorption. The MBPK analysis of fenofibrate was done to estimate the PK parameters under the fasting, normal meal, and high-fat meal conditions. The model performed well in the prediction of the outcome, and AUC was 1.65 and 1.86 times after a normal meal and a high-fat meal, respectively, compared to fasting. These methods help scientists to measure the change in the concentration of the drug because of the FE and to make sure that the ratio of AUC and Cmax not below the limits established by the regulatory authorities [12]. The clinical data were also used to optimize important PK parameters, which included gastric emptying rate and bile acid effects. A simple one-compartment model as well as a complex MBPK model depicted an improvement in Cmax and AUC. The latter was, however, a more mechanistic understanding of the effect of different physiological parameters on the absorption between fasted and fed conditions (of standard and high-fat meals). Mechanism-based models can also be expanded to PBPK models, which will provide a better insight into the mechanisms underlying the effects of food on oral drug absorption. For more details about the PBPK and PBBM, see [102, 103, 147]. Food intake activates various biological processes, and these changes can be integrated into mechanistic PBPK models by modifying relevant model parameters. Software tools like GastroPlus® and SimCYP® are commonly used to evaluate the FE on compounds from all BCS classes [102, 103] [6]. Although these tools differ in the physiological parameters used to characterize prandial states (e.g., gastric emptying time, gastric volume, and bile salt concentrations), they typically rely on proprietary models (such as ACAT and Advanced Dissolution, Absorption, and Metabolism (ADAM) [26]. In contrast, other platforms enable researchers to develop PBPK models tailored to their needs, incorporating compartments that represent different regions of the gastrointestinal tract, linked by differential equations to model the key processes involved in oral absorption [36]. To predict FE, PBPK models are employed to estimate AUC, Cmax, and Tmax in both fed and fasted situations. The use of these parameters helps determine if FE is present or not by comparing AUC and Cmax ratios to the regulatory standards (generally 80–125%). Predicting the outcomes allows PBPK models to be important tools for FE simulation before clinical trials.

Introduction › Current methods for studying food effects › In silico methods

While PBPK models are predictive, there remains a large amount of uncertainty for predicting drug absorption and FE. The problem comes when publication bias, which exaggerates successful results, exacerbates the problem, and the models are limited by low confidence in predictions. They are becoming ever more sophisticated in vitro and in vivo experiments to mimic human conditions, but still do not reproduce clinical behavior. The current PBPK models that use a wide range of parameter values do not adequately describe the complex biological changes following food consumption, and more research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms that underlie FE [26]. Little is known about physiological changes like lymphatic uptake that lead to increased drug concentration in systemic circulation following high-fat meals. As a case in point, a PBPK model was unable to simulate the absorption of venetoclax following a high-fat diet, and it is exceedingly difficult to simulate the effects of food, particularly in the case of lipophilic substances [43]. There are still gaps in the knowledge about bile-micelle binding, enzyme activity, and transporter interactions of FE as well. Drug absorption is also difficult to predict due to individual diets and food composition differences and this factor is not always included in models. Also, the gastrointestinal activity is nonhomogeneous due to various eating habits, and it makes the matter even more difficult. FE studies have been carried out in adults, and there has been insufficient research on pediatric and special populations. The absorption of drugs is even more complicated in children in relation to food because the physiological changes are fast. These groups have insufficient data and, therefore are challenging to test PBPK models, although sensitivity analyses may be used to approximate the accuracy of the unavailable data [136]. Lastly, PBPK models are useful during drug development but are not sufficient enough to make prediction. The current limitation of the models is defined by the fact that the biology that takes place after a meal is not well known. Thus, the conclusions that can be obtained with the help of PBPK models must be approached with care and additional research is necessary to seal the knowledge gaps concerning the food-drug interactions. [26].

Introduction to machine learning and data science

ML and data science have greatly changed pharmaceutical research by making it possible to derive meaningful patterns from complex biological and chemical datasets [133]. The computational power used by ML algorithms (Classification of ML algorithms (Fig. 2)) allows analyzing vast amounts of data, speeding up the process of new drug discovery, development, and the upstream regulatory process. One of the features of ML is that it can discover non-linear relations and do data-driven predictions, which provides new insight about drug behavior and patient responses, and distinguishes itself from traditional statistical techniques.ML offers numerous applications in pharmaceutical research, driven by the exponential growth of big data and the availability of large medical datasets, enabled by high-performance computational techniques [84]. The datasets in which these statistics were derived are high-throughput screens, genomic sequencing, electronic health, and molecular simulation. With its ability to integrate and analyze diverse types of data, ML enables more accurate predictions of drug–target interactions, pharmacokinetic modeling, and toxicity assessment, significantly reducing both the duration and cost of drug development projects [119]. The main strength of ML in pharmaceutical research is that it enables the discovery of obscured correlations that would otherwise remain hidden in a conventional statistical analysis. In particular, this capability can be used in the prediction of drug efficacy or optimization of formulations and the identification of biomarkers of disease progression. Besides that, the use of ML models is also applied in clinical trials to select the appropriate groups of patients based on medical data and, consequently, reduce the number of failures by establishing the potential safety issues at an earlier stage of the development cycle [133]. Its practical applications have been expanded with the combination of ML and artificial intelligence (AI) and other computational methods, such as deep learning and natural language processing (NLP). With the help of artificial intelligence systems, scientists acquire a vast opportunity to process large scientific databases and find valuable discoveries that give rise to experimental and testable hypotheses. Researchers access AI-driven machine learning platforms to stay informed about current developments in drug discovery [94]. Due to the rapid increase of ML-based solutions, pharmaceutical firms and research institutes are more inclined towards automated pipelines for drug screens, virtual simulation, and precision medicine strategies. Besides this, these advancements contribute towards the personalized treatment approach by accounting for the therapy for the patient’s individual characteristics. The continued evolution of the field is oriented towards the development of interpretable and explainable ML models, maintaining the viability and acceptance by the regulator of AI methodologies in pharmaceutical research [101, 119, 133]. Introduction to machine learning and data science › Types of ML models › Supervised learning

In supervised learning, an algorithm is trained on a dataset with labels, i.e., we know what the desired output is. It learns to map input data to corresponding output by finding patterns and relationships in the labeled data. This approach is widely used in various application areas, such as image recognition, natural language processing, and pattern prediction. Task-driven strategies focus on achieving specific outcomes based on a given combination of inputs. Labeled data is used to train algorithms for tasks such as data classification or outcome prediction. In supervised learning, classification involves predicting a label, while regression is used to predict a continuous quantity. Several techniques can be employed to address supervised learning tasks, depending on the problem domain. These techniques include Naïve Bayes, K-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, ensemble learning, random forest, linear regression, and support vector regression [35, 53, 114].

Introduction to machine learning and data science › Types of ML models › Unsupervised learning

Unsupervised learning involves training algorithms on data without labeled outputs, where the algorithm is not explicitly told what patterns to identify. Instead, it must discover patterns and relationships in the data on its own. It is often used as an exploratory data analysis to find out the hidden structure or clusters in the dataset. It is a data-driven method, which means insights, patterns, and structures are discovered directly from the data without the need for annotations. Unsupervised learning tasks include clustering, dimensionality reduction, visualization, association rule mining, and anomaly detection. For clustering, techniques such as hierarchical clustering, K-means, K-medoids, single linkage, complete linkage, and BOTS are used, and for association learning algorithms and feature selection, Pearson correlation and principal component analysis are popular, based on the nature of data [13, 133].

Introduction to machine learning and data science › Types of ML models › Reinforcement learning (RL)

Reinforcement is an AI technique in which training algorithms make optimal decisions by learning from sequential interactions. Unlike supervised learning, where a model learns from labeled datasets, RL models learn through trial and error by getting the feedback of rewards or penalties [13].

ML is widely used to determine the PK parameters and to predict FE during drug development. [45, 60]. Food-drug interactions cause a change in the bioavailability of drugs, which results in failure to achieve the desired therapeutic outcome [127]. This portion deals with how ML can be used to predict the effect of food on drug absorption, especially in an end-to-end process (Workflow for Predicting Food Effects shown in Fig. 5), including data requirements, feature engineering, model development, interpretability, and validation. Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Data requirements and feature engineering

The quality and the diversity of the data set used to train the ML models relies on the validity of the results of the models in predicting the FE on drug absorption [138]. The dataset is expected to have a model of drugs that have varying biopharmaceutical classifications (BCS). Typically, it has many physicochemical, biopharmaceutical, and pharmacokinetic properties that are critical in training the machine learning algorithms [68]. Important physicochemical parameters influencing drug absorption are the molecular weight (MW), polar surface area (PSA), hydrogen bond (HP), and lipophilicity (LogP). Such values are typically retrieved using the existing databases such as PubChem, DrugBank, and other previously developed models [16, 68, 111]. Fraction absorbed (Fa), intestinal permeability, solubility, and dose/solubility ratios are other aspects of the FE on drug absorption and bioavailability [98]. The chosen feature engineering process is the process of selection of most relevant features, which are known to influence food-drug interaction, such as the solubility, permeability, and physiological factors that influence the absorption. The training of the various ML models is grounded on the physicochemical and biopharmaceutical preprocessed data [111]. The modes of feature selection are meant to select the most important of the variables among the variables available to predict the effects of food, hence making the models accurate and effective [84].

ML models for predicting FE on drug absorption go through a number of key steps, including algorithm selection, data preparation, model training, and evaluation.

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Model development › Algorithm selection

The choice of ML algorithms that will lead to the FE prediction depends on the nature of the data, what the prediction aims to achieve, and the sophistication of relationships within the data [56]. FE prediction is typically done by supervised learning algorithms, including Random Forests (RF), SVM, and Gradient Boosting Machines (GBMs) [16]. Scenario models have been formulated wherein the datasets, which are labeled, have been made available to represent known food-drug interaction outcomes. In situations where the interactions between drug properties and food types affecting absorption are too complex and nonlinear to be modeled with more traditional mathematical models, more modern methods based on neural networks, such as feedforward neural networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), are frequently used to learn such complex patterns. In addition, it is also possible to use ensemble techniques, such as stacking or bagging, to integrate multiple individual models to not only boost predictive accuracy but also render the predictions resistant to overfitting [115].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Model development › Data preprocessing and handling imbalanced data

One of the most challenging tasks in the ML model development is to prepare the dataset sufficiently. However, in the case of FE prediction, it is quite common that the datasets that are available are imbalanced, and some food effect categories are underrepresented [15]. To this end, stratified sampling techniques are used to ensure that the distribution of FE categories (positive, negative, and no effect) in the training set covers the overall distribution. For example, random oversampling techniques can be used to artificially over-sample the minority classes (for instance, when there are more ‘no effect’ cases than ‘positive’ or ‘negative’ food effects) to safely over-represent these classes during the model training. This is achieved by increasing the number of instances of underrepresented classes in an attempt to improve model learning and to increase generalization to new instances [121].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Model development › Model evaluation and performance metrics

Several metrics are used to check the performance of the trained models, like the Confusion matrix such as, sensitivity (recall), precision (positive predictive value), and the Kappa statistic [93]. Sensitivity and precision truly depict the model's capability in detecting correct positive and negative food effects, and Kappa is developed to measure the agreement between observed and expected accuracy, with greater Kappa values representing better model performance. The AUC is calculated for each class pair (e.g., “no effect,” “positive effect,” “negative effect”), as an indicator of the model’s discriminatory power for the different food-effect categories for more in-depth evaluation [1].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Model development › Model training and hyperparameter tuning

Once the appropriate algorithm is chosen, then comes the second step of train the model using the dataset prepared. Then, the training process consists of changing the internal parameters of the ML model until its prediction of FE is optimized. Tuning hyperparameters is key to the successful training of a model and has a great impact on model performance [17]. It is required to select proper hyperparameters, such as the number of trees in a random forest, the degree of the polynomial kernel in SVMs [20], or the learning rate in gradient boosting machines. Usually, grid search techniques are used to find the optimal hyperparameter values for this purpose. When training, cross-validation techniques, e.g., tenfold cross-validation, are utilized to assess whether the model generalizes and to prevent overfitting. Here, we split the dataset into several subsets and train the model on a subset and test it on the other data in each cycle [137].

The other important factor is model interpretability. In order to understand which variables are most important in predicting the FE, one can quantify feature importance (the AUC of each variable predicting class), it tells which variables are the most important in the prediction. And research techniques like varImp in the caret package of R may be used to comprehend the significance of factors like the solubility and permeability of the drug, properties at the molecular level, and gastrointestinal considerations. It assists in ranking features based on the effect they have on predicting food, and prompts further optimizations of the model [8]. Additionally, the verification of the developed model is highly significant to ensure that it can be considered reliable. This involves testing the trained models on new (independent) data or on new representative data to test their ability to be generalized to new situations. It also guarantees the confirmation of such a model, which would enable the application of such a model to be resistant and adjust to various situations of drug-food interactions [137].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Model interpretability

Transparency of ML models is an essential part of drug development, particularly where the regulatory approval or the clinical use is at stake [79]. This is where methods of describing feature importance in food classification, such as Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), can be applied [113]. Such techniques help the researcher determine the most significant features of food composition or the properties of drugs that cause changes in bioavailability caused by the intake of food. In the case of drugs that are undergoing clinical trials, there is a need for the regulatory bodies to see the transparency of the model. Although many ML models are not interpretable, the use of interpretable approaches helps us to ensure that the predictions are accurate and understandable, which is significant to drug development stakeholders [91].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Validation and evaluation

ML models can only be trusted with a full and stringent validation and evaluation process. In many cases, cross-validation (e.g., k-fold) and external validation using separate datasets are used to measure the performance of models [59]. It is based on external validation to make sure that the prediction of the model is not based on particular training data, but can also forecast new and unseen data. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score are the most significant performance metrics to predict food effects models. These are the measures that can be applied in order to see the goodness of the model that predicts food-drug interactions [99]. Besides, in order to demonstrate its usefulness in practice, it will be necessary to use real clinical data to validate the predictions of the ML, and the model outputs can be integrated with experimental or patient data to estimate the usefulness of the model in the real-life setting [67].

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Case studies and applications

ML has already been successfully implemented in various fields regarding the prediction of FE and optimization of drug development [119]. FE prediction on drug pharmacokinetics is quite critical with the help of ML and AI, as large and diverse datasets of preclinical and clinical studies can be analyzed [115]. These technologies are aimed at seeing trends, finding complex interrelationships, and seeing trends that are not visible with traditional methods. Data-driven approaches in AI/ML help food–drug interaction researchers to learn more about food–drug interactions and formulate drugs optimally in early stages of drug development [115]. Using a dataset of 311 oral drugs, which are described by 23 in silico calculated physicochemical features, Gavins et al. predict FE with various ML models. This is a large dataset for FE prediction, compared to studies that used much smaller numbers of compounds (e.g., 53) [51]. Random Forest (RF) was found to be better than logistic regression, SVM, and k-NN in all three classification tasks, with accuracies and specificities in the range of 70% to 80%. The factor that most accurately predicted the results was the dose number, which tied drug solubility to changes in food absorption. The study clearly showed that ML is useful for FE prediction, which makes it important for early drug development because this model uses in silico calculated features that may be the only data available at early-stage drug development. The limitation of this study was the exclusion of formulation type and pKa, which are known to influence FE [52]. Sharma et al. supervised ML models that were applied to a dataset comprising 309 drugs to identify the key physicochemical and physiological properties influencing FE on oral drug absorption. FE was classified into positive, negative, and no food effects. Input features included drug properties such as logP, dose number, pKa, permeability, and efflux saturation index, as well as physiological parameters like bile acid-mediated solubilization and splanchnic blood flow. The study found that drugs with high logP (≥ 4.3), high dose number, and high extraction ratios were more likely to exhibit a positive FE, whereas those with low permeability and high efflux saturation were associated with negative FE. For weakly acidic drugs (pKa > 4.3), there was a greater likelihood of positive FE because of increased ionization and delayed gastric emptying in the fed state. The prediction accuracy of the ML model was 60% and was better than the conventional BCS, which is about 50%. This shows concretely how ML can not only predict FE outcomes but also identify the most important features influencing FE [121]. Bennett Lenane et al. classified drugs approved between 2016 and 2020 into three categories: positive, negative, or no food effect and used Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models to analyze over 250 drug properties. The ANN model had higher predictive accuracy than the SVM model (82% in training and 72% in testing) and the SVM model (72% in training and 69% in testing). The BCS was accurate only 46% of the time, and the ANN performed significantly better than the BCS. Octanol–Water Partition Coefficient (S + logP), Number of Hydrogen Bond Donors (HBD), Topological Polar Surface Area (T_PSA), and dose were identified as the critical predictors [16]. These findings show the potential of ML tools to enhance early FE predictions in preclinical drug development.

Machine learning approaches to predict food effects › Challenges and limitations

ML has the ability to predict FE on drug absorption and bioavailability, and its application in drug development is restricted by a number of challenges [45]. The data quality and data quantity are the top concerns. Nevertheless, ethics, logistics, and financial factors tend to restrict clinical data on FE, and thus, the datasets used should be of high quality and comprehensive to make the ML models successful. Also, the diversity of datasets can be small, which can be a barrier to training models capable of adequately predicting food-drug interactions under varying conditions. Various big datasets involving the drug formulation, demographics, and types of food are required to enhance the performance of the model. Another challenge is the model's generalizability that defines how the model performs well on new and unseen data [101]. In FE prediction, generalizability is challenging due to the intricacy of the factors that regulate drug absorption, such as the type of food, meal mix, digestive state, and individual traits. The models trained on specific drugs or types of food might not extrapolate to other drugs or food types, and so the models must be able to adjust to various drugs, formulations, and patient populations. Practical challenges arise from the need to integrate ML models into the traditional drug development pipeline. Early stages of drug development (e.g., preclinical studies and clinical trials) are not uniform, which can prevent the adoption of ML approaches that are incompatible with existing methodologies [101]. In addition, regulatory bodies such as the FDA and EMA have not yet set forth well-defined rules for validating AI-driven tools to predict FE, which are hindering their rapid adoption. Furthermore, implementation of ML models is a resource and expertise-heavy process, which may be a barrier for smaller pharmaceutical companies or research institutions [101]. Finally, a cost–benefit analysis is needed to decide if investing in ML is worthwhile. Acquiring high-quality datasets, as well as training and validating models, is costly, and developing and deploying ML models is expensive. While the potential benefits (better drug formulation, higher success rates in clinical trials, faster drug approval) are great, pharmaceutical companies must weigh the potential benefits against the upfront costs. The long-term sustainability and effectiveness of ML tools in FE prediction depend on striking the right balance between cost and benefit [48].

Integration, future directions, and conclusions › Integration with other technologies › In silico modeling (e.g., PBPK models)

The coupling of ML with PBPK modeling greatly advanced the prediction of PK properties and improved the efficiency and accuracy of the drug development process [34]. To predict plasma and tissue concentration and key PK parameters such as AUC, Cmax, and half-life (t₁/₂), in human and animal organisms, Kamiya and colleagues combine a simplified PBPK model with an ML-based QSAR. Their simplified PBPK model’s inputs include only three parameters, namely absorption rate constant (kₐ), volume of systemic circulation (V₁), and hepatic intrinsic clearance (Clₕ, int) [66]. Estimation and optimization of these parameters is then performed using an ensemble learning method called Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) [146]. Also, with 1,718 molecular descriptors based on a dataset of 246 diverse chemicals, the predicted values of a PK parameter value (kₐ, V₁, and Cl) and some PK metrics (Cmax and AUC) in rats were in good agreement with observed values, with correlation coefficients of 0.6 to 0.8 [34]. Additionally, Kamiya et al. compiled data on 212 chemicals from the literature and predicted the plasma concentrations in humans after an oral dose. The resulting Cmax and AUC were strongly correlated, with r values of 0.85 and 0.80, respectively. [65]. For instance, Antontsev et al. applied an ML/AI-based PK/PD modeling platform BIOiSIM to estimate the PK outputs such as AUC, Cmax, volume of distribution (Vd), and mean residence time [10]. The BIOiSIM is an AI biosimulation software having a PBPK model with 14 individual compartments for the major organs. Estimation of unknown PK parameters: partition coefficients (Kp), blood-to-plasma ratio, and first-order absorption rate constant (h⁻1) was done in this model through integration of existing in vivo datasets, which were then used in simulating the PBPK model. All the compounds demonstrated R2 values between 0.6 and 0.99 for PK outputs [85]. These studies further add to the notion of the use of ML along with PBPK modeling to predict PK parameters; if backed up with a better PK parameter prediction would lead to an efficient and accurate drug development process. Integration, future directions, and conclusions › Integration with other technologies › Deep learning for pharmacokinetic modeling

Drug development and regulatory processes have seen some recent interest in applying recent advances in deep learning. One such use case is deep learning for PK and pharmacodynamic (PD) modeling as deployed by researchers at the U.S. FDA [29]. For instance, Liu et al. presented a PK/PD model built upon a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, which is a kind of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) that can learn temporal relations. This model was intended to model PK/PD profiles of a hypothetical drug after having been trained on time-sequenced plasma concentration data [81]. The LSTM model was able to effectively predict PD responses for individual patients on certain dosing regimens, but failed to accurately predict responses for patients under different dosing schedules (twice or thrice daily dosing). These challenges demonstrate the problems of RNN-based PK/PD models in real-world clinical settings [27]. In the case of time series, such as PK studies, RNNs can be found, and in particular, LSTM networks. Prediction accuracy is, however, also influenced by the architecture and performance limitations involved with it. The restrictions in terms of sampling rate and frequencies of RNNs, where continuous data in time series is viewed as discrete time sequences, are one of these problems. This brings about high computational requirements and creates memory usage issues. In addition, big inconsistent gaps between the observations may reduce the predictive accuracy of the model since RNNs rely on the past data points to make predictions, and big gaps may be detrimental to such a dependency [25]. Nevertheless, in order to overcome some of these shortcomings, a Neural-Ordinary Differential Equation (Neural-ODE) model has been proposed to tackle some of these shortcomings [25]. Lu et al. introduced the Neural-ODE model to integrate deep learning with differential equations to process time-series data more efficiently. Neural-ODE models are designed to handle irregular time series, unlike RNNs, and happen to be the perfect use case for PK modeling, where data points might not occur regularly [82, 83]. Lu et al. used a Neural-ODE PK model to clinical data of trastuzumab emtansine [18], a conjugated monoclonal antibody used in breast cancer treatment, in their 2021 study. Their model outperformed LSTM and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), as well as commonly used traditional population PK models based on nonlinear mixed effects methods when doing so. Through this, Neural-ODE models were proven to be better at predicting PK parameters, including AUC and Cmax [82, 83]. While the above studies emphasize the general value of ML-PBPK and ML-deep learning integration in pharmacokinetic modeling, their application in the context of FE remains an emerging and promising area. Food intake significantly alters physiological variables such as gastric pH, bile salt secretion, gastric emptying rate, and intestinal transit time, all of which influence oral drug absorption. When integrated with PBPK models, ML can be used to estimate or predict these food-induced changes using existing clinical datasets, enabling improved simulation of fed vs. fasted scenarios. For instance, ML-assisted parameter estimation for bile acid concentrations or splanchnic blood flow can enhance model accuracy in capturing positive food effects, particularly for lipophilic or high-extraction drugs. Similarly, deep learning models like Neural-ODEs can be trained on time-series data from fed and fasted PK profiles to detect temporal shifts in absorption patterns due to food. Thus, integrating ML with mechanistic or neural-network models specifically tailored for food-related physiological modulation has the potential to substantially improve early-stage prediction of food effects in drug development.

Integration, future directions, and conclusions › Future directions

Studies suggest that the pharmaceutical industry is expected to develop 50 new drugs by utilizing ML and AI, resulting in an estimated $50 billion cost saving in the preclinical over the next decades [133]. Many pharmaceutical organizations now work with AI startups after AI investment in drug discovery exceeded $5.2 billion during 2021 [119]. The prediction of FE benefits from advances in ML and its deep learning tools like CNNs and RNNs, which process substantial complex data sets during ADME evaluations. While deep learning models are powerful, they tend to be 'black boxes' because they are not transparent, and it is hard to understand how decisions are being made using them. Such complex models need explainable AI (XAI) that enhances model transparency and aids model refinement to generate predictions, which is necessary for regulatory approval and clinical application [106]. In particular, one difficult problem to help improve food effect prediction models is the lack of reliable and diversified data sources, as most organizations keep their data inaccessible. Such data sharing within collaboration among pharmaceutical companies, academic researchers, and health regulators enhances the use of more diverse and bigger data sets, thereby increasing the accuracy of the model. To improve predictive ability, collaboration is therefore required between teams within several fields, for instance, pharmacologists, data scientists, clinicians, and regulatory professionals. However, regulatory authorities such as the FDA and EMA will have to establish standards to test ML models for these models to be safe and effective, particularly in terms of patient privacy and performance of the models. Although ML models can enhance the speed and accuracy of predicting FE, they need to be well validated for use in the clinic. In addition, ML models needed to be well-tuned on patient data so that there is no bias and it is being fairly treated across different population groups. With ML systems dealing with sensitive medical data, there is a need for privacy and security regulations to be updated to protect patients.

Integration, future directions, and conclusions › Conclusion

Pharmaceutical research has emerged as a very fruitful area where ML can be applied, and more particularly, it can be used to forecast how foods influence drug absorption. The integration of ML models in the drug development process is an encouraging solution because interactions between food and drugs are unpredictable. The ML is much more effective than traditional methods in terms of detecting patterns and forecasting the FE with the use of a large amount of data, which allows the development of personalized drugs. However, several issues are still present, such as the quality of data, the interpretability of models, and the necessity to possess a strong validation. These challenges should be overcome to design and execute ML-based schemes that can be engaged into fully applied in regulatory practice. Since the ML technology is still evolving, when it is combined with other emerging technologies, including high-throughput screening, PBPK modeling, it may enable the technology to enable more predictive capacity to help the process of drug development as a whole. The future of ML in predicting food effects is bright, and the emerging trends include deep learning and explainable AI. Nevertheless, ethical concerns, regulatory factors, and data-sharing factors will play an important role to address and reveal. Finally, this strategy of providing ML in the study and management of food effects can change how patients throughout the world receive safer and more effective therapeutic management.
