Abstract

How artificial intelligence (AI) can be rationally applied to antibacterial drug discovery and development to overcome antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains insufficiently defined, particularly across different resistance mechanisms and translational stages. The global emergence and dissemination of AMR are compromising conventional antibacterial therapies, while slow antibiotic discovery, limited target diversity, and reliance on single-mechanism drug design further increase the risk of inadequate treatment options. With strengths in multi-omics integration, complex pattern recognition, and structure-guided molecular design, AI offers a promising approach to support next-generation antibacterial development. This review focuses on AI-related strategies for antimicrobial development against bacterial resistance and provides a systematic overview of advances in target identification, molecular design, activity prediction, and clinical translation. Rather than proposing a new theoretical framework, the review introduces a pragmatic synthesis tool for evaluating where AI adds value and where important limitations remain, considering application objectives, data properties, validation depth, biological interpretability, and translational feasibility. Major challenges are highlighted, including data heterogeneity, clonal bias, limited external validation, discrepancies between in vitro activity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy, and the absence of regulatory approval for any AI-discovered or AI-designed antibacterial agent. Correspondingly, future directions are delineated, including multimodal data integration, explainable AI methodologies, validation through synthetic biology, and coordinated international regulatory efforts. Overall, this review provides a structured overview of how AI can be integrated with rational antibacterial design while considering both its potential and current limitations for addressing multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was associated with 4.71 million deaths worldwide in 2021, including 1.14 million deaths directly attributable to AMR, and this burden is projected to increase by 2050 [1]. The 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List further highlights clinically important drug-resistant pathogens, including carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa [2]. Despite continued efforts to develop new antibacterial agents, discovery remains challenging because of bacterial biological complexity, restricted chemical diversity, limited exploration of chemical space, and the continuous emergence of resistance mechanisms. Conventional target-based and empirical screening approaches have historically focused on a relatively narrow set of essential bacterial processes, such as cell-wall synthesis, DNA replication, nucleic acid metabolism, protein synthesis, and membrane homeostasis, potentially limiting target diversity and contributing to repeated exploration of related chemical scaffolds and cross-resistance [3]. These approaches also require substantial experimental resources and provide limited ability to predict pharmacological properties and in vivo efficacy. Artificial intelligence (AI) has therefore emerged as an increasingly important strategy for integrating heterogeneous biological, genomic, and chemical information in antibacterial discovery and resistance research. As shown in Fig. 1, a macroscopic roadmap of technological evolution is provided. AI approaches have shown potential in resistance prediction, target identification, molecular design, optimization, and candidate prioritization. By enabling the analysis of high-dimensional and heterogeneous datasets, AI can complement conventional discovery strategies and facilitate the identification of biological and chemical patterns that may be difficult to capture through empirical approaches alone. However, AI predictions primarily reflect patterns present in available datasets and therefore remain highly dependent on data quality, biological representation, and model generalizability. Differences in strain composition, experimental protocols, phenotype annotation, and retrospective data collection can introduce systematic bias, while strain-specific biology, clonal bias, limited external validation, and discrepancies between in vitro activity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy may restrict translational utility. Limited interpretability, insufficient prospective evaluation, and challenges in clinical implementation further emphasize that AI should complement rather than replace experimental and clinical evaluation. Previous reviews have established complementary perspectives on AI in antibacterial research. Melo et al. [4] emphasized AI-enabled screening, molecular representation, drug-likeness, and de novo design; Lluka and Stokes [5] focused on how machine learning (ML) can reinvigorate established antibiotic-discovery strategies and support systems biology, combinations, antimicrobial peptides, and mechanism-of-action studies; and Cesaro et al. [6] adopted a broader infectious-disease perspective spanning diagnosis, surveillance, AMR, and therapeutic discovery [4–6]. Wong et al. [7,8] further demonstrated the value of explainable deep learning and benchmarked structure-enabled workflows for antibiotic discovery. Existing works illustrate the diversity of current approaches in terms of research scope, methodological organization, coverage of resistance mechanisms, and consideration of translational challenges (see Table S1 for a broader comparison with representative recent reviews). The present review uses clinically relevant resistance mechanisms and development stage as a linked organizing axis, separating methodological roles from biological applications and connecting upstream target selection with downstream molecular or regimen design and efficacy prediction. As a narrative review, this article does not aim to provide a systematic quantitative synthesis of the literature; rather, it integrates representative advances, conceptual developments, and translational considerations to offer a comprehensive interpretation of the evolving landscape of AI-driven antibacterial discovery. Rather than proposing a new theoretical framework, it provides a pragmatic assessment tool for evaluating where AI adds value and where important limitations remain. This tool considers application objectives, data properties, validation depth, biological interpretability, and translational feasibility, and distinguishes hypothesis generation from target validation, candidate generation from experimentally confirmed activity, and retrospective prediction from prospective clinical utility. It also incorporates unsuccessful or weakly translating outcomes, including poor cross-dataset generalization, biologically inactive generated candidates, structure-prediction uncertainty, and the persistent in vitro–in vivo–clinical gap. Accordingly, this review integrates resistance mechanism analysis with AI-assisted target discovery, molecular design, comparative evaluation of AI approaches, and antibacterial efficacy prediction within a unified antibacterial discovery perspective. The aim is to clarify where AI can provide practical value across the antibacterial development pipeline while identifying the data, biological, experimental, and translational limitations that currently constrain its broader application. Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Classification of core mechanisms of bacterial resistance

Bacterial resistance develops through dynamic interactions among genetic determinants, antibacterial selective pressures, and bacterial adaptive responses. Beyond well-established mechanisms such as enzymatic inactivation, target modification, efflux, and biofilm-associated tolerance [9,10], resistance can also arise from altered permeability, target protection, metabolic bypass, and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [11,12]. Collectively, these mechanisms can decrease intracellular drug exposure and maintain bacterial survival by preserving target function, bypassing antibiotic inhibition, or promoting the acquisition and spread of resistance determinants. Beyond genetic resistance mechanisms, phenotypic survival strategies also contribute to treatment failure. Tolerance enables transient survival during antibiotic exposure, whereas persistence involves dormant subpopulations that resume growth after antibiotic exposure is discontinued and may contribute to recurrent infections [13,14]. Heteroresistance refers to bacterial populations containing a minority fraction with elevated resistance phenotypes that may escape routine susceptibility testing [15]. These mechanisms frequently coexist within clinical isolates and may limit the effectiveness of antibacterial strategies that rely on a single target [16,17].

Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Methodological classification and roles of AI in antibacterial resistance research

To distinguish AI from the broader field of computational biology, this review uses AI to denote learning or generative models that infer representations, decision rules, or molecular distributions from data. These include classical ML methods such as random forests, XGBoost, and support vector machines; deep-learning architectures such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and graph neural networks (GNNs); and generative or foundation models including variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), diffusion models, reinforcement learning (RL), transformers, and protein-language models. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) are treated as complementary analytical methods, whereas conventional molecular docking, molecular dynamics, and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) simulations are non-AI computational tools. AlphaFold2 is an AI structure predictor, but subsequent docking of its predicted structures is not itself AI-driven. Methodologically, AI contributes mainly to mechanism inference, candidate-space exploration, and resistance or treatment prediction. Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Methodological classification and roles of AI in antibacterial resistance research › Learning-based approaches for resistance mechanism inference

AI-based mechanism inference integrates genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, phenotypic, and clinical data to prioritize resistance-associated features and connect molecular variation with phenotype. Classical models remain effective for structured datasets with predefined variables, whereas deep or network-based approaches can be advantageous for higher-dimensional nonlinear relationships [18,19]. Their main output is a testable mechanistic hypothesis, not proof of causality; feature importance or predictive association must therefore be followed by functional validation.

Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Methodological classification and roles of AI in antibacterial resistance research › Generative and optimization approaches for candidate-space exploration

Conventional antimicrobial peptide (AMP) discovery combines experimental screening, database mining, and virtual screening, but efficient identification of active candidates from large peptide spaces remains challenging [20]. Generative and optimization models explore chemical or peptide space under activity, structural, and developability constraints [21,22]. GNNs and end-to-end representation learning are most likely to outperform descriptor-based quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) or random forests when training sets are sufficiently large and diverse and molecular topology or interaction context carries information not captured by predefined descriptors; for smaller, well-characterized datasets, classical models may be equally or more robust [23,24]. Likewise, generative workflows are most useful when candidate generation is coupled to predictive filtering and experimental validation, as illustrated by integrated discovery pipelines such as NG1 (shown in Fig. 4) [25]. Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Methodological classification and roles of AI in antibacterial resistance research › Predictive models for resistance phenotypes and treatment response

Predictive models differ from mechanism-inference and molecular-generation approaches because their primary output is a probability, class, or quantitative estimate for a defined biological or clinical endpoint. CNNs can extract resistance-related features from matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI-MS) spectra. Genome-based predictors such as GenTB and resistance-gene annotation resources such as hierarchical multi-task deep learning framework for antibiotic resistance gene annotation (HMD-ARG) illustrate complementary endpoints, namely, phenotype-oriented decision support versus mechanistic annotation, while sequence-based and longitudinal models support genomic resistance prediction, surveillance, and evolutionary forecasting [26–29]. Performance should therefore be judged against the intended endpoint and the population in which the model must generalize, rather than by algorithm family alone.

Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Methodological classification and roles of AI in antibacterial resistance research › Emerging AI paradigms: Explainable AI, foundation models, large language models, and agentic AI

Emerging paradigms mainly add enabling capabilities to established workflows. Explainable AI can identify features contributing to predictions, protein-language and foundation models can transfer sequence or molecular representations to data-limited tasks, large language models (LLMs) can support peptide generation or structured knowledge extraction, and agentic systems aim to connect hypothesis generation, prediction, and experimental planning [7,18,19,30]. These emerging paradigms already have concrete applications in AMP design. BroadAMP-GPT uses a generative pretrained transformer (GPT)-based language model to generate broad-spectrum AMPs with activity against multidrug-resistant ESKAPE (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.) pathogens [31], while a latent diffusion model has been applied to expand peptide sequence space and generate diverse, potent AMP candidates [32]. These benefits also introduce distinct limitations, as explanations are not necessarily causal, foundation models may inherit biases from their pretraining data, generative systems may produce plausible yet inactive candidates, and agentic workflows can raise concerns regarding reproducibility, validation, and safety. As summarized in Table 1, no method is optimal across all stages, and method complexity is justified only when it adds measurable value over simpler baselines. These model classes are suited to different stages of antibacterial discovery. GNNs are most useful when molecular topology or interaction relationships are central to prediction, particularly for drug-target recognition or graph-structured molecular data [21,24]. VAEs provide a continuous latent representation that facilitates controllable exploration and optimization of molecular space [33], whereas GANs are more suitable for activity-guided generation and expansion of candidate diversity [34]. RL is particularly useful for multi-objective optimization when antibacterial activity, selectivity, synthetic accessibility, and other properties must be balanced simultaneously [23]. Transformers and protein-language or foundation models are better suited to large-scale sequence representation and transfer to downstream tasks, particularly when task-specific labeled data are limited [31,35]. Thus, model selection should be determined by data structure, development objective, and validation requirements rather than model complexity alone. Resistance Mechanisms and Methodological Roles of AI › Selection of AI strategies across antibacterial development stages

AI strategy selection should follow the maturity of biological evidence and the intended decision. Poorly characterized resistance states favor multi-omics or single-cell approaches for hypothesis generation; established genotype–phenotype relationships support supervised prediction and target prioritization, well-characterized targets can justify structure-informed optimization, and emerging population-level resistance patterns are better addressed by surveillance or forecasting models [36–38]. Thus, AI strategies should be selected according to the maturity of biological evidence and the specific research objective. The integration of AI with emerging biological technologies, including single-cell sequencing and multi-omics analysis, may further expand the identification of previously unrecognized resistance mechanisms. However, their performance remains sensitive to differences in bacterial species, strain backgrounds, experimental procedures, data quality, and biological complexity, which can limit model reliability and generalizability. More importantly, computational success does not necessarily translate into therapeutic success. AI-guided candidates with promising in vitro activity may subsequently show poor PK properties, host toxicity, limited in vivo efficacy, or rapid resistance development [39]. Generative models may also produce chemically novel candidates with favorable computational scores that are difficult to synthesize or prove microbiologically inactive during experimental validation [40]. Similar limitations apply to predictive and structure-based approaches. Genomic resistance models trained on limited strain collections may show reduced performance in geographically or phylogenetically distinct populations, highlighting the importance of dataset diversity and external validation [41]. Although protein structure prediction has expanded access to structural information, predicted structures do not necessarily ensure accurate ligand-binding assessment, and docking-based applications may still require complementary rescoring and experimental confirmation [42]. Overall, current AI approaches are more appropriately regarded as tools for hypothesis generation, candidate prioritization, and iterative optimization within integrated computational and experimental workflows rather than as independently validated platforms that can replace microbiological and pharmacological evaluation [43].

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery

Accurate identification and optimization of antibacterial targets are essential for addressing drug resistance. Compared with conventional experimental approaches, AI can integrate genomic, structural, and pharmacological data to prioritize targets and predict target–compound interactions [44]. As shown in Fig. 5, major applications include protein–drug interaction analysis, protein structure prediction, synthetic protein design, and functional prediction [45,46]. Structural tools such as AlphaFold2 can also provide useful information when experimental structures are unavailable, particularly when combined with complementary computational and experimental validation [8]. Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-guided optimization of traditional drug resistance targets

Well-characterized targets such as PBP2a and β-lactamases provide a relatively mature setting for AI-guided optimization because resistance mechanisms, sequence variation, and structural information are often available [47]. Mutation prioritization and target-binding analysis are best viewed as consecutive steps within the same optimization workflow rather than as separate discovery pathways.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-guided optimization of traditional drug resistance targets › AI-driven characterization of target mutation patterns

Supervised learning methods, including random forest and XGBoost, integrate bacterial genomic or mutation features with antibacterial susceptibility data to identify variants associated with altered drug responses, whereas sequence-based models such as hidden Markov models are better suited for detecting conserved and variable residues within resistance-related protein families [48,49]. These approaches bridge mutation prioritization with structural and functional interpretation. TB-AMRpred represents a phenotype-associated application that identifies known and candidate resistance mutations from paired genomic and susceptibility data in Mycobacterium tuberculosis [50]. In Escherichia coli, the integration of ML with genome-scale metabolic models extends mutation analysis beyond individual variants toward network-level resistance determinants [51], while related predictive strategies may facilitate downstream antibacterial sequence optimization after informative resistance-associated features have been identified [52]. However, the reliability of AI-based mutation analysis depends strongly on the accurate representation of genotype–phenotype relationships across diverse bacterial lineages and resistance backgrounds [53,54]. Therefore, these approaches are currently most valuable for prioritizing experimentally testable variants and generating mechanistic hypotheses rather than independently establishing causal resistance mechanisms.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-guided optimization of traditional drug resistance targets › AI-assisted target-binding analysis and redesign

Resistance-associated mutations can reshape binding-site geometry and weaken target–drug interactions, but the computational roles within this workflow should be separated. Conventional docking and molecular dynamics are structure- and physics-based tools for comparing ligand compatibility across alternative conformations, whereas ML rescoring or learned interaction models constitute the AI component [55,56]. Studies spanning omics-informed target prioritization and mutation-specific virtual screening collectively indicate that these approaches are most useful for narrowing candidates and generating testable structural hypotheses rather than establishing antibacterial activity on their own [57,58]. Deep-learning structure predictors such as AlphaFold2 can expand structural coverage [59], but predicted structures do not guarantee accurate ligand-binding assessment, and AlphaFold2-based docking can remain unreliable even when ML rescoring improves rankings [8]. Thus, the value of an AI-assisted structural workflow depends on the quality of the structural model, compound-space coverage, and experimental confirmation [60,61].

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of novel resistance-associated targets

Novel target discovery is most useful when resistance biology is incompletely characterized and candidate vulnerabilities are not obvious [62]. Multi-omics integration and single-cell analysis provide complementary perspectives, with multi-omics approaches capturing coordinated network-level states and single-cell analysis resolving rare or transient subpopulations that may be overlooked in bulk measurements.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of novel resistance-associated targets › Target discovery based on multi-omics integration

Learning-based multi-omics methods can integrate genomic, transcriptional, metabolic, and phenotypic information, while WGCNA and related correlation-network methods serve as complementary non-AI analyses [63]. Compared with single-gene analysis or conventional genome-wide association approaches, the recurring value across E. coli and cross-species studies is the shift from isolated determinants toward network-level resistance modules and coordinated biological states [64,65]. This broader view can improve target prioritization, but its reliability depends on compatibility among datasets, annotation quality, and subsequent functional validation because network association does not by itself establish causality.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of novel resistance-associated targets › Rare target discovery based on single-cell sequencing

AI-assisted bacterial single-cell analysis enables the detection of rare resistant or drug-tolerant subpopulations that may remain unresolved in bulk sequencing approaches. Bacterial single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is not itself an AI technology but provides high-dimensional data that can be analyzed using ML and other computational approaches to detect rare resistant or drug-tolerant states obscured in bulk measurements. Platforms such as PETRI-seq, microSPLiT, BacDrop, and M3-seq have expanded the resolution and throughput of bacterial single-cell profiling [66,67]. Compared with multi-omics integration, single-cell approaches are better suited to resolving cell-to-cell heterogeneity, transient stress responses, and uncommon subpopulations. BacDrop and M3-seq illustrate complementary strengths, respectively resolving antibiotic-associated heterogeneous states and enabling scalable detection of rare bacterial populations [68,69]. These platforms are experimental data-generation tools rather than AI models, and their value for AI-assisted target discovery lies in supplying single-cell datasets for computational prioritization of unusual states or candidate regulators. Integration with genome-wide CRISPRi screening further links rare-state detection to functional validation, as demonstrated for persistence regulators in E. coli [70]. However, bacterial scRNA-seq remains limited by low mRNA abundance, incomplete transcript capture, and species-dependent differences in cell-wall structure and sample processing, restricting sensitivity and cross-species applicability. Improved MATQ-seq still captures only part of the transcriptome, while broader use of BacDrop requires further optimization of permeabilization and transcript-capture strategies [68,71,72]. Thus, candidate targets from single-cell analyses should be treated as hypotheses requiring independent functional validation.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of resistance-reversal targets for restoring antibacterial susceptibility

Resistance-reversal target discovery seeks to restore susceptibility by interfering with regulatory or accessory processes that maintain resistance, including efflux control, biofilm physiology, and resistance-gene expression [73,74]. Compared with conventional essential-gene targeting, this strategy is attractive when an existing antibiotic can be potentiated, but it requires evidence that the predicted regulator is functionally actionable rather than merely correlated with resistance.

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of resistance-reversal targets for restoring antibacterial susceptibility › AI-based discovery of efflux pump and biofilm disruption targets

Efflux pump regulation and biofilm-associated resistance involve complex regulatory networks that are difficult to resolve by single-variable analyses. AI-assisted integration of transcriptomic, genomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and phenotypic data can prioritize resistance-related regulators and functional sites. In P. aeruginosa, such approaches have identified antibiotic-responsive regulatory modules and functionally relevant MexB residues [75,76]. For biofilm resistance, TargIDe predicts targets from compounds with confirmed antibiofilm activity, while computational screening has identified LasR-targeted inhibitors [20,77,78]. Multimodal profiling can further link biofilm phenotypes with regulatory determinants and susceptibility patterns. These approaches support mechanism-oriented target prioritization, although predicted targets still require genetic or pharmacological validation and evaluation with conventional antibacterials or antibiofilm adjuvants [79].

Application and Evaluation of AI in Antibacterial Drug Target Discovery › AI-driven discovery of resistance-reversal targets for restoring antibacterial susceptibility › AI-guided discovery of resistance-gene silencing targets

Resistance-gene silencing offers a more sequence-specific reversal strategy. TargetRNA3 and sRNAdeep support prediction of bacterial small RNA (sRNA) interactions and regulatory genes, while computational optimization of crRNA–Cas13 interactions extends the concept to programmable transcript suppression [80–82]. The main bottleneck is no longer target prediction alone but delivery and intracellular function, including stability, off-target activity, variable expression level, and inefficient bacterial entry, which can erase the advantage of a highly specific computational design [83]. These resistance-reversal routes also differ in translational tractability. Efflux or quorum-sensing regulators may be accessible to small-molecule inhibition but can have network-level pleiotropic effects, whereas RNA or CRISPR-based silencing offers sequence specificity at the cost of a more difficult delivery problem. Consequently, computational target confidence should not be separated from intervention feasibility. A regulator with strong omics support may still be a poor therapeutic target if intracellular exposure cannot be achieved, while a moderately ranked but chemically tractable accessory target may offer a more realistic route to restore antibiotic susceptibility.

Based on the comparative analysis above, Fig. 6 provides a pragmatic synthesis of AI-assisted target discovery strategies. Its role is primarily upstream, namely, to determine which resistance-associated target or regulatory process is sufficiently supported to justify subsequent intervention development. It organizes current approaches into 3 complementary categories according to the maturity of biological evidence [84,85]. Traditional target optimization is most suitable when resistance-associated targets have been established and sufficient structural or mutational information supports further refinement [86]. AMR-reversal target discovery is particularly valuable when existing antibiotics can be potentiated through interference with regulatory or accessory mechanisms, including efflux activity, biofilm formation, and adaptive resistance pathways [87]. By contrast, novel target discovery is mainly applicable to poorly characterized resistance mechanisms or biological contexts in which previously unrecognized therapeutic vulnerabilities remain to be explored [88]. The practical value of this synthesis tool lies in matching target discovery strategies to the biological maturity of candidate targets rather than favoring any specific computational method. Sequence and phenotypic data support variant prioritization, multi-omics analyses facilitate the discovery of previously unrecognized resistance determinants, and structural or network-based approaches help convert computational associations into experimentally testable targets [89]. Accordingly, data quality, biological plausibility, interpretability, and experimental validation determine whether an AI-prioritized target should advance beyond target selection. Once a target or resistance mechanism is sufficiently supported, the central question shifts from what should be targeted to how that information can be translated into an effective molecule or therapeutic regimen. The Applications and Evaluations of AI in the Design of Antibacterial Drug Molecules

Antibacterial molecular design translates validated resistance mechanisms and targets into optimized molecules or therapeutic strategies. AI-driven approaches can be broadly classified into mutation-tolerant design, multi-mechanism design, and combination-oriented regimen optimization. These strategies aim to maintain activity against target variants, overcome interconnected resistance pathways, or improve interactions between existing agents. AI can reduce the chemical and biological search space and prioritize candidates across multiple objectives, although experimental validation remains essential to confirm potency, selectivity, pharmacological properties, and translational potential. The Applications and Evaluations of AI in the Design of Antibacterial Drug Molecules › Mutation-tolerant molecular design for target structural variants

Resistance mutations can alter target conformation and ligand binding, limiting designs based on a single structure [90]. Mutation-tolerant design therefore emphasizes conformational coverage. Multi-conformation docking evaluates compound compatibility with altered targets, while generative models and RL expand chemical space and optimize multiple design objectives [91–93]. Their effectiveness depends on reliable structural information and experimental characterization of resistance mechanisms.

The Applications and Evaluations of AI in the Design of Antibacterial Drug Molecules › Mutation-tolerant molecular design for target structural variants › Multi-conformational computational design and AI-assisted prioritization

Resistance mutations can alter binding-site geometry and target conformational populations, limiting the reliability of single-structure screening. Conformational-ensemble approaches can better capture these changes, although studies of TEM β-lactamase variants show that population-weighted modeling may outperform conventional ensemble docking, indicating that more conformations do not necessarily improve prediction [94]. A complementary strategy is to prioritize compounds that retain activity across resistant variants, as demonstrated by allosteric and multi-variant dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) inhibitors with activity against trimethoprim-resistant mutants [95,96]. However, mutation tolerance remains compound-dependent, as clinical imipenemase (IMP)-type metallo- β-lactamase variants can differentially affect inhibitor potency [97]. ML-guided screening has further extended variant-aware prioritization to mutant GyrA and ParC, but such predictions still require experimental validation [98].

The Applications and Evaluations of AI in the Design of Antibacterial Drug Molecules › Mutation-tolerant molecular design for target structural variants › Generative and reinforcement-learning optimization of mutation-tolerant molecules

Generative models and RL provide complementary strategies for expanding and optimizing candidate space in mutation-tolerant design. VAE-, GAN-, and diffusion-based approaches can generate novel molecules or peptide sequences beyond conventional screening libraries and prioritize candidates according to activity-related or structural constraints. Although direct applications to mutation-tolerant antibacterial small-molecule design remain limited, studies by Tucs et al. [99] and Lin et al. [100] demonstrated the feasibility of activity-guided GAN generation. More recent AMP-design strategies have extended this framework through different generative paradigms. Wang et al. [32] developed a latent diffusion pipeline coupled with molecular dynamics, generating peptides with lower sequence similarity and greater diversity than those produced by previously reported approaches, including GAN-based models. AMP-Designer further applied an LLM-based foundation model to support property-guided and strain-specific AMP generation, including settings with limited target-specific data [101]. For emerging drug-resistant pathogens, Yu et al. [102] combined meta-learning with RL to enable rapid adaptation from small pathogen-specific datasets. These approaches address different design needs rather than representing a single superior modeling strategy, with diffusion favoring broader sequence exploration, foundation models facilitating transferable and controllable design, and meta-learning providing an advantage when pathogen-specific data are limited. Importantly, computational prioritization does not necessarily translate into experimental activity, as illustrated by recent toxin-derived AMP screening in which only a limited subset of highly ranked candidates showed measurable antibacterial effects [103]. Thus, experimental validation remains essential when translating AI-generated or AI-prioritized peptides into viable antibacterial candidates. Importantly, generative models are more likely to yield experimentally tractable candidates when generation is constrained by synthetic accessibility or reaction feasibility, target- or pocket-specific interactions, and relevant physicochemical or off-target properties [22,104,105]. Without such constraints, they may primarily expand chemical space, generating structures that are difficult to synthesize or fail to show the predicted biological activity during experimental validation. RL is particularly useful when multiple molecular properties must be optimized simultaneously. By incorporating antibacterial activity, binding performance, drug-likeness, synthetic accessibility, or pharmacological properties into reward functions, RL can iteratively prioritize candidates across competing design objectives [106,107]. AMPainter provides a representative example, using deep RL to optimize antimicrobial peptide sequences and substantially improve antibacterial activity [108]. Compared with generative models that emphasize candidate-space exploration, RL places greater emphasis on multi-objective optimization. However, both approaches depend strongly on the quality of training data, scoring models, and optimization criteria. Thus, computational novelty alone should not be considered evidence of a viable antibacterial candidate. Their application to mutation-tolerant antibacterial small molecules remains comparatively underdeveloped, and experimental validation is still required to confirm activity, toxicity, stability, and efficacy against resistant variants.

Complex resistance phenotypes often involve multiple mechanisms, including enzymatic inactivation, efflux, altered permeability, and biofilm-associated tolerance [109]. Molecular design must therefore address interconnected resistance pathways rather than a single target. Current strategies include multi-target docking for structure-guided interaction analysis, multi-task learning for simultaneous efficacy and safety optimization, and fragment assembly for combining functional chemical motifs. Their applicability depends mainly on available mechanistic knowledge, structural information, and chemically tractable design elements. Multi-target computational screening can prioritize molecules engaging complementary bacterial pathways, but predicted binding does not always translate into balanced biological activity. ULD1/ULD2 and ketolide-quinolone hybrids provide relatively strong evidence because their dual mechanisms were experimentally supported [92]. By contrast, only subsets of designed gyrase/DHFR inhibitors retained dual-target activity, while AbaI/AbaR and GyrA/ParC studies remain largely computational [98,110,111]. Because most studies do not report the full proportion of prioritized candidates that remain active after validation, prediction-to-validation success rates are still difficult to estimate. Multi-target design should therefore be judged by balanced target engagement, phenotypic activity, and resistance suppression rather than computational scores alone.

Multi-task learning is an AI modeling strategy suited to antibacterial design when activity across pathogens or mechanisms must be balanced with toxicity and developability [112]. Unlike multi-target docking, which is a non-AI structure-based tool for assessing ligand compatibility with predefined proteins, multi-task models learn shared representations across empirical labels and can support prediction, prioritization, or generation. This flexibility is also reflected in dsAMPGAN, which couples AMP recognition and generation within a dual-task framework and thus provides an alternative multi-task paradigm for AMP design. As summarized in Fig. 7, M3-CAD, APEX, and PolyCLOVER illustrate distinct applications of this principle rather than interchangeable success cases. M3-CAD emphasizes multimodal and mechanism-aware AMP generation, APEX prioritizes peptides at the proteome scale, and PolyCLOVER combines multi-task prediction with active learning to explore sparsely labeled polymer space [113–115]. Importantly, their experimental conversion rates are not equivalent. APEX yielded 41 active peptides among 69 synthesized candidates (59%), whereas M3-CAD reported in vitro antibacterial activity for 20 high-priority peptides but advanced only leading candidates to deeper mechanistic and in vivo validation [114,115]. PolyCLOVER experimentally tested successive candidate batches and ultimately identified 3 lead polymers from a library of approximately 100,000 structures [113]. These figures are not directly comparable because the studies used different preselection thresholds, molecular classes, activity criteria, and validation depths. Thus, AI performance should be judged not only by predictive metrics or successful lead examples but also by the proportion of prioritized candidates that retain multi-property activity after standardized experimental testing. Inconsistent reporting of this conversion currently limits robust cross-study estimates of prediction-to-validation success. Fragment assembly bridges fragment-based optimization and de novo generation by combining predefined functional motifs with optimized linkers to maintain structural compatibility and chemical feasibility [116]. Rule-based methods such as retrosynthetic combinatorial analysis procedure (RECAP) and breaking of retrosynthetically interesting chemical substructures (BRICS) support systematic fragment recombination, while generative models provide greater flexibility for linker and pocket-conditioned design. DeLinker [117], DiffLinker [118], and DiffSBDD [104] exemplify increasingly flexible approaches to linker or structure generation. Compared with unconstrained de novo generation, fragment assembly offers greater interpretability and a more restricted chemical space, but computationally compatible assemblies may still lose target engagement, synergy, or synthetic tractability. Therefore, fragment-based candidates require phenotypic and resistance validation before their predicted advantages can be established.

Combination-oriented design addresses antibacterial resistance by optimizing therapeutic regimens rather than developing a standalone antibacterial agent. AI can contribute at 3 complementary levels by predicting drug interactions, identifying biologically complementary pathways, and optimizing dosing under PK and PD constraints [119]. These capabilities support combination prioritization, mechanistic interpretation, and the design of clinically relevant drug exposure, respectively.

The Applications and Evaluations of AI in the Design of Antibacterial Drug Molecules › Combination-oriented molecular design for optimizing antibacterial regimens › AI-assisted prediction and network-informed design of synergistic combinations

ML combination models integrate drug descriptors, target or omics information, resistance features, and measured interaction data to reduce the experimental screening space [120]. INDIGO and later chemogenomic or transcriptomic approaches show how biological context can improve prediction beyond chemical descriptors alone [121]. Yet, greater model complexity does not guarantee generalization across pathogens, concentration ranges, interaction metrics, or laboratory protocols; checkerboard, time-kill, and resistance-suppression experiments remain necessary before predicted synergy is considered actionable. Network-informed models extend pairwise prediction by connecting drugs with targets, metabolic pathways, and resistance determinants. CALMA combines metabolic modeling with deep learning, while large interaction maps and chemical-genetic networks provide systems-level hypotheses for complementary combinations [122,123]. Such models can improve mechanistic plausibility, but incomplete pathway annotation and context-dependent bacterial physiology limit transfer between strains and infection conditions.

Whereas combination discovery addresses which agents should be paired, dose optimization determines how selected drugs should be administered to maximize bacterial clearance while limiting resistance emergence and toxicity. PK/PD models, bacterial dynamic models, population PK, and optimization algorithms can be integrated to evaluate drug ratios, dosing intervals, and exposure targets [124,125], making this approach especially relevant when effective combinations have been identified but their optimal therapeutic exposure remains uncertain. Smith et al. [126] demonstrated this strategy by integrating hollow-fiber infection data, PK/PD modeling, population PK, and a genetic algorithm to optimize meropenem and polymyxin B therapy against carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii. Subsequent studies have extended this framework to multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens and triple-drug regimens [127,128]. By coupling dynamic infection models with clinically achievable drug exposures, these approaches provide a translational pathway from combination efficacy assessment to optimized regimen design. Nevertheless, their clinical applicability depends on whether PK assumptions and dosing constraints adequately account for interindividual differences in organ function, disease severity, and treatment response variability.

Following upstream target selection, the downstream challenge is to determine how a sufficiently characterized target or resistance mechanism should be translated into an intervention [129]. Accordingly, Fig. 8 functions as a pragmatic synthesis and strategy-selection tool that summarizes 3 major design routes without introducing a separate theoretical model. Mutation-tolerant molecular design is particularly suitable when resistance mutations have been structurally defined and their effects on target interactions can be evaluated; multi-mechanism synergistic molecular design is more applicable to resistance phenotypes involving interconnected targets or pathways; whereas combination-oriented molecular design is preferable when existing antibacterial agents can be strategically integrated to enhance efficacy or limit resistance development. Unlike the upstream target-selection step, evaluation at this downstream stage is intervention-specific. Mutation-informed approaches depend largely on reliable structural and mutational information, multi-mechanism strategies require evidence linking multiple targets or biological activities, and combination or regimen optimization relies more extensively on quantitative drug interaction and PK/PD data. Structural characterization of PBP2a E239R demonstrates how mutation-specific insights can guide compound refinement [130,131], while the complex resistance profiles observed in CRAB and CRE provide a strong rationale for multi-mechanism intervention strategies [132]. Established therapeutic regimens, including polymyxin-based combinations, are more appropriately optimized through dose-exposure modeling approaches [133]. Across all 3 routes, computational success should be reported together with synthesis success, the fraction of candidates retaining predicted selectivity or multi-target activity, phenotypic potency across resistant strains, toxicity, PK/PD behavior, resistance emergence, and in vivo confirmation. Experimental validation varies substantially across representative AI-generated AMP studies. BroadAMP-GPT reported potent activity in 57% of tested candidates, AMPainter identified active AMPs in 6 of 10 de novo candidates, a ProtGPT2-based pipeline found potent activity in 4 of 9 candidates, and APEX yielded 41 active peptides among 69 synthesized candidates, whereas CVAE-BIO reported that 18.5% met the more stringent minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≤10 μg/ml criterion [31,108,115,131,134]. These results are not directly comparable because candidate preselection and definitions of activity differ among studies, but they illustrate that computational prioritization does not uniformly translate into experimental success. Because these denominators are inconsistently reported, current literature probably overrepresents successful examples and does not yet support robust estimates of overall AI design success rates. Conventional antibacterial susceptibility testing often requires 48 to 72 h, highlighting the potential of AI-assisted approaches for accelerating susceptibility prediction [136,137]. Current evidence does not support a universally superior platform; instead, available approaches appear to provide complementary sources of information. For example, GenseqAMR predicted antibacterial susceptibility in Klebsiella pneumoniae using clinical shotgun metagenomic data, achieving area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.926 to 0.956 within 18.34 h [138], whereas matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF)-based and nanomotion-assisted approaches infer susceptibility profiles from phenotypic signatures [139,140]. Genomic approaches provide broader mechanistic insights, whereas phenotypic methods can capture resistance mechanisms that remain unresolved by currently identified genetic determinants. Nevertheless, reported performance remains highly dependent on sample quality, pathogen diversity, and external validation. AI-based rapid prediction is therefore best positioned as a complementary tool that accelerates conventional susceptibility testing rather than replacing established diagnostic workflows.

Clinical outcomes cannot be inferred from antibacterial susceptibility alone because treatment response is also influenced by drug exposure, infection site, host status, comorbidities, and treatment history. Accordingly, clinical outcome models integrate microbiological data with patient and therapeutic variables to support individualized decision-making. Rhodes et al. [141] showed that incorporating patient characteristics improved early risk stratification for methicillin-resistant S. aureus infection in hospitalized patients with community-acquired pneumonia. Subsequent studies using electronic health records, prior cultures, antibacterial exposure, and susceptibility profiles further demonstrate the value of incorporating clinical context rather than relying on isolated predictors [142–144]. However, these models are more sensitive than in vitro predictors to differences in patient populations, prescribing practices, and healthcare settings, making external and prospective validation essential. Their main value therefore lies in bridging microbiological susceptibility with real-world treatment conditions to identify therapies most likely to achieve meaningful clinical outcomes.

Predicting resistance evolution extends AI-based assessment beyond current susceptibility by estimating how efficacy may change under future selective pressure [145]. Existing studies are more clearly understood according to decision scale rather than through individual case descriptions. Treatment-policy models address therapy-associated evolutionary dynamics [146], hospital-level models estimate institutional AMR risk [147], and genomic models examine dissemination or HGT at the population level [148,149]. Comparison across these scales shows that evolutionary prediction can support therapeutic planning, surveillance, and population-risk assessment, although the required data, time horizon, and interpretation differ substantially. Compared with susceptibility testing and clinical outcome prediction, however, evolutionary modeling remains inherently more uncertain because resistance trajectories are shaped by dynamic selective pressures, transmission patterns, mutational events, and horizontal gene exchange. Accordingly, its practical utility is likely to be greatest in identifying emerging resistance risks and informing preventive strategies, whereas accurate long-term prediction remains limited by rare evolutionary events, incomplete surveillance coverage, and regional differences in sampling and epidemiological context.

Applications of AI in Antibacterial Efficacy Prediction › Integrated assessment of AI-based antibacterial efficacy prediction

AI-assisted efficacy prediction can instead be considered a sequential decision framework that extends from rapid susceptibility assessment to patient-specific treatment prediction and, ultimately, resistance risk forecasting [150]. The appropriate computational strategy is therefore determined by the clinical decision context and the type of information available at each stage. In vitro models emphasize rapid phenotypic or genotypic characterization, clinical prediction models incorporate host and treatment-related variables, and evolutionary models rely on longitudinal surveillance data to estimate emerging resistance risks. Clinical-stage programs illustrate that AI can contribute at different points in antibacterial development, from early design decisions to later-stage analysis and optimization [151,152]. For LBP-EC01 and BX004, ML platforms were used mainly to support phage-cocktail selection and optimization rather than to generate phages de novo [153,154]. In comparison, AlphaFold2-based modeling of ibezapolstat contributed primarily to structural and mechanistic interpretation after the candidate had already been established [155]. P-PEAKS UTI and DANCE further extend this spectrum by using computational approaches for phage–host prediction, genomics-guided selection, and personalized matching (Table 2) [156,157]. These examples highlight the diverse roles of AI across the development process and underscore the importance of distinguishing design-defining applications from downstream or auxiliary support. The translational outcomes of these programs also illustrate the current limitations of AI-assisted antibacterial development. Although several candidates have progressed into clinical evaluation, not all programs have advanced successfully. The BX004 phase 2b program, for example, was discontinued following unexpectedly high adverse-event rates and Data Monitoring Committee feedback, whereas the DANCE trial was terminated after reaching its minimum enrollment threshold. These outcomes cannot be attributed directly to AI, but they demonstrate that computationally guided design or selection does not necessarily translate into clinical success. Important bottlenecks also remain across different application settings. Susceptibility models can be affected by assay quality, phenotype definition, and clonal or site-specific bias, whereas clinical-outcome models depend on representative prospective and multicenter data. Reported AUC values, MIC changes, and other performance metrics are still dominated by retrospective, single-center, or in vitro evaluations, with prospective multicenter studies linked directly to clinical outcomes remaining limited. Resistance forecasting requires sufficiently longitudinal surveillance datasets, and molecular or phage optimization models may still inadequately capture safety, PK, host–environment interactions, and manufacturing constraints [158]. As of 2026, no antibacterial agent with both an AI-discovered target and an AI-designed therapeutic structure has received regulatory approval, and prospective multicenter evaluations linked directly to clinical outcomes remain limited. AI-based prediction and optimization should therefore be interpreted within the context of their validation setting and intended biological or clinical application. Despite substantial progress in AI-assisted antibacterial discovery, translation remains limited. Data limitations are better viewed as an evidence-quality problem than simply a shortage of samples. Differences in antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) methods, breakpoint definitions, sequencing workflows, annotation practices, and metadata can generate dataset shift, while over-representation of related strains can cause clonal leakage and inflate internal validation [54,161]. The priority is therefore not only to enlarge datasets but also to standardize metadata, separate related clones across training and test sets, and evaluate models in geographically and temporally independent cohorts. Prospective multicenter studies should additionally assess calibration, failure cases, and clinically meaningful outcomes rather than relying on internal numerical performance alone [161,162].

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Current challenges limiting clinical translation of AI antibacterial discovery › Core technical barriers

Technical barriers can be grouped into 2 main problems, including model interpretability and biological transferability. Deep-learning and generative models may rank or generate candidates without providing transparent mechanistic explanations [163,164], while in vitro-optimized outputs may fail in vivo because metabolism, tissue distribution, immune responses, infection microenvironments, and patient-specific PK are incompletely represented [165,166]. Docking and molecular dynamics can provide complementary structural checks but are neither substitutes for experimental validation nor AI models by themselves [167]. The priority is therefore to connect model outputs with mechanistic testing, PK/PD assessment, and iterative experimental feedback rather than adding more disconnected computational modules [134,165,167].

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Current challenges limiting clinical translation of AI antibacterial discovery › The last-mile obstacles to clinical translation

AI has substantially expanded target identification, virtual screening, molecular generation, and lead optimization, but these advances have not yet translated into demonstrably higher clinical success rates. Only a limited number of computationally discovered or designed antibacterial candidates have reached clinical trials, and no AI-discovered antibacterial drug has received regulatory approval [159,160]. Improvements in computational ranking, MIC prediction, or other surrogate endpoints therefore do not establish clinical benefit; AI-informed candidates or decision-support systems should demonstrate clinically meaningful added value against appropriate standard-of-care comparators. Regulatory expectations are also evolving around model credibility, data traceability, reproducibility, bias mitigation, and interpretability [168–170]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration–European Medicines Agency (FDA-EMA) Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice emphasize human-centered and risk-based evaluation, clear context of use, data governance, performance assessment, lifecycle management, and transparent communication [169]. Computational outputs, including de novo molecules, predictive models, and optimized therapeutic strategies, should therefore show biological plausibility, experimental reproducibility, and clinical relevance. Limited mechanistic transparency may further complicate regulatory confidence [171,172]. In practical implementation, developers should define context of use, maintain traceable training and validation datasets, establish version control, quantify uncertainty, preserve audit records, and reproducibly link predictions to supporting experimental evidence. Clinical translation also depends on population and implementation context. Performance may decline when infection-site biology, patient characteristics, prescribing practices, or resistance epidemiology differ across institutions [161,162]. Prospective multicenter studies should test added value beyond existing workflows, while implementation studies directly assess laboratory turnaround time, electronic health record (EHR) and AST interoperability, antimicrobial-stewardship integration, reimbursement, clinician acceptance, and performance drift [160,173]. These factors should be evaluated directly rather than inferred from retrospective computational performance.

The application of AI in antibacterial discovery and resistance surveillance raises 3 distinct ethical and safety issues. First, models that combine pathogen genomics with molecular design capabilities could be misused to enhance virulence, resistance, or immune evasion, warranting risk-proportionate access controls, audit mechanisms, and biosafety and biosecurity review [174]. Second, genomic surveillance and EHR-linked AMR modeling may expose sensitive patient, community, or institutional information, supporting data minimization, secure governance, and privacy-preserving or federated analytical approaches where appropriate [175,176]. In addition, AI-derived drugs or decision tools may widen disparities if training datasets, clinical trials, manufacturing capacity, affordability, or implementation resources underrepresent low-resource settings [176,177]. Ethical safety should therefore be treated as a cross-cutting criterion from model development through clinical deployment rather than as a post hoc consideration.

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Future breakthrough directions › Building standardized and integrated antibacterial data infrastructures

A standardized and integrated antibacterial data infrastructure is essential for robust and generalizable AI. International organizations, including World Health Organization (WHO), European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and China CDC, could support harmonized protocols integrating molecular, phenotypic, exposure-related, and clinical outcome data. Core requirements include sequencing quality control, AST procedures, infection-site annotation, treatment outcomes, and adverse-event documentation. Standardized AST methods together with WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) could improve cross-laboratory comparability, consistent reporting, and AI-compatible data exchange [180,181]. Global repositories should represent susceptible and resistant strains, resistance determinants, genetic variants, biofilm-associated phenotypes, and treatment outcomes while recognizing high-quality data contributors. Federated learning can enable collaborative model development across institutions without transferring raw patient or institutional data [175,182]. Beyond conventional resistance labels, next-generation datasets should capture intermediate phenotypes, biofilm responses, and temporal changes under antibiotic exposure. Rapid phenotypic AST technologies can provide dynamic growth and response features beyond static endpoints [183], enabling models to move from binary susceptibility classification toward prediction of resistance trajectories and treatment responses.

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Future breakthrough directions › Developing interpretable, biologically integrated, and resistance-aware AI models

Future AI models should combine explainability, biological knowledge, and resistance-aware modeling across antibacterial discovery workflows. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) should be incorporated into molecular generation, activity prediction, and clinical decision support to improve interpretability [163]. Attention-based graph models, feature attribution, molecular fragment analysis, and 3-dimensional (3D) visualization can help identify functionally important chemical groups, binding regions, and mutation-sensitive interactions [164], while interpretable modeling can relate resistance determinants and strain characteristics to AMR predictions [171]. In parallel, models should move beyond general antibacterial activity and incorporate resistance mechanisms as design principles [184]. Mechanism-oriented approaches can integrate target mutations, enzymatic inactivation, efflux, biofilm-associated tolerance, membrane permeability, persistence, host–pathogen interactions, and microbiome effects. Multi-objective optimization should also balance potency, target selectivity, resistance suppression, biofilm penetration, PK, toxicity, and synthetic feasibility, supporting mutation-tolerant, biofilm-penetrating, or resistance-reversing agents against difficult pathogens, including multidrug-resistant A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa [114].

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Future breakthrough directions › Establishing an integrated translational validation and clinical evaluation framework

A standardized translational framework should bridge computational predictions with experimental validation, animal efficacy, and clinical outcomes. Multiscale approaches incorporating infection-site biology, host PK, physiologically based pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PBPK/PD) modeling, organ-on-chip systems, and humanized infection models may improve translational relevance [185,186]. Computational predictions should be iteratively refined using orthogonal experimental evidence, including activity and safety assessments where appropriate [134,167]. Before clinical advancement, candidates should also be independently evaluated across laboratories, diverse strain collections, relevant infection models, resistance-evolution conditions, and clinically realistic dosing regimens. As candidates enter clinical development, AI may support patient stratification, trial optimization, and real-world evidence generation [160,179]. However, prospective and external validation remain essential before such models can reliably guide clinical decisions. Multicenter networks and federated-learning frameworks may further enable distributed data analysis while improving privacy protection and external validation [175,182]. Harmonized AST and surveillance standards [180,181], together with PK/PD and clinical outcome data, can further strengthen cross-site comparability. Regulatory planning should therefore begin early and continue throughout development. Current guidance emphasizes data governance, risk-based performance assessment, model life-cycle management, human oversight, and performance monitoring [168–170]. Clinical implementation should additionally consider cybersecurity, interoperability, and compatibility with healthcare workflows [169,170,179].

Core Challenges and Future Breakthrough Directions of AI-Empowered Antibacterial Drug and Antibiotic Design › Future breakthrough directions › A staged roadmap from discovery to real-world implementation

A practical roadmap for translating AI-enabled antibacterial discovery can be organized into 4 iterative stages. First, data readiness requires representative, standardized datasets with documented provenance, quantified uncertainty, and predefined external test cohorts to limit data leakage and clonal bias [54]. Second, translational validation requires independent evaluation across laboratories, diverse strain panels, resistance-evolution assays, relevant PK/PD and toxicity studies, and appropriate in vivo models [187]. Third, clinical evidence should come from prospective, preferably multicenter studies with predefined endpoints, subgroup analyses, and direct assessment of added value beyond conventional discovery or clinical workflows [188,189]. The final stage addresses implementation and lifecycle governance through regulatory documentation, interoperable deployment, antimicrobial-stewardship integration, economic evaluation, equitable access, model version control, and post-deployment surveillance. Advancement should follow predefined decision criteria, so strong internal metrics alone cannot support clinical translation without independent validation and demonstrated clinical utility.

Conclusion

The global spread of bacterial resistance has become one of the defining public health challenges of our time, with AMR now responsible for a substantial share of deaths worldwide [182]. Traditional antimicrobial discovery is increasingly constrained by target homogeneity, limited chemical innovation, and insufficient consideration of resistance evolution. By harnessing large-scale data and uncovering hidden biological patterns, AI opens new avenues for reshaping antibacterial drug discovery [190]. This review has traced how AI is being applied across antimicrobial and antibiotic design. In target discovery, it can bring together multi-omics, genomic, and phenotypic data to pinpoint regulatory nodes tied to resistance, virulence, or biofilm formation, widening the pool of targets worth pursuing. In molecular design, deep learning and generative models open up new regions of chemical and peptide space, helping surface antibiotics and antimicrobial peptides with genuinely novel structures [191]. Recent studies further demonstrate that explainable deep learning can identify antibacterial structural classes with improved mechanistic insight, while generative AI can accelerate the discovery of antimicrobial peptides against multidrug-resistant bacteria [12,182]. In efficacy prediction and resistance control, AI supports susceptibility prediction, candidate prioritization, early warning of resistance evolution, and the rational design of combination or resistance-reversal strategies. Advances like these can make early-stage screening more efficient and feed useful evidence into precision antimicrobial therapy. Nevertheless, several barriers remain, including heterogeneous data quality, limited model interpretability, insufficient in vivo validation, and incomplete clinical integration. Building on the pragmatic synthesis tools and the staged roadmap for clinical translation outlined in this review, we suggest a gradual shift toward a more resistance-aware, AI-assisted research and development (R&D) model that complements conventional strategies, supported by more standardized data infrastructures, more interpretable AI platforms, and better-coordinated translational and regulatory pathways. If these efforts can be advanced through cross-sector collaboration, such a model may help accelerate the development of new anti-resistance agents and support a more proactive response to AMR, although its real-world value will need to be confirmed through further clinical and translational work.
