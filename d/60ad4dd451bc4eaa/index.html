Abstract

Analgesic drug development poses distinctive challenges, including high placebo response rates, variability in subjective pain assessment, opioid safety and abuse-liability concerns, and difficulty in optimizing dose across heterogeneous pain conditions. Model-Informed Drug Development (MIDD) - a suite of quantitative modeling and simulation methodologies encompassing population pharmacokinetics (PopPK), physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) analysis, exposure-response (E-R) modeling, clinical trial simulation (CTS), and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) - offers tools directly relevant to these challenges and is increasingly integrated across all phases of drug development. This narrative review summarizes MIDD methodologies and their applications across clinical trial phases, the regulatory frameworks governing their use, landmark cross-therapeutic-area and analgesic-specific case studies, and emerging directions, with the aim of clarifying both the progress achieved and the gaps that remain in the application of MIDD to analgesic development. This is a non-systematic, narrative review; relevant literature was identified through PubMed and EMBASE searches (through early 2026) and regulatory agency databases (FDA Drugs@FDA, EMA EPAR repository), supplemented by hand-searching of reference lists, without a prespecified protocol or PRISMA methodology. Modeling and simulation analyses collectively contributed to regulatory decisions in more than 60% of novel drug approvals reviewed by the FDA between 2010 and 2020, spanning PopPK, PBPK, and E-R applications; clinical trial simulation has been associated with higher Phase II-to-Phase III transition rates and greater statistical power for a given sample size; and PBPK modeling has increasingly supported pediatric dose selection, with benchmark evaluations demonstrating clearance predictions within twofold of observed values for most studied drugs. Regulatory institutionalization of MIDD - through the FDA's MIDD (Paired) Meeting Program (established under PDUFA VI in 2018 and continued under PDUFA VII), EMA qualification opinions, ICH guidances, and the FDA's Project Optimus initiative for oncology dose optimization (launched 2021; draft guidance 2023; final guidance 2024) - has reduced regulatory uncertainty and created incentives for MIDD investment. Within analgesic development specifically, MIDD tools have been applied to opioid pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic and respiratory-safety modeling, population pharmacokinetics informing tapentadol and buprenorphine extended-release development, PBPK-supported bioequivalence of topical and generic analgesics, abuse-deterrent formulation assessment, and simulation-based trial designs addressing placebo response in chronic pain; however, no single analgesic has yet achieved the MIDD-centric development characteristic of oncology or rare disease. MIDD represents a substantial and still-maturing shift toward evidence-driven, model-guided drug development, with meaningful but incompletely realized potential to address the specific scientific and regulatory challenges of analgesic drug development.

Introduction

Analgesic drug development occurs against a backdrop of substantial and rising disease burden: an estimated 24.3% of United States adults experienced chronic pain, and 8.5% experienced high-impact chronic pain that limited daily activities in 2023.1 Relative to many other therapeutic areas, analgesic clinical trials face well-recognized methodological challenges, including high and variable placebo response rates, the subjective and multidimensional nature of pain as an endpoint, concerns regarding opioid safety, tolerance, and abuse liability, and persistent difficulty in identifying doses that separate analgesic efficacy from adverse effects. These features motivate the application of quantitative, model-based approaches throughout analgesic development and form a recurring point of reference for the applications discussed in this review. The discovery and development of a new pharmaceutical agent from initial target identification to regulatory approval is a resource-intensive and failure-prone endeavor. Estimates of the capitalized cost of bringing a single new molecular entity (NME) to market are approximately $1 billion or more, a figure that includes the cost of failed programs.2,3 Despite decades of investment in biomarker science, genomics, and combinatorial chemistry, the overall probability of a drug entering Phase I clinical trials ultimately achieving regulatory approval remains approximately 10 to 14%, with late-stage attrition – failures in Phase II and Phase III – accounting for the largest fraction of development cost and time loss.4,5 The leading causes of clinical trial failure are straightforward: insufficient efficacy, unacceptable toxicity, and suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties.6 Surprisingly, a significant proportion of these failures are not attributable to inherent deficiencies in the mechanism of action of the drug itself, but rather to preventable errors in dose selection, inadequate characterization of drug exposure–response relationships, failure to account for inter-individual variability in drug disposition and pharmacodynamics, and insufficient understanding of disease progression dynamics in the target patient population.7 These are precisely the deficiencies that quantitative modeling and simulation – collectively organized under the MIDD framework – are designed to address.8 The term “Model-Informed Drug Development” was formally adopted in the scientific and regulatory lexicon during the mid-2010s to encompass a broad set of quantitative approaches that had previously operated under various nomenclatures including “model-based drug development”, “pharmacometrics”, and “quantitative pharmacology”.9,10 This terminological evolution reflects genuine scientific continuity rather than simple synonymy: Lalonde et al described an early, largely conceptual and case-based framework for “model-based drug development” without the quantitative regulatory-impact data implied by later usage, whereas the term “MIDD” was subsequently formalized and operationalized in FDA regulatory practice, most notably by Wang et al The unifying principle of MIDD is that mathematical models, developed iteratively using experimental data and theoretical frameworks, can serve as quantitative decision-support tools that improve the quality and efficiency of development decisions relative to conventional empirical methods.11 These models encode mechanistic or empirical understanding of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination (ADME); pharmacological receptor kinetics; biomarker and disease dynamics; and patient population heterogeneity. They are used to simulate clinical scenarios, predict outcomes in unstudied populations, optimize trial design, and inform regulatory submissions.12,13 MIDD has evolved from a niche discipline to a mainstream component of pharmaceutical development strategy, driven by advances in computational power, software development, and a growing evidence base. Regulatory agencies have played a pivotal role in accelerating adoption: the FDA’s MIDD (Paired) Meeting Program, established under the sixth Prescription Drug User Fee Act (PDUFA VI, FDA Reauthorization Act of 2017) and initiated in 2018, and continued under PDUFA VII, the EMA’s 2020 guidance on PBPK modeling qualification, and the FDA Oncology Center of Excellence’s Project Optimus initiative for oncology dose optimization (launched in 2021, with draft guidance issued in January 2023 and final guidance issued in August 2024) have each institutionalized specific MIDD methodologies within the regulatory review process.14,15 This narrative review provides an overview and summary of the MIDD methodological landscape, its applications across clinical trial phases, regulatory acceptance and guidance, and emerging advances and applications, with particular attention to analgesic drug development, an area in which MIDD approaches remain, as detailed in later sections, less consolidated than in oncology or rare-disease therapeutics despite facing many of the challenges that MIDD is best suited to address. This is a narrative, non-systematic review rather than a systematic review conducted under a prespecified protocol or PRISMA methodology. Relevant literature was identified through PubMed and EMBASE searches (covering publications through early 2026) using combinations of the terms “model-informed drug development”, “pharmacometrics”, “population pharmacokinetics”, “physiologically based pharmacokinetics”, “exposure-response”, “clinical trial simulation”, “quantitative systems pharmacology”, and “analgesic” or specific analgesic drug and drug-class names, together with hand-searching of reference lists and searches of FDA (Drugs@FDA) and EMA (EPAR) regulatory databases; searches were limited to English-language sources. No formal quality-appraisal instrument or inclusion/exclusion algorithm was applied, consistent with the conventions of a narrative review, and this limitation is revisited in the Challenges and Limitations section below.

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Population Pharmacokinetics

Population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modeling, developed using nonlinear mixed-effects (NLME) statistical frameworks, characterizes the time-course of drug concentrations across a population of patients or subjects, explicitly quantifying both typical (fixed-effect) PK parameters and inter-individual variability (IIV) in those parameters.16 PopPK models are constructed using concentration–time data pooled across subjects, often with sparse sampling designs that would be insufficient for traditional compartmental analysis in individual subjects but are ideally suited to population-level inference. The regulatory applications of PopPK models are extensive: they provide dose–exposure relationship quantification, covariate analyses identifying patient characteristics (eg, sex, body weight, renal function, age, or genetic polymorphisms) that significantly alter PK, and post-hoc Bayesian estimation of individual exposures for exposure-response analyses.17 Software platforms including NONMEM (ICON), Monolix (SOLUTIONSplus), and Phoenix NLME (Certara) have achieved wide adoption in the pharmaceutical industry and are considered industry-standard tools by the FDA and EMA. The regulatory acceptance of PopPK as a primary or supportive analysis in drug labeling is now firmly established; labeling language derived directly from PopPK covariate analyses appears in multiple approved drug product labels across all therapeutic areas.

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Modeling

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models represent a mechanistically deeper approach in which drug disposition is predicted from first principles using the physiology of the human body, such as organ volumes, blood flow rates, tissue composition – combined with drug-specific physicochemical properties (eg, lipophilicity, protein binding, membrane permeability, etc) and biochemical parameters (eg, CYP enzyme kinetics, transporter affinities, etc).18 Unlike empirical compartmental models, PBPK models can be used to predict PK behavior a priori or in scenarios not directly studied, including pediatric populations, organ-disease patients, drug–drug interaction scenarios involving novel combinations, and formulation changes.19 The regulatory acceptance of PBPK as a decision-support tool has grown rapidly over the past decade. FDA’s 2018 guidance on PBPK analyses and EMA’s 2020 qualification opinion provide specific criteria for model qualification and the types of regulatory questions that PBPK can address. PBPK platforms including Simcyp Simulator (Certara), PK-Sim/MoBi (Bayer/Open Systems Pharmacology Consortium), and GastroPlus (SIMULATIONSplus) are widely used by both industry and regulatory scientists. Perhaps the most consequential application of PBPK in regulatory practice has been pediatric extrapolation: by integrating age-dependent physiological parameters with pediatric-specific drug metabolism ontogeny data, PBPK models can predict pediatric PK with sufficient confidence to support initial dose selection in pediatric clinical trials, substantially reducing the burden of pediatric study design and enabling dosing recommendations in neonates and infants for whom dedicated PK studies are logistically and ethically challenging.20

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Pharmacokinetic-Pharmacodynamic Modeling

PK/PD models extend the pharmacokinetic framework to explicitly link drug exposure to a pharmacological effect, biomarker response, or clinical endpoint. The architecture of PK/PD models ranges from simple direct-link concentration-effect models – such as the sigmoid Emax model, a form classically applied in models of opioid-induced respiratory depression, or simpler power models21 – in which effect is a direct function of drug concentration – to mechanistic indirect-response models incorporating production and degradation of biomarker mediators, to complex systems-pharmacology models that represent entire signaling cascades or disease processes.22 The central utility of this type of modeling in drug development lies in its capacity to translate preclinical pharmacological data into quantitative predictions of clinical response, enabling rational human dose selection for Phase II studies, and to define therapeutic windows – the exposure range associated with target pharmacological effect while remaining below thresholds associated with adverse events. In oncology, PK/PD models have been used to characterize the relationship between drug exposure and tumor size dynamics, informing dose selection and enabling simulation of progression-free survival distributions under different dosing regimens.23 In anti-infective development, PK/PD models based on pharmacodynamic indices (time above MIC, AUC/MIC ratio) have guided dose selection and the design of susceptibility breakpoints for antibiotics, representing one of the most mature and regulatory-impactful applications of PK/PD modeling in drug development.24,25

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Exposure-Response Analysis

Exposure-response (E-R) analysis is a broadly-applied MIDD methodology that empirically characterizes the statistical relationship between measures of drug exposure – typically area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), maximum concentration (Cmax), or trough concentration (Ctrough) – and clinical outcomes, including efficacy endpoints and adverse events. E-R analyses are often conducted retrospectively using data from Phase II or Phase III trials, but increasingly they are pre-specified as primary or supportive analyses with regulatory implications.26 The FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) and Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) both issue guidance encouraging E-R analyses as a standard component of NDA and BLA submissions, particularly when dose-response was not formally studied or when post-approval dose optimization is under consideration.

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Clinical Trial Simulation

Clinical trial simulation (CTS) is perhaps the most directly decision-relevant application of MIDD from the perspective of clinical program strategy. CTS integrates all available quantitative knowledge about a drug – its PK/PD, E-R relationships, variability, and disease context – into a simulation capable of generating virtual clinical trials under specified design parameters (sample size, dose levels, dosing regimen, patient population, endpoint definition, statistical analysis plan).27 By executing thousands of simulations across design alternatives, CTS enables sponsors to prospectively evaluate the trial design characteristics – power, probability of success, Type I error rate – of proposed trial designs before committing to them, identifying designs that maximize the probability of a definitive trial outcome given realistic assumptions about the biology and the patient population. The value of CTS extends beyond statistical power calculations to encompass a much richer exploration of design space: optimal sampling schedules for PK sub-studies, patient stratification strategies, adaptive design evaluation, endpoint selection, and go/no-go decision threshold calibration. Model-based drug development programs that incorporate CTS have been described, conceptually and through illustrative case examples, as achieving higher Phase II-to-Phase III transition rates and lower late-stage attrition than programs that do not utilize quantitative simulation, although rigorous prospective quantification of this association – as distinct from case-based illustration – remains limited.9

The MIDD Methodological “Landscape” › Quantitative Systems Pharmacology

Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) represents the most mechanistically ambitious type of MIDD, integrating mechanistic models of disease biology – signaling networks, immune cell dynamics, tumor growth kinetics, organ physiology – with pharmacological drug action models to generate multi-scale predictions of therapeutic response.28,29 QSP models are typically large-scale ordinary differential equation systems calibrated against diverse biological datasets, and they are used to address questions at the intersection of drug mechanism and disease biology that are beyond the scope of more conventional PK/PD models. MIDD Applications Across Clinical Development Phases › Preclinical to First-in-Human Translation

The translation from preclinical studies to first-in-human (FIH) dose selection represents one of the earliest opportunities for MIDD to add value. Conventional allometric scaling – extrapolating animal PK parameters to humans based on body weight – remains widely used, but is recognized as insufficient for drugs subject to significant species differences in metabolic pathways, protein binding, or active transport.39 PBPK modeling, which incorporates species-specific physiological and biochemical parameters, provides a more mechanistically rigorous basis for human PK prediction and has demonstrated superior predictive accuracy for CYP-metabolized drugs and drugs subject to hepatic uptake transporters in multiple benchmarking studies.40 For the selection of the starting dose in FIH studies, regulatory guidance requires that the minimum anticipated biological effect level (MABEL) approach be applied for high-risk biologics, particularly immunomodulatory monoclonal antibodies and engineered receptor-binding agents.41 MABEL calculations explicitly incorporate PK/PD models that link drug exposure to receptor occupancy or downstream biomarker response, enabling a target-engagement-informed starting dose that minimizes the risk of first-dose adverse events while providing an interpretable pharmacological signal. The tragic first-in-human trial of TGN1412 in 2006, which resulted in cytokine storm and multi-organ failure in all six healthy volunteers, has been retrospectively analyzed through the lens of MABEL and PK/PD modeling to illustrate how quantitative approaches could have identified the risk of supraphysiological receptor occupancy at the tested dose.42

MIDD Applications Across Clinical Development Phases › Phase I: Dose Escalation and Pharmacological Characterization

In Phase I dose-escalation studies, particularly in oncology where dose-escalation is conducted in patients with no other therapeutic options, MIDD tools serve dual functions: informing the design of the dose-escalation scheme, and enabling real-time decision support during the trial through model-based dose-escalation algorithms. Model-based designs including the Bayesian optimal interval (BOIN) design and the continual reassessment method (CRM) explicitly use PKPD or dose-toxicity probability models updated with accumulating trial data to recommend dose levels for subsequent cohorts, enabling more efficient identification of the recommended Phase II dose (RP2D) with fewer patients exposed to subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic doses compared to traditional rule-based designs.43–45 Phase I PKPD data are also used to generate initial E-R models that inform Phase II dose selection. The FDA guidance on oncology dose optimization specifically endorses the use of E-R analyses from Phase I to inform dose selection for randomized Phase II trials, representing a shift from the traditional maximum tolerated dose (MTD) paradigm toward a paradigm of optimized dose, defined as the dose that maximizes the benefit–risk ratio rather than merely the dose below the MTD.46 This guidance explicitly identifies MIDD as the analytical framework for implementing this new paradigm, requiring sponsors of oncology NDA/BLAs to include E-R analyses as a standard component of the submission.

MIDD Applications Across Clinical Development Phases › Phase II: Dose-Ranging and Proof of Concept

Phase II is the phase where most attrition occurs and where the consequences of flawed dose selection are most consequential. A drug that reaches Phase II with an inadequate dose range will likely produce an ambiguous efficacy signal – falsely negative trials have been estimated to account for a substantial proportion of Phase II failures that would have succeeded with better dose selection.37,47 MIDD contributes to Phase II success in multiple ways: PopPK models developed from Phase I data are used to predict PK in the broader and often more heterogeneous Phase II population; PK/PD and E-R models provide the quantitative basis for dose-range selection; and CTS enables simulation-based validation of the Phase II design before trial initiation. The concept of “learn and confirm” – a drug development philosophy in which Phase II is explicitly framed as a learning phase designed to generate quantitative understanding sufficient to confirm efficacy in Phase III – is fundamentally a MIDD-based framework.48 In this paradigm, Phase II is not primarily designed for hypothesis testing but for model estimation: generation of sufficiently precise and accurate E-R and PK/PD models to inform a highly powered, optimally designed Phase III. Dose–response data from Phase II are analyzed using E-R modeling to generate confidence intervals around the exposure–efficacy relationship, which are then used as the basis for Phase III dose selection.

MIDD Applications Across Clinical Development Phases › Phase III: Confirmatory Trials and Bridging

In Phase III confirmatory trials, MIDD continues to provide value through several mechanisms. CTS is used to prospectively evaluate the statistical operating characteristics of the confirmatory trial design, including the probability of achieving the primary endpoint across a range of plausible true treatment effect scenarios derived from Phase II E-R models.49 Adaptive trial designs – including sample size re-estimation, dose selection adaptations, and population enrichment adaptations – are enabled and validated by CTS prior to trial initiation. MIDD also supports bridging analyses – the extrapolation of clinical evidence from one population or setting to another. Geographic bridging studies, which evaluate whether PK and E-R relationships established in one population are consistent with those in other populations who may have different CYP2C19 or CYP2D6 phenotype distributions, are increasingly analyzed using PopPK modeling rather than traditional bioequivalence study designs. This approach has been formalized in regulatory guidance from the Japanese Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) and in FDA guidance on the use of foreign clinical data.

MIDD Applications Across Clinical Development Phases › Post-Approval and Lifecycle Management

The utility of MIDD extends beyond initial approval into post-approval drug management. PopPK and E-R models established during development are frequently updated with post-approval pharmacovigilance data, large-scale registry datasets, and real-world evidence to refine dosing recommendations, identify previously uncharacterized sources of variability, and assess whether dose adjustments are warranted for specific patient subgroups. Several major post-approval label changes – including dose adjustments for renal or hepatic impairment, pediatric dosing recommendations, and drug–drug interaction warnings – have been implemented based on MIDD analyses rather than dedicated post-approval clinical trials, representing substantial time and resource savings.50

MIDD in Special Populations › Pediatric Drug Development

The application of MIDD to pediatric drug development is one of its most compelling and significant use cases. Pediatric patients represent a vulnerable population in whom dedicated randomized clinical trials are frequently impractical, enroll slowly, and in many cases ethically challenging – particularly in neonates and infants, in life-threatening conditions where placebo-controlled designs are inappropriate, and in rare pediatric diseases where eligible patient populations are too small to power traditional trials.51 Regulatory initiatives, including the FDA Pediatric Research Equity Act (PREA) and the EMA Paediatric Regulation, have created legal frameworks requiring pediatric investigation plans for most new drug applications, while simultaneously recognizing that traditional adult trial designs cannot simply be replicated in pediatric populations. PBPK modeling has emerged as the primary MIDD tool for pediatric dose prediction.52 By incorporating developmental pharmacology, such as ontogeny of CYP enzymes, renal glomerular filtration rate maturation, age-dependent changes in body composition and plasma protein concentrations, PBPK models can predict pediatric PK across age groups with quantified uncertainty. Benchmark evaluations across cohorts of 11 to 34 drugs have demonstrated that PBPK-predicted pediatric clearance falls within 2-fold of observed clearance in the large majority of cases,53 a degree of predictive accuracy deemed sufficient by regulatory agencies to support the design of pediatric PK studies.54 The EMA now has published specific criteria for PBPK model qualification in the pediatric context, and both the FDA and EMA routinely accept PBPK-based pediatric dose predictions as the basis for initial dose selection in pediatric trials.55

MIDD in Special Populations › Renal and Hepatic Impairment

Traditional dedicated renal and hepatic impairment studies – in which small numbers of patients with varying degrees of organ dysfunction are enrolled in non-randomized PK studies – provide limited statistical power to quantify the degree of PK alteration and are resource-intensive. PopPK analyses that incorporate measures of renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, serum creatinine, etc) or hepatic function (Child-Pugh score, total bilirubin, albumin, etc) as covariates in drug clearance or volume parameters can provide equivalent or superior insight about the magnitude and variability of PK alterations in organ-impaired patients using data from the broader development program, without requiring dedicated impairment studies. The FDA has published guidance specifically addressing the use of PopPK to replace or supplement dedicated renal impairment studies, representing a regulatory pathway for MIDD-based impairment assessment.56

MIDD in Special Populations › Geriatric Populations

Age-related physiological changes, such as declining renal function, reduced hepatic blood flow and CYP enzyme activity, altered body composition with increased fat fraction, and decreased albumin in frail patients, predictably alter the PK of many drugs in elderly patients. PopPK models that incorporate these covariates provide a quantitative basis for assessing whether dose adjustment is warranted in geriatric patients and, if so, the magnitude of adjustment required.57 The regulatory expectation that drug labels include specific geriatric dosing information, formalized in the FDA’s Geriatric Labeling Guidance, has created a strong incentive to conduct geriatric-specific covariate analyses as part of the PopPK analysis in their NDA submissions.58

Regulatory Framework and Guidance for MIDD › FDA MIDD Action Plan and Guidance Documents

Although the manuscript literature and FDA communications are sometimes loosely described as a discrete “2019 MIDD Action Plan”, no single FDA policy document bears that title; rather, the relevant regulatory commitment is the MIDD (Paired) Meeting Pilot Program, established as a PDUFA VI performance goal under the FDA Reauthorization Act of 2017 and initiated in April 2018, and continued as the MIDD Paired Meeting Program under PDUFA VII from fiscal year 2023 onward. The program’s strategic aims, as described by FDA scientists, include expanding the use of quantitative modeling and simulation across FDA review divisions, developing therapeutic area-specific MIDD guidance, enlarging the pharmacometrics review workforce, and establishing public–private partnerships to advance MIDD science and standards.31 Accompanying this Action Plan, FDA has issued a series of MIDD-specific guidance documents covering PopPK, PBPK, E-R analysis, CTS, and DDI evaluation, providing clear expectations for the content, quality, and regulatory implications of MIDD analyses submitted as part of NDA and BLA applications. An analysis of FDA review activities between 2010 and 2020 documented that modeling and simulation analyses contributed to regulatory decisions in over 60% of approved NME applications, with PopPK, PBPK, and E-R analyses each appearing with high frequency across therapeutic areas.10 These analyses supported a broad range of labeling decisions, including dose selection, dose adjustment recommendations for special populations, drug–drug interaction characterization, and pediatric dosing. The analysis also identified a growing trend toward more complex and mechanistically detailed modeling submissions, reflecting both the increasing sophistication of MIDD capabilities and the regulatory desire for model-informed evidence.

Regulatory Framework and Guidance for MIDD › EMA Qualification Opinions and Guidelines

The European Medicines Agency has developed a parallel regulatory infrastructure for MIDD through its qualification opinion process, in which novel methodologies can be formally qualified for specific applications before they are used in regulatory submissions. The EMA has issued qualification opinions for PBPK modeling in DDI prediction, for specific disease progression models in Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease, and for tumor growth models in oncology, each providing formal regulatory endorsement for the use of these methods in specified contexts.15 The EMA’s position on model-based meta-analysis (MBMA) and the guideline on exposure-response analysis provide complementary frameworks for the quantitative review of evidence across studies and programs. The harmonization of MIDD regulatory expectations between FDA and EMA, facilitated through the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) and bilateral agency interactions, has been important for global drug development programs. ICH M12 (drug interaction studies) and ICH E14 (clinical evaluation of the QT interval corrected for heart rate [QTc] prolongation) both incorporate MIDD methods, particularly PBPK for DDI and exposure-QTc modeling, as acceptable or preferred approaches to address specific regulatory questions.32

Regulatory Framework and Guidance for MIDD › Project Optimus and Oncology Dose Optimization

The FDA Oncology Center of Excellence’s Project Optimus initiative – launched in 2021, with draft dose-optimization guidance issued in January 2023 and final guidance finalized in August 2024 – represents a particularly informative MIDD policy development.46 Historically, oncology drug development has been dominated by the maximum tolerated dose paradigm, in which Phase I dose escalation is conducted to identify the highest dose compatible with acceptable acute toxicity, and this dose is then carried forward into Phase II and III without systematic evaluation of whether lower doses might provide equivalent efficacy with superior tolerability – a question of particular clinical relevance for chronic oral oncology agents. Project Optimus explicitly differs from this paradigm, requiring sponsors of new oncology drugs to conduct randomized dose optimization trials comparing their proposed dose to at least one lower dose, and mandating E-R analyses as the analytical framework for interpreting these comparisons. This guidance elevates MIDD from a supportive to a required analytical tool in oncology development, with direct implications for trial design and approval strategy. It also reflects a broader conceptual shift in regulatory approach toward patient-centered drug development, in which the goal is not merely to demonstrate that a drug works at some dose, but to identify the dose that maximizes the patient benefit–risk balance – a goal that is fundamentally dependent on the quantitative characterization of exposure–response relationships that MIDD provides.59

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development

The empirical evidence for MIDD’s impact on clinical development outcomes is growing, although rigorous comparative analyses are methodologically challenging due to the difficulty of constructing counterfactual scenarios in which identical programs were conducted without MIDD. One analysis of pharmaceutical industry programs reported that model-based development was associated with a statistically significant increase in Phase II-to-Phase III transition rates.9 Another analysis demonstrated through simulation that CTS-informed trial designs achieved substantially higher power for the same sample size compared to designs derived without quantitative simulation.27 And a retrospective analysis of FDA approval actions found that programs utilizing MIDD throughout development had shorter total development timelines on average, though the authors cautioned that this association may partly reflect the higher resourcing of MIDD-intensive programs.10 The evidence for MIDD’s positive impact on clinical drug development has accumulated substantially over the past two decades, particularly in the domains of dose selection, trial design efficiency, and regulatory decision-making. At the regulatory level, the FDA’s MIDD Paired Meeting Pilot Program received multiple meeting requests across nearly all major therapeutic areas, including oncology, neurology, cardiovascular disease, and infectious diseases, with dose selection representing the most frequently addressed topic.11 Industry participants in that program reported three categories of concrete benefit: improvements in development efficiency (including trial streamlining and cost savings), alignment with the FDA on development strategy, and enhanced internal clarity regarding MIDD-appropriate approaches. More broadly, model-based strategies including population pharmacokinetics, exposure-response analysis, and PBPK modeling have collectively supported regulatory decisions and enabled label claims in lieu of dedicated clinical studies across more than 120 FDA-approved novel drugs, demonstrating that MIDD is no longer a supplementary tool, but a substantive generator of regulatory evidence in its own right.11,60 Beyond regulatory interactions, the quantitative impact of MIDD is measurable across the development lifecycle through its capacity to reduce late-stage attrition, which historically has been hindered by inadequate dose selection, unexpected drug–drug interactions, and failure to characterize pharmacokinetic variability in key subpopulations. PBPK modeling has been particularly impactful in replacing or informing dedicated clinical studies for drug–drug interactions and special populations, with the FDA’s regulatory science surveys documenting consistent year-over-year growth in the proportion of NDA/BLA submissions incorporating PBPK models, reaching approximately 26–33% of approved new drugs between 2018 and 2023.33,61 Clinical trial simulation, a key MIDD tool, enables sponsors to identify trial designs that are statistically powered to detect meaningful treatment effects, adjust dose-escalation schemes adaptively using Bayesian integration of real-time PK/PD data, and rationally extrapolate efficacy across age groups or disease states where direct testing poses ethical or practical constraints.62 Taken together, the evidence supports a view of MIDD not merely as an efficiency gain, but as a mechanism for reducing the number of patients exposed to suboptimal doses during development, improving the scientific rigor of dose-labeling decisions, and compressing timelines in areas of high unmet medical need – a convergence of scientific, economic, and ethical benefits that distinguishes MIDD from earlier modeling-only approaches.11,60 Selected Landmark MIDD Applications with Regulatory or Clinical Impact

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Selected Case Studies › Imatinib (Gleevec®, etc) – PopPK-Driven Dosing in CML

Imatinib mesylate (Gleevec®, Novartis), the landmark BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor for chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), represents an early and historically significant example of PopPK and E-R analysis informing clinical development. Population PK analyses of imatinib demonstrated that trough plasma concentrations (Ctrough) were significantly associated with molecular response rates in CML patients, establishing an exposure–response relationship that could be used to guide dose optimization in patients achieving suboptimal responses.36 This E-R analysis contributed to the recommendation that Ctrough monitoring be considered in patients with inadequate molecular response, and it informed dose adjustment strategies that have been incorporated into clinical practice guidelines – one of the earliest examples of therapeutic drug monitoring guided by formal PK/PD modeling in oncology.63

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Selected Case Studies › Sildenafil (Viagra®, etc) – PBPK for Drug Interaction Labeling

The use of PBPK modeling to characterize the drug–drug interaction profile of sildenafil citrate (Viagra®, Pfizer) provides a regulatory case study in PBPK-based DDI assessment. Sildenafil is metabolized primarily by CYP3A4, and its co‑administration with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors results in substantial increases in sildenafil exposure that are clinically meaningful given its narrow therapeutic window in pulmonary arterial hypertension. PBPK simulations of CYP3A4-mediated DDIs, validated against observed clinical DDI data with ketoconazole and other inhibitors, enabled the prediction of DDI magnitude for inhibitors not directly studied in clinical DDI trials, supporting comprehensive DDI labeling without requiring individual clinical studies for each potential inhibitor – a regulatory application that has since become standard practice for metabolically susceptible compounds.64

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Selected Case Studies › Atezolizumab (Tecentriq®, etc) – PK/PD and Exposure-Response Modeling in Immuno-Oncology

Atezolizumab (Roche/Genentech), an anti-PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitor approved across multiple tumor types, illustrates the emerging role of empirical PK/PD and E-R analysis – rather than mechanistic QSP modeling – in immuno-oncology development; the tumor-size dynamics model described below is a PK/PD-E-R application and is presented here as such, distinct from the multi-scale mechanistic QSP models described earlier. Tumor-size dynamics models incorporating drug exposure and baseline tumor burden were used to characterize the atezolizumab exposure–response relationship and to evaluate the flat-dosing strategy (a fixed dose) instead of weight-based dosing. E-R analyses demonstrated that fixed dosing provided comparable clinical outcomes to weight-based dosing across the body‑weight distribution of the clinical trial population, supporting the labeling of a flat dose and eliminating the need for dose adjustments based on body weight – a labeling decision with direct practical implications for drug preparation and administration in clinical practice.34

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Selected Case Studies › Virtual-Patient QSP Platforms in Immuno-Oncology

As a genuine example of the mechanistic QSP approach defined earlier, contemporary immuno-oncology QSP platforms integrate mechanistic representations of tumor-immune cell trafficking, antigen presentation, and cytokine signaling to generate populations of simulated (“virtual”) patients used to explore combination-therapy sequencing, biomarker-based patient selection, and dose-schedule optimization before large clinical investments are committed. These platforms remain earlier in their regulatory maturation than PopPK, PBPK, or E-R methods, but industry surveys document growing adoption for internal decision-making in oncology and immunology drug development.

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Analgesics

MIDD as a toolkit (population pharmacokinetics, exposure-response modeling, PBPK, clinical trial simulation) has been applied in the development of several analgesic drugs, but the MIDD literature rarely singles out a specific analgesic as an MIDD success story the way it does, for example, for oncology drugs or biologics. Instead, the applications tend to be embedded within the broader clinical pharmacology sections of NDA submissions. But there are some examples in the published literature.11

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Opioid Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Respiratory Safety

Opioids remain the analgesic class in which MIDD applications are most developed, reflecting both their continued clinical importance and the regulatory imperative to characterize a narrow benefit-risk margin dominated by respiratory depression. Mechanism-based PK/PD models linking opioid plasma or biophase concentration to ventilatory drive have been used to quantify the onset, offset, and pharmacological reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression, to characterize interactions between opioids and co-administered sedatives or reversal agents, and to evaluate tolerance to respiratory effects with chronic high-dose exposure. This body of work, extending the general PK/PD modeling principles described above to the specific case of ventilatory control, represents one of the more methodologically mature examples of MIDD applied to an analgesic safety endpoint rather than an efficacy endpoint.20,35

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Opioid Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Respiratory Safety › Tapentadol (Nucynta)

Tapentadol is the analgesic most frequently cited in the MIDD/pharmacometrics literature as an example of model-informed development.65–67 Population PK and exposure-response modeling were used throughout its development to characterize its dual mechanism (MOR agonism plus NRI) and to inform dose selection across its acute and chronic pain indications. The Certara MIDD literature cites tapentadol among the drugs for which model-based strategies enabled label claims. However, none of the references use the specific term “MIDD”, because tapentadol was developed before that term was codified.

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Opioid Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics, and Respiratory Safety › Buprenorphine Extended-Release (Sublocade)

The once-monthly subcutaneous formulation of buprenorphine approved in 2017 is explicitly cited in a scoping review of MIDD case studies as an example of MIDD applied to a long-acting injectable formulation – although its primary indication is opioid use disorder rather than pure analgesia.68

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Topical and Locally Acting Analgesics › Diclofenac Sodium Topical Gel 1%

This formulation of an NSAID analgesic is cited in the FDA’s MIDD review literature as an example where PBPK modeling was used to support bioequivalence determination and generic approval.69

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Topical and Locally Acting Analgesics › Local Anesthetics and PBPK-Based Bioequivalence

The PBPK-based bioequivalence approach validated for diclofenac topical gel has informed regulatory thinking about other locally acting, low-systemic-exposure analgesic dosage forms, including topical local anesthetic formulations, for which conventional systemic pharmacokinetic bioequivalence criteria are poorly suited because the therapeutic effect is local rather than driven by systemic exposure; PBPK modeling of cutaneous and subcutaneous drug penetration provides a mechanistic alternative for demonstrating bioequivalence in this class.

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Abuse-Deterrent Opioid Formulations › Opioid Abuse-Deterrent Formulations

The FDA’s MIDD lifecycle review points this out as an application where PBPK modeling of partial systemic exposure was used to support abuse-deterrence label claims, enabling the FDA to grant specific labeling language without requiring additional full clinical abuse-potential studies for each formulation.11,70

Evidence of Impact: MIDD Outcomes in Clinical Development › Placebo Response and Clinical Trial Simulation in Chronic Pain

A further analgesic-specific MIDD application, distinct from PK-driven dosing, addresses trial design rather than dose selection. Chronic pain trials are characterized by high and variable placebo response, which has been a major contributor to failed or equivocal confirmatory trials despite evidence of drug activity from other sources. Enriched-enrollment randomized-withdrawal (EERW) designs, in which only patients who demonstrate tolerability and a treatment response during an open-label lead-in are randomized to continued treatment or placebo, have been developed specifically to mitigate this challenge, and clinical trial simulation has been used to evaluate the operating characteristics – statistical power, placebo-response attenuation, and generalizability – of EERW and conventional parallel-group designs under a range of assumptions about the underlying pain condition. These simulation-based approaches to trial design represent an analgesic-specific extension of the general clinical trial simulation methodology described earlier in this review, applied to a design problem – placebo response – that is more acute in chronic pain than in most other therapeutic areas.25,38 The evidence is less direct for the classical MIDD development story – ie, a new analgesic molecule that was conceived, dose-selected, and brought to approval primarily through MIDD without conventional Phase II dose-ranging trials. That kind of transformative MIDD application has been more common for oncology and rare diseases, where trial‑size constraints and ethical pressures create stronger incentives to bolster clinical studies with model predictions. In pain trials, large patient populations and historically well-established trial designs have meant that MIDD has more often been used for the, still important, purposes of supporting and streamlining the development of analgesics rather than for replacing clinical studies. Emerging Trends in MIDD › Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence Integration

The integration of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methodologies with traditional MIDD methods represents a substantial and rapidly growing addition. Conventional NLME-based PopPK modeling, while rigorous and regulatory-accepted, is labor-intensive in model building and covariate screening, particularly for high-dimensional pharmacogenomic or multiomics datasets. But ML algorithms – including random forests, gradient boosting, neural networks, and Gaussian process regression – have demonstrated capacity to identify complex, non-linear covariate relationships in PK datasets that conventional stepwise covariate modeling may miss, and to handle high-dimensional predictor spaces that are computationally intractable for classical NLME approaches.71 Neural ordinary differential equations (neural ODEs) represent a particularly promising area of methodological development, enabling the learning of differential equation structures from data without pre-specification of a mechanistic model, with potential applications in PK/PD model building for complex multi-compartment systems or receptor-mediated drug disposition. Bayesian deep learning approaches offer a framework for uncertainty quantification in ML-based PK/PD predictions, addressing a core regulatory concern about the interpretability and confidence‑interval estimation of black-box ML models. However, significant challenges remain in demonstrating the regulatory acceptability of ML-based MIDD methods: issues of interpretability, reproducibility, out-of-distribution generalization, and the absence of established validation frameworks for ML models in the regulatory submission context are active areas of methodological research and FDA-industry dialog. Current consensus favors a complementary rather than a substitutive role: ML methods are best suited to hypothesis generation, high-dimensional covariate discovery, and pattern detection, while NLME-based PopPK and mechanistic PK/PD models remain the primary, regulatory-accepted basis for dose selection and labeling decisions.72

Emerging Trends in MIDD › Real-World Evidence and Data Integration

The increasing availability of real-world data (RWD) from electronic health records (EHRs), insurance claims databases, patient registries, and wearable devices creates opportunities to extend MIDD beyond the controlled clinical trial environment into broader patient populations and longer follow-up windows.73 PopPK and E-R analyses using real-world drug concentration and outcome data can provide evidence about the generalizability of clinical trial findings to routine clinical practice populations, which frequently differ substantially from clinical trial populations in age distribution, comorbidity burden, concomitant medication use, and adherence patterns.74 Real-world PK data, collected opportunistically during therapeutic drug monitoring in clinical practice, have been used to update and refine PopPK models for several therapeutic drug monitoring targets, including immunosuppressants and antiretrovirals.75 The FDA’s Real-World Evidence Program, established under the 21st Century Cures Act, provides a regulatory framework for evaluating real-world evidence in drug approval and labeling decisions, including post-approval label updates.76 MIDD analyses using RWD are increasingly submitted as supportive evidence in supplemental New Drug Application (sNDA) and supplemental Biologics License Application (sBLA) submissions for label expansions to new populations or indications, with the population PK model serving as the analytical bridge between the controlled trial data and the real-world dataset.

Emerging Trends in MIDD › Precision Medicine and Pharmacogenomics

The convergence of MIDD with pharmacogenomics and precision medicine represents a promising direction for individualized dosing and treatment optimization. Pharmacogenomic variants in drug-metabolizing enzymes (eg, CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, UGT1A1, DPYD) and drug transporters (eg, SLCO1B1, ABCB1) are established determinants of inter-individual PK variability for numerous drugs and are beginning to appear as covariates in PopPK models with regulatory labeling implications. The integration of polygenic risk scores – composite genetic predictors that aggregate the effects of many common variants – into PopPK covariate analysis represents an evolving area that may improve predictive accuracy beyond what single-gene pharmacogenomic biomarkers can achieve.77 MIDD also provides the analytical framework for model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) – the clinical application of Bayesian forecasting using individual patient PK data (measured drug concentrations) to generate personalized dose recommendations at the bedside.78 MIPD has demonstrated clinical outcomes benefits relative to conventional therapeutic drug monitoring in several contexts, including vancomycin dosing in critically ill patients, tacrolimus dosing in solid organ transplant recipients, and PK-guided busulfan conditioning in hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.79 Increasingly, software platforms implementing Bayesian MIPD algorithms are being integrated into clinical decision support systems in hospital pharmacies.

Emerging Trends in MIDD › Digital Biomarkers and Wearable Technology

The proliferation of wearable medical devices capable of continuous physiological monitoring – including accelerometers, photoplethysmography-based heart rate monitors, continuous glucose monitors, and ambulatory ECG devices – creates new data streams with potential relevance to PK/PD modeling and clinical trial endpoint measurement.80 Digital biomarkers derived from wearable devices may serve as more sensitive and “real-world” valid measures of drug effect than conventional clinic-based assessments, enabling richer PK/PD characterization with lower patient burden. The integration of wearable-derived digital biomarkers into MIDD frameworks is an emerging area with implications for clinical trial design, particularly in CNS disorders, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic conditions where physiological dynamics are relevant to drug effect.

Challenges, Limitations, and Critical Perspectives

Despite the compelling evidence for MIDD’s value, several significant challenges and limitations should be acknowledged to provide a balanced assessment of the current state of the field. First, the quality and regulatory impact of MIDD analyses depend on the quality and quantity of the underlying data. Models built on sparse, heterogeneous, or unavoidably biased datasets – a common challenge in rare diseases, pediatric oncology, and early-phase programs with small sample sizes – are subject to substantial parameter uncertainty that may limit their predictive reliability and regulatory utility.81 The regulatory requirement for model qualification and external validation, when applicable, imposes resource demands that may be disproportionate for smaller development programs.13 Second, the MIDD field faces a persistent challenge of model misspecification – the risk that the chosen model structure does not adequately represent the underlying biology, leading to biased parameter estimates and misleading predictions.82 While rigorous diagnostic and validation procedures exist to identify model misspecification, these require specialized expertise that is not uniformly available across the pharmaceutical industry, particularly in smaller biotechnology companies and academic drug development programs. Third, the regulatory acceptance of MIDD analyses varies across therapeutic areas, regulatory agencies, and individual reviewers, introducing uncertainty for sponsors considering MIDD as a substitute for conventional clinical studies. While FDA and EMA guidance documents have substantially improved the clarity of regulatory expectations, the practical outcome of specific MIDD submissions remains difficult to predict with certainty, and several high-profile cases in which MIDD predictions failed to anticipate actual clinical outcomes – including several PBPK-based DDI predictions that underestimated interaction magnitude – have tempered enthusiasm for blanket reliance on modeling.83 Fourth, issues of diversity and equity in MIDD models deserve attention. Historically, the patient populations contributing data to development-stage PopPK and E-R models have been predominantly white, male, and from high-income countries, limiting the generalizability of covariate relationships and model-based dosing recommendations to more diverse populations.84 The FDA’s commitment to clinical trial diversity, reflected in recent guidance on enrollment diversity and in FDA’s Drug Trials Snapshots program, creates an imperative for MIDD practitioners to evaluate and document the performance of their models across racial, ethnic, and geographic subgroups.85 Fifth, and specific to analgesic development, several practical barriers constrain wider clinical translation of MIDD beyond the scientific and data-quality limitations already discussed above. The cost of building and validating mechanistic PBPK or QSP models, and the specialized pharmacometric expertise required to interpret and defend them to regulators, can be prohibitive for the smaller sponsors and academic groups that develop many analgesics and generic or repurposed products. Regulatory review of MIDD submissions in pain indications is not yet supported by therapeutic-area-specific FDA guidance comparable to that available for oncology dose optimization, so applicants face greater uncertainty about the level of model validation reviewers will expect, and outcomes can vary across reviewers and review divisions. Finally, because no exemplar MIDD-centric analgesic approval yet exists to serve as a template, sponsors have fewer precedents to draw on when proposing MIDD-based approaches for pain indications than their counterparts in oncology. Addressing these barriers will require several steps, which include: development of FDA and EMA guidance specific to MIDD applications in analgesic and pain-related indications; pre-competitive, cross-sponsor data-sharing initiatives, analogous to existing oncology and QT-interval consortia, to build shared opioid and non-opioid analgesic PK/PD and placebo-response databases large enough to support robust covariate and QSP model-building; expanded pharmacometric training capacity focused specifically on pain therapeutics; and prospective, sponsor-led case reports documenting instances in which MIDD analyses altered dose selection or trial design decisions in analgesic programs, so that a body of analgesic-specific precedent can accumulate in a manner comparable to that which has developed in oncology over the past two decades.

Conclusions

Model-Informed Drug Development has progressed from a promising methodological framework into an established and increasingly indispensable component of contemporary pharmaceutical development strategy. The evidence base accumulated over the past two decades compellingly supports MIDD’s capacity to improve the efficiency and success rate of clinical drug development through rational dose selection, optimized trial design, special population extrapolation, and regulatory label support – contributions that translate into tangible benefits for patients in the form of faster access to optimally dosed therapies. The regulatory institutionalization of MIDD, through the FDA’s MIDD (Paired) Meeting Program, EMA qualification opinions, ICH guidances, and Project Optimus, has reduced the uncertainty associated with using MIDD in regulatory submissions, and has created clear incentives for pharmaceutical sponsors to invest in MIDD capabilities. The growing body of published analyses documenting MIDD’s contribution to specific drug approvals and labeling decisions provides an expanding foundation of precedents that further reduces regulatory risk and enables sponsors to refer to concrete examples when making the case for MIDD-based approaches in regulatory submissions.31 Regarding analgesics specifically, MIDD tools have been routinely applied throughout development phases – most clearly for opioid pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic and respiratory-safety modeling, eg, population pharmacokinetics informing tapentadol and buprenorphine extended-release development, PBPK-supported bioequivalence, eg, for topical NSAID and local-anesthetic generics, abuse-deterrent opioid formulation assessment, and simulation-based trial designs addressing placebo response in chronic pain – yet no single analgesic has achieved the kind of MIDD-centric approval narrative that characterizes recent oncology checkpoint inhibitors or rare pediatric disease drugs. This gap reflects the barriers to translation discussed above – cost, specialized expertise, the absence of pain-specific regulatory guidance, and the absence of an established precedent – rather than any inherent unsuitability of analgesic development to model-based approaches; on the contrary, the challenges that most define analgesic drug development, including high placebo response, opioid safety, and the difficulty of separating efficacy from adverse effects at the individual-patient level, are precisely the kinds of problems that MIDD methodologies are designed to address. Narrowing this gap will depend more on the regulatory, data-sharing, and workforce steps outlined above than on further methodological innovation alone. Looking forward, the integration of machine learning, real-world data, digital biomarkers, and pharmacogenomics into MIDD frameworks promises to further extend the reach and precision of quantitative drug development. Equally important is the expansion of MIDD into historically underserved areas – rare diseases, neglected tropical diseases, low-income country settings – where the efficiency gains from MIDD may be most consequential and where dedicated regulatory guidance and capacity-building efforts are needed. Ultimately, the realization of MIDD’s full potential depends on cross-disciplinary collaboration among clinical pharmacologists, pharmacometricians, biostatisticians, clinicians, and regulatory agencies – a collaboration that requires not only technical expertise, but a shared commitment to the principle that quantitative understanding of drug behavior is a prerequisite for the safe and effective use of medicines. The evidence reviewed here supports the conclusion that this aspiration is well-placed: MIDD is not merely a methodological refinement, but a fundamental shift in the pathway to drug development, one with the potential to substantially reduce the human and economic costs of pharmaceutical attrition in clinical trials, while also accelerating the delivery of novel therapies to the patients who need them most.
