Abstract

This review examines the application of machine learning (ML) in physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling through improved prediction of input parameters, particularly via quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models, for absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties across drug development phases. Traditional PBPK models, while mechanistically sound, face limitations in applicability domain when compound-specific physicochemical and biochemical parameters (e.g., partition coefficients, metabolic clearance rates, plasma protein binding) cannot be reliably determined or estimated for novel chemicals. ML-enhanced QSAR models address these input parameter limitations by predicting required parameters directly from chemical structure, thereby extending PBPK applicability to diverse chemical space without altering the mechanistic model framework itself. Recent advances in ML-powered QSAR methodology generate higher-quality predictions of partition coefficients, clearance rates, and binding parameters. When these ML-predicted parameters are integrated as inputs into conventional PBPK frameworks, the resulting simulations often demonstrate significantly improved predictive accuracy compared to PBPK models using conventional regression-based QSAR inputs. This workflow-wherein ML enhances QSAR prediction quality, which subsequently improves PBPK simulation reliability through better input parameters-facilitates critical drug discovery and development decisions, from early candidate selection to clinical translation and safety assessment, potentially reducing pharmacokinetic-related drug candidate failures. However, challenges remain, including limited chemical diversity in QSAR training data sets, model interpretability concerns, and evolving regulatory considerations for ML-derived parameters in PBPK submissions. Future developments point toward seamless integration of ML-enhanced QSAR predictions as validated inputs for mechanistic PBPK models through standardized validation approaches and collaboration among academia, industry, and regulatory bodies.

Introduction

The absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties of drug candidates can determine their success or failure in development. The last 30 years have witnessed a transition from laboratory‐intensive methods to sophisticated computational approaches for predicting these properties—driven by technological advances and the escalating complexity and costs of pharmaceutical development. Early ADME prediction relied on laboratory experiments complemented by mathematical models. Hansch's pioneering quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) work in the 1960s and 1970s provided computational tools to complement experimental approaches, reducing the number of required experiments rather than replacing laboratory testing [1]. However, the widespread adoption in pharmaceutical ADME practice took decades, as the discipline was not an early adopter of the technology [1]. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, while conceptually developed during this period, faced limited acceptance in pharmaceutical applications due to its resource‐intensive nature [1]. Broader implementation of PBPK modeling in drug development occurred in the early 2000s, facilitated by advances in computational resources, increased availability of in vitro data, and regulatory guidance [1, 2]. During the 1990s and the 2000s, QSAR techniques evolved to predict ADME parameters—such as lipophilicity, permeability, and metabolic clearance—from molecular structure with enhanced predictive accuracy [1]. These ADME parameters serve as inputs for mechanistic PBPK models, which then simulate pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles and tissue exposures. Computational methods incorporated biological complexity through compartmental models and nonlinear kinetic functions (e.g., Michaelis–Menten kinetics), though their predictive accuracy was initially constrained by limited computational resources and sparse experimental data sets. The financial cost of being wrong in ADME prediction is massive. Almost 40% of drug candidates stuck in clinical trials in the early 1990s were due to inadequate PK performance [3]. Improved computational ADME screening methods, including QSAR models, subsequently enabled better candidate selection during preclinical development, reducing PK‐related clinical failures to < 10% by the 2000s [3]. This shift illustrates the utility of early‐stage prediction tools in filtering out compounds with unfavorable PK profiles before they reach high‐stakes clinical trials. Better PK prediction methods during early discovery and preclinical stages represent a critical strategic priority for the pharmaceutical industry. Such tools are essential for mitigating the profound financial risks associated with drug development, where total capitalized cost per approved molecule is estimated to range from $314 million to $4.46 billion [4]. Machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) have also proved to be a paradigm shifter in early‐stage ADME prediction, providing unique advantages in dealing with complex biological data as well as pattern recognition across huge experimental data sets. ML algorithms employ rigorous mathematical frameworks with flexible architectures that can automatically learn complex nonlinear relationships between molecular features and ADME outcomes from training data, reducing dependence on manual specifications of functional forms and feature engineering [5]. By leveraging heterogeneous data sources, including molecular structures and genomic information, these models facilitate integrative predictions that better reflect biological complexity [6]. A core strength of ML in drug development is perhaps its capacity for continuous, data‐driven optimization. This iterative, self‐improving nature addresses the high‐failure rate and longitudinal, cyclical nature of pharmaceutical research [7]. The scope of ADME prediction has been furthered by recent advances in ML, particularly deep learning models such as graph neural networks (GNNs). Unlike traditional methods, these platforms can analyze molecular patterns directly to capture intricate details and global shapes that dictate ADME profiles [8]. Moreover, their ability to quantify uncertainty and justify specific predictions has made them highly attractive to both scientists and regulatory bodies [9]. This review explores the synergy between classical pharmacological principles and contemporary data‐driven methodologies. We analyze diverse ML architectures, with a dedicated focus on data quality assurance and robust validation frameworks. Beyond technical implementation, we examine real‐world applications across the drug development lifecycle and address critical industrial challenges, including data scarcity, the imperative for model explainability, and evolving regulatory landscapes. By synthesizing these emerging technologies, we aim to delineate the industry's trajectory and provide strategies to overcome current bottlenecks. Ultimately, this work serves as a comprehensive resource for researchers at the intersection of drug development and computational science, offering both the theoretical foundations and the practical insights necessary to optimize ML‐enabled ADME prediction [10].

Evolution of Machine Learning‐Enhanced PBPK Modeling › Traditional PBPK Approaches and Their Limitations

PBPK modeling is a mechanistic framework that mathematically represents drug disposition by simulating individual organs and tissues as compartments connected by blood flow. PBPK modeling has been shown to be an effective tool in drug development for predicting time‐concentration PK profiles and key exposure metrics (e.g., Cmax and AUC) in plasma and tissues. While PBPK is typically not employed during initial high‐throughput compound screening due to the requirement for compound‐specific input parameters, it becomes valuable once lead candidates are identified and preliminary ADME data become available [11, 12]. PBPK model parameterization strategies differ in how they integrate experimental data and prior information, though terminology in the literature varies. A fundamental distinction exists between population pharmacokinetic (popPK) and PBPK approaches: popPK models employ “top‐down” parameter estimation by fitting compartmental structures directly to observed clinical concentration‐time data, whereas PBPK models incorporate prior physiological and physicochemical knowledge into model architecture [12]. Within PBPK approaches, the field recognizes a spectrum of parameterization strategies. “Bottom‐up” PBPK relies predominantly on in vitro and in silico data to predict drug‐specific parameters before in vivo studies, thereby minimizing animal use but requiring extensive experimental characterization [12, 13]. “Middle‐out” approaches represent a cornerstone of modern PBPK, where parameters are iteratively refined as additional in vitro and in vivo data become available throughout the drug development lifecycle [12, 14]. PBPK frameworks may also incorporate clinical concentration data for parameter refinement, a process often described as integrating “top‐down” elements [15]. However, this remains distinct from purely empirical popPK because the underlying model architecture retains its physiological basis even during data‐fitting [15]. ML‐enhanced QSAR predictions serve as in silico inputs within these PBPK parameterization frameworks—most commonly supporting “bottom‐up” or “middle‐out” strategies—by providing predicted values for compound‐specific parameters (tissue partition coefficients, clearances, plasma protein binding) that would otherwise require experimental measurement [10, 16, 17]. This workflow maintains the mechanistic PBPK model structure while improving input parameter quality through data‐driven prediction methods. Advanced PBPK models contain 30–50 compound‐specific parameters, such as tissue‐to‐plasma partition coefficients (Kp), metabolic clearances, binding propensities, and absorption rate constants [11, 18]. Obtaining these parameters involves extensive laboratory experimentation, which causes bottlenecks in development timelines and utilizes substantial resources [11, 19, 20]. For instance, the acquisition of all partition coefficients necessary for a single compound can take 3–4 weeks of continuous laboratory effort [19]. Classical PBPK predictive validity is still difficult for various compounds [21]. Other limitations are computational demands, where whole‐body simulations with full coverage take significant processing time on typical computing systems. A major limitation arises when there are knowledge gaps for certain metabolic pathways, such that PK outcomes cannot be accurately predicted, especially in the case of drug–drug interactions (DDI) [23]. Evolution of Machine Learning‐Enhanced PBPK Modeling › Integration of ML‐Predicted Parameters Into PBPK Models

The integration of ML with traditional PBPK modeling represents an emerging approach to address certain limitations of conventional methodologies. Contemporary approaches usually comprise a three‐step process: construction of PK profile databases, creation of ML models for the prediction of key ADME parameters, and incorporating these ML‐predicted parameters as inputs into mechanistic PBPK models to simulate detailed PK profiles (Figure 1) [33, 34]. This method can streamline model construction by reducing the number of experimental measurements required for certain parameters. Notable applications of this integrated approach include its use in nanomedicine drug delivery systems [34, 38]. As shown in Table 2, studies by Chou et al. and Lin et al. applied an ML‐enhanced nanoparticle‐specific PBPK model to predict nanoparticle tumor delivery with good accuracy, having R 2 values over 0.8 [34, 38]. These models incorporated deep neural networks (DNNs) to predict distribution parameters that were then integrated into the mechanistic PBPK frameworks [34, 38]. Additional algorithmic approaches continue to be explored for PBPK parameter prediction. Neural ordinary differential equation (Neural‐ODE) frameworks represent one such approach, combining differential equation solutions within neural network models [52]. These frameworks outperform traditional population PK model methods and less complex ML options across a wide range of dosing scenarios [52]. This integration offers an emergent framework for combining the mechanistic aspect of the PBPK model with the pattern recognition capabilities of deep learning. While classical PBPK models and ML‐PBPK models demonstrate disparate degrees of accuracy for different compounds and parameters, integrated approaches (i.e., PBPK models using ML‐predicted parameters) are promising in some applications. For example, in a head‐to‐head comparison on the same data set of 40 small molecules administered intravenously in humans, PBPK models supplemented with ML‐predicted parameters (fup, Caco‐2 permeability, and total plasma clearance) achieved 65% predictive accuracy within twofold error for area under curve (AUC) values, compared to 47.5% predictive accuracy when using standard in vitro experimental inputs under identical modeling conditions [13]. Evolution of Machine Learning‐Enhanced PBPK Modeling › Advanced Parameter Optimization and Population Variability

One of the advantages of ML‐aided PBPK modeling is its capability to simultaneously refine parameter optimization and the representation of population variability. This dual enhancement addresses two primary limitations of conventional PBPK modeling: the difficulty of precise parameter estimation and the authentic representation of human biological diversity. ML algorithms have greatly enhanced the prediction of compound‐specific parameters that are traditionally labor‐intensive to derive experimentally. For example, Kamiya et al. applied a light gradient‐boosting machine (LightGBM) algorithm to predict several input parameters (e.g., absorption rate constant and volume of distribution), creating an ML‐enabled PBPK model that can simulate PK profiles for over 200 compounds based solely on their molecular descriptors [45]. In a similar manner, research by Führer et al. showed that clearance parameters could be accurately forecasted from chemical structures using DNNs with little dependence on costly in vitro testing [31]. Population heterogeneity in PBPK models stems from inter‐individual variability in physiological parameters (e.g., organ size, blood flow rate), biochemical properties (e.g., CYP450 enzyme abundance), and genetic polymorphisms. ML methods excel at detecting subtle, non‐linear patterns within these complex data sets that often elude deterministic or traditional parametric modeling [23, 24]. Sophisticated frameworks, such as nested Monte Carlo simulations, now readily decouple parameter uncertainty (knowledge gaps) from intrinsic biological variability [53]. This distinction is critical for drug developers to decide whether to further characterize a sensitive subpopulation or invest in experimental assays to reduce parameter error. Several ML methods have been used to describe population variability, such as the Bayesian analysis, neural network, and ensemble methods. When data are sparse, population‐based methods like nonparametric adaptive grid (NPAG) or Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling are instrumental in identifying potential covariates and quantifying uncertainty [54]. A major evolution in ML‐PBPK is the shift from single‐point predictions to probabilistic outputs [53, 54, 55]. Bayesian frameworks and ensemble techniques now provide prediction confidence intervals, offering a quantitative measure of model reliability [53]. For example, Gülave et al. demonstrated that ensemble approaches could not only enhance the accuracy of blood–brain barrier (BBB) transport prediction but also forecast uncertainty for better‐informed, risk‐based decision‐making during lead optimization [26]. These developments indicate that ML‐PBPK modeling is moving beyond merely making predictions more precise to delivering more comprehensive decision‐making support during the drug development process. By integrating parameter optimization with robust variability characterization, these combined strategies offer a mathematically rigorous alternative to traditional PBPK workflows.

Advanced ML Architectures for QSAR Models‐Based ADME Prediction › Modern QSAR Approaches

Initial QSAR strategies were defined by linear statistical techniques, but current tendencies have made more sophisticated ML algorithms that can determine complex, non‐linear relations between molecular structure and ADME properties [63]. Contemporary QSAR strategies have shifted further away from traditional fingerprint‐based representations to leverage more nuanced molecular descriptors. The history of QSAR modeling has been marked by a succession of innovations. For example, sophisticated quantitative read‐across structure–activity relationship (q‐RASAR) by ML combines the advantages of QSAR and Read‐Across [64]. This strategy enables improved prediction based on structurally similar compounds that have known attributes. Another significant accomplishment is the development of quantitative read‐across structure–property relationship (q‐RASPR) model strategies, which attempt intrinsic membrane permeability prediction by creating interpretable models relating atomic and structural properties to PK characteristics directly [65]. Ensemble techniques frequently outperform single algorithms for the ADME prediction by minimizing model‐specific biases. Fan et al. demonstrated that consensus‐based ensemble methods made more accurate predictions of the half‐life of the small‐molecule drugs compared to individual techniques [66]. Di Lascio et al. established that ensemble models effectively balance the strengths of global and local models, providing robust predictions across diverse chemical spaces [10]. Deep learning has further enhanced QSAR model predictivity. For example, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for multi‐variable image analysis QSAR (MIA‐QSAR) have proven effective in characterizing ADME parameters, such as plasma protein binding [17]. Similarly, convolutional recurrent neural networks and transfer learning (CRNNTL) methods have shown high efficiency in predicting certain drug properties even when training data sets are limited [57]. DNN–based QSAR approaches have also been successfully applied to predict elimination half‐lives in dogs, supporting preclinical drug development and interspecies extrapolation [67]. GNNs are an advancement in molecular representation to forecast ADME. In contrast to typical molecular descriptors or fingerprints with the risk of overlooking structural details, GNNs maintain the full topological molecule structure since molecules are represented as graphs where atoms are nodes and bonds are edges [61]. This type of representation enables the model to learn meaningful structural features influencing ADME properties irrespective of pre‐determined descriptors. The latest developments in GNN models have resulted in remarkable improvements in ADME prediction accuracy. The message passing neural network (MPNN) architecture has proven superior in deciphering intricate atomic interactions and their cumulative impact on PK properties [68]. More recently, the integration of ensemble learning methods with GNNs has significantly enhanced the prediction of clearance and volume of distribution [69]. Attention mechanisms, adapted from natural language processing, have been successfully integrated into molecular modeling to identify the most important regions of a molecule when predicting ADME properties [70]. The combination of attention mechanisms and GNNs has been exceedingly powerful in capturing non‐linear structure–property relationships for drug candidates. A cutting‐edge evolution in this regard is the incorporation of quantum computing concepts into the GNN model. Zheng et al. proposed QCS‐ADME, a quantum circuit search method for drug property prediction, which demonstrated improved results on imbalanced data sets—a common challenge in ADME modeling where specific property values are sparsely represented [8].

Advanced ML Architectures for QSAR Models‐Based ADME Prediction › Multimodal Learning Integration

Multimodal learning approaches combine different types of data to enhance the accuracy of ADME prediction. Rather than looking only at chemical structures, these approaches integrate other information, such as genomic data, protein structures, and physiological parameters, to create a more complete picture of how a drug behaves. This combination is valuable for understanding intricate ADME behaviors that are sensitive to how a drug interacts with specific biological systems. Proteochemometric (PCM) modeling is a well‐established multi‐modal method that considers both the drug molecule and its target protein simultaneously to more precisely predict how they interact [71]. This method has been markedly improved with the incorporation of ML methods, providing more sophisticated modeling of drug‐target interactions that influence ADME characteristics. Recent progress has also led to models that integrate experimental lab results between various assays and in silico prediction. For example, Cozac et al. introduced the kMoL library, which uses federated learning for drug discovery. This allows different researchers to merge data from a variety of sources without compromising on data privacy, which is crucial for protecting proprietary drug information while still improving model accuracy [60].

Advanced ML Architectures for QSAR Models‐Based ADME Prediction › Comparative Analysis of ML Algorithms

The wide variety of ML methods available for ADME prediction offers both opportunities and challenges. Table 3 provides a comparison of the main ML paradigms, highlighting their strengths, limitations, along with representative applications and performance metrics. Data set size significantly influences algorithm selection for ADME prediction. Traditional ML methods, such as random forest (RF) and gradient boosting machine (GBM), demonstrate robust performance on moderately sized data sets and maintain stability across various ADME endpoints [16]. In contrast, deep learning approaches, particularly GNNs, generally require much larger data sets to be effective. Walter et al. demonstrated that with industrial‐scale data sets containing 10s of 1000s to over 100,000 compounds, multi‐task GNN models outperformed traditional approaches across multiple ADME endpoints. However, they also noted that without enough data, these advanced models struggle to beat simpler methods based on traditional chemical descriptors [58]. Performance varies depending on the specific ADME task. While RF and GBM models are excellent for “Yes/No” classifications—such as predicting if a drug will cross the BBB—no single algorithm is best for every property [16]. Di Lascio et al. illustrated that “local” ML models typically performed well in ADME prediction of compounds with similarities to the training set, while “global” ML models trained on the data set of various compounds generally have better predictive performance for diverse chemical sets [10]. This suggests that the best algorithm selection must be data set‐dependent and property‐specific. Endpoint complexity further guides algorithmic choice. For endpoints with limited data, multi‐task learning (training on several properties at once) and transfer learning (using a pre‐trained model) are effective strategies to boost prediction accuracy [58, 72]. Model interpretability also influences algorithm selection in ADME prediction. A key advantage of traditional ML methods like RF and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) is that they can be easily “explained” using tools like SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), identifying which molecular descriptors most strongly influence predictions. Recent studies have successfully applied these interpretability methods to understand the physicochemical features governing metabolic stability [62], tissue distribution [74], and elimination half‐life [66], facilitating rational compound optimization. While deep learning models present greater interpretability challenges, new techniques are making them more transparent. In practice, the best results are often achieved by testing multiple ML algorithms with cross‐validation on the specific data set or employing ensemble strategies that combine diverse methods. Computational needs and deployment costs also differ. Simple models like RF and SVMs are efficient and easy to use for relatively small‐sized data sets and can be appropriately deployed into production. Deep learning methods tend to demand high computations during training but can provide quicker inference times once the models are deployed. To save time and resources, recent studies have used transfer learning to adapt existing models to new tasks [57]. Likewise, federated learning architectures now allow models to be trained across different research sites without moving the actual data, ensuring both efficiency and privacy [59]. Data Quality and Integration Frameworks

The quality, consistency, and interoperability of the input data can greatly affect the reliability of ML‐enhanced PBPK and QSAR models. This section explores critical methods of data handling, quality control (QC), validation, and the integration of different data sources in ADME prediction systems.

Data Quality and Integration Frameworks › Data Standardization and Structure Preprocessing

The reliance on multiple experimental data sources represents a great challenge in the ML‐enhanced ADME prediction. Pharmaceutical research typically extracts data from public databases (e.g., ChEMBL, PubChem), published literature, and clinical trials [33]. The alignment of data from multiple sources requires the harmonization of the units, measurement protocols, and reporting formats [75, 76]. Recent studies have shown that implementing standardized frameworks could improve the compatibility across different databases and help the workflow of automatically integrating data. Experimental conditions, such as assay protocols, analytical methods, and biological reagent sources, can significantly influence measured ADME parameters and introduce systematic variability across data sets that must be addressed during data integration [77]. For example, protein binding assays may yield inconsistent results depending on plasma source (human vs. animal), protein concentration, and the analytical equipment used [13, 33]. Likewise, the permeability results from different cell lines or membrane systems, like the human colon adenocarcinoma (Caco‐2) cell monolayers and parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA), cannot be directly compared without the normalization of the experimental results [78]. The numerical ranges of the molecular descriptors used in QSAR and ML‐PBPK models are often wide, which makes standardized feature engineering approaches important. The standardization of the chemical structure is usually required to deal with molecular variations, including tautomerism, stereochemistry, and salt forms. One example is the open‐source workflow using the Konstanz Information Miner (KNIME), which was developed to standardize the chemical structures [77]. The standardization process includes 8 major steps from validation to output generation [77]. This workflow has been successfully adopted by global collaborative modeling projects [79, 80] and the Distributed Structure‐Searchable Toxicity (DSSTox) Database of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [81]. When integrating PK data from multiple studies into ML‐enhanced PBPK or QSAR models, time‐concentration profiles and derived PK parameters require specialized normalization methods to ensure comparability. Cubic splines are one of the resampling techniques that standardize temporal resolution across studies with different sampling schedules [82], enabling ML algorithms to extract consistent features from concentration‐time curves for PBPK model training or validation. Compounds exhibiting linear PK, dose‐ and body weight‐normalized exposure metrics (AUC/dose/kg and Cmax/dose/kg) facilitate cross‐study integration by removing dosing regimen variability—a critical preprocessing step when training ML models on pooled clinical data sets [83].

Data Quality and Integration Frameworks › Quality Control, Validation, and Data Integration

Recent research indicates that the variations in ML data preprocessing can spread through the entire modeling pipeline. These variations often create hidden biases that could disproportionately affect certain chemical domains without being detected by standard accuracy metrics [84]. Therefore, rigorous QC protocols are key for ensuring the robustness of the ML models. The management of the missing data is one of the major challenges in the ADME data set. Multiple imputation is considered a more flexible missing data approach than single imputation, especially when the pattern of the missing data correlates with the feature of the chemical structure [85]. Nevertheless, simpler methods like mean imputation are still commonly used because of their computational efficiency [86]. For PBPK modeling, missing data handling needs methods that preserve physiological plausibility. ML methods can help predict key ADME input parameters such as logP and tissue partition coefficients for data‐limited compounds while maintaining consistency with physicochemical constraints [87]. Additionally, Bayesian and population‐based modeling approaches can characterize parameter uncertainty and interindividual variability in PBPK models, which is essential for maintaining mechanistic integrity [55]. To further ensure data quality, batch effect correction techniques address experimental variability unrelated to biological differences. Techniques like ComBat and ComBat‐Seq implemented in R or pyComBat in Python utilize empirical Bayes methods to remove technical biases [88]. These steps are crucial for integrating data from different laboratories, ensuring that the model learns true biological signals rather than experimental artifacts. For validation, the standard k‐fold cross‐validation sometimes provides overly optimistic performance estimates for ADME predictions. This is because structurally similar compounds may appear in both the training and test sets, leading the model to “memorize” rather than “learn” [89]. To address this, non‐random cross‐validation methods, including scaffold‐splits and time‐splits, could be better alternatives. Scaffold‐splits ensure a range of structurally diverse compounds are represented across the validation folds, while the time‐splits method is a better simulation of the expected performance in drug discovery contexts [90]. External validation against independent data sets remains the gold standard for proving model reliability [91]. On another note, reporting performance metrics must be consistent with the intended applications. For example, balanced accuracy and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) are useful for classification problems with class imbalance [91]. For regression models, such as those predicting PK parameters that span several orders of magnitude, the geometric mean fold error (GMFE) is preferred. GMFE often accommodates the logarithmic distribution of PK parameters and reliably maintains accuracy across the entire concentration ranges [24]. While techniques like Gaussian processes offer extensive uncertainty quantification, they are computationally demanding [92]. Their complexity increases dramatically as more compounds are added (scaling cubically), which can make them impractical for large‐scale ADME parameter prediction without approximation techniques [92]. Finally, ML strategies are transforming data integration methods for ADME predictions in drug discovery. Modern multi‐modal methods now integrate in vitro measurements with whole‐organism PK, refining traditional in vitro‐in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) approaches [12, 13]. ML‐driven strategies can now identify complex relationships between cellular metabolism and systemic drug clearance [5, 11]. Additionally, multi‐task learning methods improve the stability of prediction performance for ADME predictions by modeling several related ADME endpoints simultaneously, allowing the model to learn shared biological patterns across different parameters [6, 7].

Data Quality and Integration Frameworks › Strategies to Address Data Quality Challenges

Limited chemical diversity and data quality issues pose significant challenges for developing generalizable ADME prediction models. Multiple complementary strategies have emerged to address these limitations at both the algorithmic and organizational levels. At the algorithmic level, transfer Learning enables leveraging knowledge from large, general data sets. In this approach, models are pre‐trained on extensive molecular property data sets before being “fine‐tuned” for specific ADME endpoints where data may be scarce [57, 72]. Data augmentation further expands limited data sets through generating alternative chemical representations (such as different SMILES strings) of the same molecule [57]. Moreover, multi‐task learning improves stability by training a single model on several related ADME endpoints at once, allowing the system to identify shared biological patterns that a single‐task model might miss [7, 58]. At the organizational level, large‐scale collaborations are solving the problem of data silos. Consortium‐based projects, like MachinE Learning Ledger Orchestration for Drug DiscoverY (MELLODDY), demonstrate that federated learning enables collaborative model training across organizations without direct data sharing, achieving performance improvements while preserving confidentiality [59, 90]. Furthermore, the industry is adopting more rigorous standardized validation and quality assurance (QA) protocols. Instead of relying on standard cross‐validation, modern workflows use scaffold‐splitting, time‐splitting, and prospective temporal validation to ensure models can truly predict new chemical structures [32, 64, 65, 93]. QA pipelines now incorporate automated structure verification, statistical outlier detection, and detailed data provenance, as seen in standardization workflows used for global collaborative QSAR projects [77, 88]. Recent industrial applications that combine multi‐company data through federated and multi‐task learning have shown significant gains in accuracy. These successes highlight that standardization and batch effect correction are not just technical steps but are essential requirements for model robustness in drug development [9, 30, 58, 59].

Implementation in Drug Discovery and Development Pipeline › Integrated Applications for Drug Candidate Selection and Safety Assessment

The field of drug discovery has increasingly explored the integration of ML methods with PBPK modeling to enhance prediction capabilities for drug candidates [31, 35, 87]. Recent advances have demonstrated approaches where ML algorithms predict key input parameters, including clearance, volume of distribution, Kp, and oral bioavailability, which are then incorporated into traditional PBPK model structures [31, 32, 35]. For instance, one study developed DNNs to predict rat PK parameters from molecular structures, with these ML‐derived parameters subsequently used in mechanistic PBPK frameworks [31]. Another approach combined ML‐based similarity assessments with mechanistic partitioning equations to improve predictions of small molecule distribution in vivo [87]. While ML‐predicted parameters may carry higher uncertainty than experimental data, they enable the high‐throughput screening of virtual libraries, allowing researchers to prioritize lead compounds before synthesis [32, 39]. DDIs remain a formidable barrier to drug safety, accounting for approximately 30% of all adverse drug events [94]. ML approaches, particularly graph‐based models, have demonstrated value in predicting potential DDIs involving Cytochrome P450 (CYP)‐mediated metabolism [95]. The GraphCySoM model represents a graph‐based system that learns atom‐level interactions to detect structural features of CYP‐mediated metabolism better than conventional descriptor‐based methods [95]. While both traditional PBPK models and ML‐enhanced approaches continue to face challenges when predicting interactions between novel medications with limited mechanistic data [23], these computational methods provide complementary tools for assessing DDI risk during drug development. These developments extend beyond the DDI landscape to broader safety assessment applications. The ML‐PBPK models have demonstrated the ability to forecast drug bioaccumulation in specific tissues, which could support toxicity evaluation. By integrating quantitative adverse outcome pathways (qAOPs), these ML‐PBPK models connect molecular structures to toxicity endpoints like hepatotoxicity and developmental neurotoxicity [96]. Furthermore, ML‐enhanced QSAR models are increasingly used to screen for cardiotoxicity and sensory irritation (skin or eye) during the early development phase, significantly reducing the likelihood of late‐stage attrition [96].

Implementation in Drug Discovery and Development Pipeline › Patient‐Specific and Population Considerations

Three main innovations in ML‐enhanced modeling approaches address population variability issues through: (1) PBPK models with ML‐Derived Parameters for clinical translation, (2) multi‐task learning for special populations, and (3) regulatory‐grade predictive frameworks. First, recent research demonstrated that ML‐PBPK tools could provide more accurate human PK parameter predictions than traditional in vitro approaches alone [13]. Additionally, ML demonstrates the ability to forecast essential ADME parameters from restricted data sets when analyzing vulnerable population groups [74]. For example, researchers utilized a RF algorithm to develop a two‐dimensional (2D) QSAR model that predicts Kp values by using physicochemical descriptors instead of in vivo data [97]. Recent advances in ML‐based PK modeling extend beyond human applications to veterinary medicine [93]. Wu et al. developed ML‐based QSAR models for predicting drug plasma half‐lives in food‐producing animals, with their DNN achieving strong predictive accuracy (R 2 of 0.67) [93]. These veterinary models offer dual benefits: they improve animal health and support human safety by better predicting drug clearance and residue levels across the food supply chain.

Future Perspectives and Recommendations › Emerging Technologies and Regulatory Considerations

The future of ML‐enhanced ADME prediction lies in unified systems that harmonize disparate methodologies. A key trend is the transition toward platforms that would merge ML with mechanistic models, gradually dissolving the divide between data‐driven and mechanistic approaches. Neural‐ODE architectures exemplify this shift by embedding fundamental PK principles directly into neural network design, preserving mechanistic interpretability while leveraging the predictive power of deep learning [52]. Furthermore, novel approaches now unite molecular, cellular, and physiological scales into single computational frameworks, achieving up to a 17.5% improvement in translational accuracy over traditional sequential methods [13]. Moreover, advanced generative AI systems now optimize molecular structure for both target binding and desirable ADME profiles simultaneously [68]. Additionally, new models now use biological context, such as protein structures, together with tissue‐specific gene expression patterns and systems biology networks, to enhance predictions of ADME behavior in different contexts [49, 71, 74]. This contextual embedding is especially critical for compounds that have intricate disposition patterns or those targeting specific tissue compartments. In nanomedicine, ML approaches are transforming how nanoparticle‐based therapeutics are designed and regulated. Nanoinformatics platforms now utilize ML to predict the biodistribution, efficacy, and toxicity from physicochemical properties, often incorporating real‐time monitoring data to optimize delivery systems dynamically [98]. Regulatory interest in these tools has matured. The FDA's 2013 Nanotechnology Regulatory Science Research Plan emphasizes the need for robust “structure–function relationship” data to understand the in vivo fate of nanomaterials [99]. This regulatory framework demonstrates the FDA's recognition that additional approaches are needed to address the unique challenges of nanoscale materials in biological environments, with particular focus on developing risk assessment methodologies and modeling tools specific to nanotechnology‐enabled products [99]. The regulatory landscape is evolving rapidly to match these technological strides. A foundational milestone was reached with the European Medicines Agency (EMA)'s September 2024 Reflection Paper on the use of AI in the medicinal product lifecycle, which established a risk‐based framework for AI applications from drug discovery through to post‐authorization [100]. This was complemented by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)'s draft guidance on “Considerations for the Use of Artificial Intelligence to Support Regulatory Decision‐Making for Drug and Biological Products” and the subsequent joint EU‐US guiding principles for good AI practice in drug development in January 2026 [101, 102]. Modern regulatory principles place a strong emphasis on model transparency, explainability, and interpretability, requiring that AI‐driven conclusions remain grounded in mechanistic understanding to ensure patient safety [100, 101, 102]. Advanced modeling architectures that lack transparent internal data representation face increasing recognition from regulatory agencies regarding their performance versus interpretability challenges [100]. The current regulatory guidelines for PBPK modeling require showing predictive performance across multiple compounds with similar ADME characteristics for high‐impact applications [103, 104]. ML‐enhanced approaches would probably need to meet these established validation standards at minimum, while potentially addressing additional considerations related to their unique methodology.

Future Perspectives and Recommendations › Open Challenges and Research Priorities

The implementation of ML‐enhanced PBPK and QSAR approaches has brought significant progress, yet multiple unresolved challenges persist. The main barrier to progress stems from limited data availability because many models lack sufficient chemical diversity and balanced structural class representation [58]. In nanomedicine, ML‐based modeling faces unique challenges beyond conventional pharmaceuticals [105]. The limited market share of nanomedicines results in fewer clinical studies and limited data availability, making “big data” approaches less feasible compared to other research areas where ML typically excels [105]. The complex nature of nanomaterials presents multi‐dimensional challenges, especially in modeling the in vivo behavior of these nanomaterials, where the interplay between nanomaterial properties and biological systems creates significant variability [105]. Rather than purely data‐driven approaches, nanomedicine development often requires the design and extrapolation of an intelligent model combining human expertise with computational frameworks [105]. This is particularly evident in PK analyses, where the small quantity of available PK data limits the application of ML and artificial neural networks (ANNs), necessitating PBPK models with ML‐Derived Parameters that integrate physiological with non‐physiological compartments to better understand relationships between critical quality attributes and PK behavior without overestimating in vitro effects [105]. Furthermore, the research shows that publicly accessible ADME data sets contain only a small fraction of the essential chemical space required for drug development applications, which leads to risks when models encounter new molecular structures [7, 81]. On another note, the combination of molecular‐level predictions with physiological models faces major challenges because parameter uncertainties propagate through the system. Hines et al. revealed through their research that QSAR‐predicted parameter uncertainties lead to specific chemical‐dependent changes in PBPK model results [55]. The analysis revealed that fraction unbound, intrinsic clearance, and LogP input parameters had the greatest potential to affect PBPK predictions while some chemicals showed greatly broader uncertainty ranges than others [55]. The application of QSAR prediction uncertainty ranges to IVIVE calculations resulted in equivalent administered dose (EAD) estimates that were more than 60% lower for specific compounds because parameter uncertainty strongly affects prediction outcomes [55]. In addition, the acceptance of ML‐based ADME predictions by regulatory bodies depends on interpretability, but the trade‐off between model complexity and explanatory power remains a persistent challenge. A 2024 study quantified this tradeoff by comparing ML‐QSAR models trained with and without high‐dimensional molecular fingerprints (1024‐bit extended‐connectivity fingerprints). Removing these complex but non‐interpretable structural representations decreased prediction performance by only 0.02 in Pearson's correlation coefficient, demonstrating that more transparent QSAR models using interpretable physicochemical descriptors can achieve comparable accuracy to models incorporating non‐interpretable features [6]. To address the interpretability‐performance tradeoff, several practical strategies have emerged. First, hierarchical modeling approaches enable starting with interpretable baseline models (e.g., linear QSAR for simplified PBPK structures) before incrementally adding complexity, allowing researchers to quantify performance gains against interpretability costs at each step [6]. Second, post hoc explainability methods—including SHAP values and attention mechanisms—can reveal which molecular features or physiological parameters drive predictions in complex models, providing mechanistic insights without sacrificing accuracy [6]. Third, ensemble strategies that combine interpretable models with high‐performance black‐box approaches offer a middle ground: transparent models guide decision‐making while complex models provide accuracy benchmarks and catch edge cases. Fourth, feature selection techniques that prioritize mechanistically relevant descriptors (e.g., LogP, molecular weight, protein binding markers) over purely statistical features help maintain biological plausibility while improving model parsimony [62, 74]. Finally, uncertainty quantification approaches—including Bayesian methods and Monte Carlo simulations—explicitly characterize prediction confidence in PBPK‐ML frameworks, enabling users to assess model reliability and identify scenarios requiring additional validation [53, 54]. Research priorities should aim to address these fundamental problems. Standardized benchmark data sets across various chemical spaces would enable objective comparison of multi‐objective optimization methods in drug discovery to better evaluate how different approaches balance competing pharmaceutical objectives while maintaining molecular diversity [7, 59]. Moreover, novel model architectures leverage available methodologies to develop the model with both interpretability and the ability to utilize complex interactions among data classes, specifically in the form of explainable AI methods that enhance ADME predictions [6]. Moreover, the integration of QSAR‐PBPK benefits from uncertainty quantification methods through the implementation of ML approaches, including Bayesian neural networks, ensemble‐based models, and Gaussian mean–variance estimators. The computational methods enable the modeling of aleatoric uncertainty (inherent experimental variability) and epistemic uncertainty (model knowledge limitations), which leads to more reliable predictions across chemical spaces and perhaps an optimal pharmaceutical resource allocation during early‐stage drug discovery [53, 54, 64]. Finally, researchers from different sectors (government, academia, and industry) should use collaborative efforts to contribute to the ML‐enhanced PBPK and QSAR model development. This could help bridge the gap between academics who focus on model building and industries that focus on evidence‐based decision making and standard operating procedures (SOPs), while continuing to support the development of standardized validation approaches and reporting metrics—ultimately with the potential to decrease the slow uptake of more complex computational approaches into pharmaceutical development workflows. For the application of nanomedicine, critical research priorities include: (1) developing specialized ML architectures that incorporate physicochemical transformations of nanomaterials in biological systems into predictive models [106]; (2) establishing comprehensive nanomaterial characterization databases with standardized protocols and reporting formats to support robust ML model development [107]; (3) creating multilevel modeling frameworks that bridge molecular interactions, cellular uptake mechanisms, and physiological distribution of nanomaterials [107]; and (4) developing interpretable PBPK models with ML‐derived parameters that can satisfy regulatory requirements while accurately capturing the unique biodistribution patterns of nanotherapeutics. These priorities align with the 2013 Nanotechnology Regulatory Science Research Plan of the FDA, which emphasizes the need for improved methods to understand structure–function relationships and ADME properties specific to nanomaterials [99].

Future Perspectives and Recommendations › Best Practices and Implementation Strategies

The successful deployment of ML‐enhanced PBPK and QSAR methods in regulatory settings demands a strategic fusion of technical development and regulatory engagement. To navigate the current landscape, researchers should satisfy a dual‐pathway validation: ensuring ML components meet the good practices from the 2026 joint EU‐US Guiding Principles, while the PBPK structures adhere to established mechanistic validation criteria [100, 101, 102, 103, 104]. Moreover, the transparency of the model architecture and the interpretation method make regulatory assessments easier and boost the credibility of the model [101]. The incorporation of ML components in PBPK models should require the explanation of prediction connections to established mechanistic understanding for better effectiveness. Furthermore, the risk‐based validation strategy matches regulatory approaches while offering an efficient implementation framework. Validation activities need to match the level of complexity and safety implications of model applications [101]. The validation requirements should be more detailed for applications that directly impact patient safety and regulatory decisions since these models need to waive studies or establish critical specifications. Nanomedicine requires additional specialized considerations for ML‐enhanced PBPK and QSAR models. The complex properties of nanomaterials require specialized validation approaches that address nanoparticle‐specific ADME processes [34, 38, 76]. While not explicitly mentioned in any current regulatory guidance, recent research demonstrates the value of combining traditional PBPK frameworks with ML components trained on nanomaterial datasets, including factors like protein corona formation and physicochemical properties. Clear documentation of training data limitations and uncertainty propagation is essential for regulatory acceptance of nanomedicine computational models. Looking ahead, we would expect a new era toward integrative frameworks that combine ML power with mechanistic understanding in the ADME process. These techniques would likely incorporate uncertainty quantification and context‐specific predictions into the shared understanding of the knowledge domain. For nanomedicines specifically, these approaches would need to address the complex interplay between nanoparticle properties and biological systems to enable accurate prediction of biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy. The integrated approaches would also facilitate transparency and regulatory compliance that the community has grown to expect. As computational methods develop, regulations and policy would similarly adapt toward a focus on interpretative models that capture the core predictive performance of algorithms for drug behavior prediction. As such, it is critical for the scientific community to prioritize developing standardized benchmarks, addressing data limitations, and establishing collaborative ecosystems across sectors. Modelers would shift from being “algorithm developers” to becoming “translational scientists”, bridging the computational innovation with biological understanding across the timelines of the drug development and regulatory processes. This evolution would require new validation approaches that promote sophistication and utility, ultimately enabling us to predict drug behavior more accurately and with greater confidence.
