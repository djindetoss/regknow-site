Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as promising tools in the healthcare sector. However, their reliability in the critical task of identifying risky drug combinations remains unvalidated. Here, we systematically evaluated the potential of LLMs for drug combination alerting under the guidance of the CoMed framework through four aspects: (1) the baseline performance of native LLMs, (2) the contribution of external knowledge grounding via Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), (3) the impact of expert-guided reasoning using context engineering, and (4) the utility of a multiagent architecture for comprehensive and interpretable risk analysis. Notably, by integrating RAG and the context engineering strategy, Qwen2.5-Max-CoT achieved outstanding performance (F1 = 0.971, AUC = 0.982), demonstrating expert-level balance between precision and recall. Furthermore, a case study on aspirin-warfarin validated CoMed's ability to generate accurate assessments in a structured and traceable HTML report. This study demonstrates that enhanced LLMs can reliably and transparently support drug combination risk alerting and clinical decision.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are quickly emerging as powerful tools in clinical medicine. − Their potential to enhance decision-making and streamline complex workflows is substantial, − particularly in applications requiring the interpretation of vast unstructured biomedical data. − Drug combination alerting represents one such critical, high-stakes domain, being essential for improving the safety of multidrug regimens and supporting personalized care. − However, despite the strong general performance of state-of-the-art LLMs, ,− their reliability, accuracy, and generalization capabilities for this specialized task have not been systematically validated. This critical reliability gap poses a significant barrier to their clinical deployment. Drug combination risk analysis is particularly challenging, demanding exceptional factual accuracy, transparent clinical reasoning, and sensitivity to the pharmacological context. ,, Generic LLMs often exhibit domain limitations, leading to well-documented issues such as hallucinations − and a lack of verifiable evidence grounding. − This deficiency creates a “black-box” problem where clinical justifications are untraceable, − making native LLMs fundamentally unsuitable for real-world alerting. − Providing specialized knowledge and targeted guidance to LLMs for specific applications is key to solve these problems. Recently, techniques like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), − structured guidance via Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, − and dedicated multiagent architectures have proven effective in boosting LLM factuality and reasoning reliability in other knowledge-intensive domains. − To bridge this reliability gap, we present a comprehensive, systematic evaluation and a novel solution. We first established the baseline performance of 10 state-of-the-art LLMs using 95 clinically curated drug pairs. Crucially, we then introduced CoMed, a specialized framework that utilizes knowledge grounding and expert-guided context engineering to enhance LLM capabilities. Our results quantify the significant performance gains achieved by CoMed, demonstrating that guided LLMs can transition from unacceptable native reliability to expert-level performance (F1 = 0.971, AUC = 0.982, AUPRC = 0.974). Beyond rigorous benchmarking, the CoMed framework further integrated the multiagent systems. These systems comprehensively summarized vast amounts of scattered medical literature, generating structured, visualized, and easily understandable warnings regarding combined medication risks. The CoMed framework enabled the generation of traceable, clinically meaningful explanations, a cornerstone of trustworthy AI in medicine. Importantly, this work established a methodological foundation for transforming LLMs into highly reliable and interpretable clinical decision support tools.

Methods › Datasets

The dataset of 95 clinically risky drug pairs was systematically curated from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) by three clinical experts among the authors (L.W., L.T.H., and Z.W.W.). The selection process prioritized combinations with high clinical prevalence in polypharmacy and a documented history of serious adverse outcomes. Crucially, the dataset was designed to cover diverse pharmacological mechanisms, ranging from pharmacokinetic to pharmacodynamic interactions, ensuring a robust evaluation of LLM reasoning capabilities. The final list was established through expert consensus to serve as a high-fidelity clinical gold standard. We evaluated comedication across 95 commonly clinical drug pairs using state-of-the-art LLMs, including GPT-5 (Data S1), o3-mini (Data S2), GPT-4o (Data S3), Deepseek-R1 (Data S4), Deepseek-V3 (Data S5), QwQ-plus (Data S6), Qwen2.5-Max (Data S7), Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (Data S8), Claude-3.7-Sonnet (Data S9), Gemini-2.0-Flash (Data S10), and Llama3.3–70b (Data S11). To systematically evaluate combination medication risks, we constructed a domain-specific corpus comprising 1,482 PubMed abstracts (retrieved evidence: RAG-filtered PubMed abstracts) corresponding to these drug pairs. For each drug pair, we retrieved the top 30 most relevant abstracts from PubMed using a “Best Match” sorting strategy. To ensure the quality of this evidence base, all 1,482 retrieved items underwent a rigorous manual screening by three independent clinical experts (L.W., L.T-H., and Z.-W.W.). The experts specifically verified whether each abstract provided substantive information regarding the concurrent use or pharmacological interaction of the drug pair. Only abstracts confirmed through this manual audit were utilized as the ground-truth evidence for evaluating the RAG-integrated LLMs. Metadata including PubMed ID (PMID), title, abstract text, journal name, authors, publication date, and corresponding PubMed URL were extracted and stored in a structured JSON format. To establish reliable reference standards, three clinical pharmacologists with a minimum of five years of experience in medication safety independently reviewed each abstract. The experts determined whether each abstract explicitly described concurrent administration of the specified drug pair, rather than merely mentioning both drugs independently. Reviewers underwent standardized training with 50 sample abstracts to ensure consistent application of inclusion criteria. Specific criteria for positive classification included: (1) explicit mention of coadministration, (2) discussion of interaction effects, or (3) comparative analysis with monotherapy. Inter-annotator agreement was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa, with κ = 0.87 indicating strong consensus. The final gold-standard label for each abstract was determined through majority voting (≥2 expert agreement), resulting in a comprehensive evaluation dataset with high-quality reference annotations (Data S12). For RAG-integrated LLMs, we performed comedication assessments using GPT-5 (Data S13), o3-mini (Data S14), GPT-4o (Data S15), Deepseek-R1 (Data S16), Deepseek-V3 (Data S17), QwQ-plus (Data S18), Qwen2.5-Max (Data S19), Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (Data S20), Claude-3.7-Sonnet (Data S21), Gemini-2.0-Flash (Data S22), and Llama3.3–70b (Data S23). For CoT-integrated LLMs, we conducted assessments using GPT-5-CoT (Data S24), GPT-4o-CoT (Data S25), Deepseek-V3-CoT (Data S26), Qwen2.5-Max-CoT (Data S27), Claude-3.7-CoT (Data S28), Gemini-2.0-Flash-CoT (Data S29), and Llama3.3–70b-CoT (Data S30). The data files are available in the Zenodo repository (10.5281/zenodo.17588193). To maintain the standards of scientific integrity and transparency, the supplementary datasets (Data S1–S32) present the raw, unedited outputs directly generated by the LLMs. Certain character artifacts (e.g., decoding anomalies of specialized symbols like nonbreaking hyphens) have been intentionally preserved. This approach ensures that the benchmarking results reflect the models’ native linguistic behavior and technical idiosyncrasies without the introduction of human postediting bias.

Methods › Combined Medication Identification with CoT-Inspired Context Engineering Strategy

A central challenge in analyzing combination medication literature is accurately distinguishing between text that merely mentions two drugs independently versus those that meaningfully discuss their concurrent use. To address this challenge, we developed a CoT-inspired context engineering strategy that guided large language models through a four-step analytical process designed to emulate expert clinical reasoning. For each abstract-drug pair, the model was presented with the text followed by four sequentially dependent questions. 1. Evidence identification: “Which sentences describe the combined use of [drug1] and [drug2]? If there is no corresponding sentence, please honestly output ‘not mentioned’.” This initial step required the extraction of textual evidence specifically related to coadministration, establishing the evidential foundation for subsequent reasoning. 2. Association assessment: “What do you think of the association between [drug1] and [drug2]? If there is no association, please output ‘no association’.” This step evaluated whether the abstract establishes a meaningful pharmacological or clinical relationship between the drugs beyond mere co-occurrence. 3. Overinterpretation analysis: “Wait, is the combined use of [drug1] and [drug2] being over-interpreted?” This critical reflection step helped mitigate false positives by prompting the model to reconsider whether the text truly discussed concurrent administration rather than sequential use, independent mentions, or hypothetical scenarios. 4. Final determination: “Please finally determine whether the TEXT mentions the combined use of [drug1] and [drug2], and explain the reasons.” The culminating step required the synthesis of prior reasoning into a binary classification with explicit justification, ensuring the decision process remained traceable. This structured reasoning approach mitigated the tendency of LLMs to over-rely on keyword matching or make unfounded inferences. Each model’s response was parsed to produce a standardized output containing: (1) a binary classification (yes/no), (2) a concise justification, and (3) the complete reasoning trace for interpretability. The resulting structured judgments served as the foundation for subsequent risk analysis in texts confirmed to discuss combination therapy.

Methods › Multiagent Framework for Comprehensive Risk Analysis

After abstracts were confirmed to discuss coadministration, CoMed applied a role-specialized multiagent pipeline that decomposed clinical reasoning into five complementary dimensions. In this work, “agent” denoted a specialized analysis module powered by an LLM and governed by common evidence-grounding and formatting policies. A controller orchestrated the agents, integrated their outputs, and produced a consolidated report. This design prioritized interpretability, modularity, and auditability over autonomous multiagent debate, and it aligned each step with established clinical considerations for combination therapy. All agents operated on the same retrieved evidence (RAG-filtered PubMed abstracts by default; full-text articles via PubMed Central and retrieving drug labeling information from DailyMed were also supported); thus, evaluation here targeted reasoning over supplied text, while retrieval quality was assessed separately. Each agent had to (i) extract and quote the sentences supporting its claims, and (ii) emit a normalized, machine-readable record. When the source text did not address a dimension, the agent must returned an explicit “not mentioned” state, which we standardized to Invalid at integration time to avoid overinterpretation. The pipeline evaluated five clinical dimensions that reflect routine decision points in combinational pharmacotherapy. For each dimension, the agent inspected the abstract, highlighted the most relevant sentences, and summarized its assessment in a compact, structured format. For transparency, each agent’s output consisted of (a) verbatim evidence excerpts (quoted sentences) and (b) a concise interpretive summary. If no relevant evidence existed, the agent returned not mentioned rather than inferring beyond the text. In a case study involving the combination of aspirin and warfarin, the analysis generated by Qwen2.5-Max within the multiagent framework was compiled in Data S31.

Methods › Orchestration, Context Passing, and Integration

A controller (MultiAgentSystem) executed the agents in a deterministic sequence, passed forward key intermediate findings as context, recorded interagent messages, and integrated results into a unified risk-benefit profile. Integration applied three policies: The final artifact was a structured report containing (i) dimension-specific summaries with quotations and links to the source abstract, (ii) a brief synthesis describing overall clinical relevance, and (iii) metadata (analysis time, model name, and abstract identifier). This report enabled a straightforward audit of how each conclusion mapped to the underlying text.

Results › Overview of CoMed

Informed by the clinical expertise of multiple physicians, CoMed has been structured into four core components (Figure ). First, CoMed takes the names of targeted drugs as input and searches for relevant literature based on these drug names (Figure a). CoMed retrieves literature from PubMed by default. It also supports access to full-text articles from PubMed Central (PMC) and drug label information from DailyMed. This literature is subsequently formatted and used as reference material for LLMs. Second, each piece of retrieved literature undergoes contextual processing structured by a carefully designed context engineering strategy, which ensures the accurate interpretation of information (Figure b). Notably, incorporating interjections such as “wait” into context engineering helps mitigate hasty inferences and improves the rigor of information parsing. Third, information validated as scientifically sound is further subjected to collaborative risk interpretation from multiple perspectives via a multiagent system (Figure c). Each agent is required to explicitly cite references supporting its output. This constraint not only enhances the traceability of reasoning but also reduces the risk of hallucinations or unsubstantiated claims. Finally, the outputs generated by all individual agents are aggregated and formatted into a structured, user-friendly HTML report (Figure d). This report integrates key combination drug information to facilitate intuitive interpretation by healthcare professionals or researchers, such as potential adverse interactions, population-specific contraindications, and clinical management recommendations. Results › Capability of Native LLMs in Identifying Combined Medication

We benchmarked the accuracy of 10 state-of-the-art LLMs using 95 clinically risky drug combinations. Precision score was 1.000 for all models by construction (FP = TN = 0) due to the set containing no negatives. Consequently, observed performance differences were driven entirely by variations in recall and the resulting F1 scores. The in-depth analysis revealed that all native LLMs achieved recall scores below 0.700, indicating a widespread failure to detect a substantial proportion of true risk combinations and suggesting insufficient reliability (Table ). Moreover, every model attained an F1 score below 0.800, reflecting limited overall judgment capability in specialized medical contexts and a high incidence of both false negatives and false positives (Table ). Even the top-performing models, GPT-5 (recall = 0.645, F1 score = 0.784) and o3-mini (recall = 0.610, F1 score = 0.757), failed to overcome this performance ceiling (Table ). Following the release of GPT-5 on August 7, 2025, OpenAI initially removed access to GPT-4o. However, in response to widespread user criticism over GPT-5′s underwhelming performance, GPT-4o was restored for paid subscribers within 24 h. In this study, we also evaluated the performance of GPT-4o in identifying combined medication. However, GPT-4o yielded lower recall and F1 scores than both GPT-5 and o3-mini, performing comparably to Claude-3.7-Sonnet (Table S1). While GPT-5 achieved the highest performance among native models (F1 score = 0.784), its Recall remained low at 0.645. This indicates that although architectural upgrades contribute to incremental statistical improvements, they do not inherently solve the critical reliability gapspecifically the high false-negative raterequired for high-stakes clinical decision support. Consequently, even the most advanced native models remain unsuitable for direct application without external guidance. Besides, reasoning capabilities did not consistently confer an advantage in drug combination alerting, while Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking demonstrated a performance gain of nearly 20% compared to its standard configuration (Recall, 0.543 vs 0.610; F1 score, 0.703 vs 0.527). Other reasoning-enabled models, including o3-mini, Deepseek-R1, and QwQ-plus, exhibited significantly lower accuracy than their nonreasoning counterparts (Table ). Furthermore, we analyzed the stability of model performance across repeated trials. Consistent with the Recall metric used throughout this study, we evaluated the run-to-run variability using the standard deviation (SD) of Recall. Interestingly, models equipped with explicit reasoning modules exhibited higher instability compared to their base counterparts. For instance, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking recorded the highest variability (Recall SD = 0.043), followed by o3-mini (SD = 0.031), QwQ-plus (SD = 0.024), and Deepseek-R1 (SD = 0.021). This fluctuation in Recallwhere the same model might identify a risk in one run but miss it in anothersuggests that the probabilistic nature of the “reasoning” process can introduce stochastic noise. In a clinical setting, such inconsistency undermines the deterministic reliability required for safety alerting systems, as practitioners need to trust that a system will invariably flag identical risks. Manual audits of model rationales revealed two recurring causes of false negatives. First, when relevant facts were missing from the retrieval context, the model often defaulted to neutral or negative judgments, even when a true interaction existed. Second, the model adopted a cautious stance to avoid overclaiming. For instance, it frequently responded with phrases like “no clear evidence.” While this strategy reduced hallucinated positives, it simultaneously lowered recall. These shortcomings underscored that LLMs’ reliance on static, generalized knowledge obtained during pretraining, which often lacks the requisite domain-specific depth and factual precision, fundamentally compromises their performance in such contexts.

Results › Capability of RAG-Integrated LLMs in Identifying Combined Medication

Integrating LLMs with an external knowledge base (RAG) shifted the evaluation focus from assessing internal memory to evaluating contextual reasoning capabilities. Consequently, the benchmarking unit transitioned from the 95 drug pairs to an evidence-level dataset comprising 1,482 automatically curated abstracts linked to these pairs. In this phase, the models were tasked not merely with recalling a risk but with accurately determining whether each specific abstract explicitly described a coadministration riska task requiring precise natural language understanding. Model performance was evaluated against the expert-annotated ground truth for each abstract using metrics including F1 score, AUC, and AUPRC. This rigorous evidence-level evaluation ensures that the reported improvements reflect the system’s ability to ground its judgments in verifiable data rather than relying on hallucination-prone internal priors. Qwen2.5-Max achieved the most notable enhancement, with an F1 score of 0.885, AUC of 0.951, and AUPRC of 0.897, outperforming GPT-5 and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (F1 score, 0.817 and 0.834; AUC, 0.918 and 0.926; AUPRC, 0.844 and 0.857; Table ). Gains were also observed for Gemini-2.0-Flash and Llama3.3–70b (Table and Table S2). These gains indicated that integrating verifiable, domain-specific evidence helped reduce unsupported assertions. Notably, GPT-5 underperformed GPT-4o when augmented with RAG, contrary to the trend observed without RAG (Table S2 and Table S3). This finding indicated that newer or ostensibly more capable models like GPT-5 may be less responsive to external knowledge grounding. This could be attributed to overreliance on internal priors or to suboptimal integration of retrieved context. Importantly, the reasoning-mode LLMs did not resolve this gap. Models marketed for enhanced reasoning (o3 mini, Deepseek-R1, QwQ-plus, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking) failed to consistently surpass their base counterparts (Table and Table S3). These results indicated that RAG addressed the issue of evidence access for LLMs but did not solve the problem of how LLMs judge this evidence. We then introduced a context engineering strategy, employing predefined multistep query templates to guide LLMs through structured reasoning within the retrieved literature. This approach facilitates LLMs’ comprehension of medical facts and causal relationships, which is crucial for enhancing both model accuracy and interpretability. Specifically, this strategy enables LLMs to identify, locate, and understand whether target drug pairs are explicitly mentioned as coadministered in the literature.

Results › Capability of CoT-Integrated LLMs in Identifying Combined Medication

Across all evaluated models, CoT-inspired context engineering strategy produced consistent and statistically significant performance gains (Figure and Table ). Qwen2.5-Max-CoT achieved superior performance, with an F1 score of 0.971. Llama3.3–70b-CoT, GPT-5-CoT, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-CoT, and Deepseek-V3-CoT also reached F1 scores near or above 0.900 (Figure c). This strategy demonstrably improved model reliability, achieving an excellent balance between risk identification and false positive avoidance. Notably, the F1 scores of the evaluated models consistently rose from below 0.800 to above 0.900 (Table ). This significant improvement underscored the crucial role of context engineering in ensuring high reliability for specialized domain tasks. Other LLMs also showed varying degrees of improvement, with most F1 scores rising above 0.850. For instance, Deepseek-V3-CoT showed an absolute F1 score gain of 16.5% (from 0.744 to 0.867). Gemini-2.0-Flash, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, and GPT-5 also achieved F1 score improvements of 9.2%, 7.5%, and 5.9%, respectively (Table ). These differential gains indicated that the context engineering strategy could narrow performance gaps while preserving each model’s operational profile. Furthermore, the AUC assessed the models’ overall discriminative capability, reflecting their ability to accurately distinguish between explicitly “mentioned” and “unmentioned” drug coadministration in the literature. All models achieved an AUC exceeding 0.920, demonstrating the strong discriminative power conferred by the context engineering strategy (Figure a, d and Table ). Notably, Qwen2.5-Max-CoT achieved an impressive AUC of 0.982, indicating exceptional distinction between mentioned and unmentioned coadministration. Additionally, the AUPRC, which more accurately reflects model performance under imbalanced positive–negative sample conditions regarding false positives and negatives, also showed remarkable gains. Qwen2.5-Max-CoT achieved an AUPRC of 0.974, while Llama3.3–70b-CoT, GPT-5-CoT, Claude-3.7-Sonnet-CoT, and Deepseek-V3-CoT also approached or exceeded 0.900 (Figure b,e and Table ). These results indicated that the models maintained high precision while achieving high recall, exhibiting very few false negatives or false positives. It was also revealed that context-engineered models significantly outperform their unguided counterparts (Table and Table S5). The top-performing model, Qwen2.5-Max-CoT, exhibited even more substantial accuracy gains (F1 score, AUC, and AUPRC) of 9.7%, 8.7%, and 3.3%, respectively, over Qwen2.5-Max. Similar performance enhancements, ranging from 2.5% to 16.6%, were observed across GPT-5-CoT, Llama3.3–70b-CoT, Claude-3.7-CoT, Gemini-2.0-Flash-CoT, and Deepseek-V3-CoT. This consistently high performance indicated that the context-engineering strategy effectively guided models in forming a deeper understanding of medical facts and causal relationships, which was paramount for enhancing both model accuracy and interpretability. Another benefit of the context engineering strategy was a marked reduction in false positives (FP) across all models (Table S4). Because the context engineering strategy requires explicit, checkable justifications before a “co-use” decision is issued, the LLMs were less prone to treat loosely related mentions as supportive evidence. In clinical terms, this translates into greater reliability: unnecessary alerts are curtailed without sacrificing sensitivity to genuine couse evidence. Given the superior performance in prior evaluations, Qwen2.5-Max-CoT was selected as the operational model and used to conduct a systematic comparison with the reasoning-oriented QwQ-plus model (Figure and Figure S1). The analysis focused on an abstract addressing low-dose aspirin and warfarin for the primary prevention of ischemic heart disease (Figure a). Qwen2.5-Max-CoT engaged in a structured four-step reasoning process, progressing from the initial detection of combination therapy mentions to the derivation of clinical implications. Specifically, the model first identified whether the coadministration was referenced (“Question 1” in Figure b), then evaluated the presence and direction of supporting evidence (“Question 2” in Figure b), subsequently assessed whether the claims risked overinterpretation (“Question 3” in Figure b), and finally articulated the clinical relevance of the findings (“Question 4” in Figure b). In evaluating potential overstatement, Qwen2.5-Max-CoT incorporated contextual cuessuch as the interjection “wait”into its reasoning (“Question 3” in Figure b). This measured response reflected an implicit understanding of evidence hierarchy, ensuring that recommendations were grounded in robust data rather than suggestive associations. By contrast, QwQ-plus adopted a direct approach that yielded terse and unstructured outputs. Upon detecting the mere co-occurrence of “warfarin” and “aspirin” within a single sentence, it prematurely inferred explicit endorsement of the combination without performing contextual verification or deeper semantic analysis (Figure c). The comparative case underscored that the context engineering strategy supported more cautious and context-aware reasoning, which constituted a key factor underlying the superior performance of LLMs in benchmark evaluations. Results › Explanation of Combination Medication by Multiagent Architecture

The literature that explicitly mentions the coadministration of targeted drug pair was used as the trusted starting point for the final drug combination alert analysis (Figure a). A context-engineered multiagent strategy guided the LLM to emulate a clinical expert team. Each agent analyzed the risks of the combined drugs from a distinct dimension: indication, target population, adverse effects, mechanism of action, and management guidance (Figure b-f). This enabled structured, interpretable, and collaborative risk assessment grounded in explicit evidence. A dedicated coordination agent integrated all insights into a clear summarization (Figure g). The final output directly supported frontline clinicians with a traceable, evidence-based reportmoving beyond speculative or generic AI responses. Specifically, the indication agent delineated treatment contexts and therapeutic objectives linked to the pair (Figure b). The selectivity agent identified population-specific usage or contraindications (e.g., pediatric subgroups or coronary artery aneurysms; Figure c). The adverse-effect agent extracted comparative harms such as bleeding relative to monotherapy (Figure d). The mechanism-of-action agent explained pharmacodynamic rationale and potential duplications (Figure e). And the clinical-management agent recovered practical guidancemonitoring targets and dosing thresholdsfrom labels and guidelines (Figure f). Finally, a coordinator integrated these outputs and output a concise, context-specific summary with inline citations (Figure g). The aspirin–warfarin example in Figure illustrated this behavior end-to-end (Data S31). Two design rules made the system explainable in practice. First, agents had to abstain when no citation was available; this suppresses unsupported assertions. Second, the coordinator performed consistency checks across agents (e.g., a high bleeding signal triggers a stronger management recommendation) before issuing the final HTML report. By turning the modules into enforceable interfaces rather than optional prompts, the multiagent design converted generic text generation into transparent, evidence-bound decision support. Results › Case Study: CoMed Analysis of the Aspirin–Warfarin Combination

Building on the multiagent explanation above, we present a case study illustrating how a clinician obtains and reviews a CoMed report for aspirin–warfarin combination alerts (Data S32). The report commenced with a clear statement of its provenance, detailing the generation time, model engine, and the precise number of retrieved and included studies (Figure a). This ensured explicit traceability of the report’s freshness and scope. An overall assessment, incorporating citations, served as the initial entry point for readers (Figure b). Subsequent sections then elaborated on this assessment, aligning with established clinical practice dimensions. The “Indications” section precisely localized contexts where co-use was reported, grounding the analysis in specific care settings and therapeutic objectives rather than mere assertions (Figure c). The “Selectivity” explicitly addressed how the benefit-harm balance might vary across different subgroups (Figure d). The “Adverse effects” section highlighted the predominant comparative risk signal, in this instance bleeding, relative to monotherapy (Figure e). The warfarin–aspirin combination is indicated in select high-thrombotic-risk scenariossuch as Kawasaki disease–associated coronary artery aneurysms in children and large anterior myocardial infarction with thromboembolic risk factorswhere dual antiplatelet and anticoagulant effects are warranted. In these specific high-risk cases, the benefit of reducing thrombotic events outweighs the increased risk of bleeding, though for most patients the combination offers no net benefit. “Mechanism” provided an explanation for the concurrent increase in efficacy and harm for this particular drug combination, thereby elucidating the biological underpinnings of both benefit and risk. Specifically, the bleeding risk arises from the irreversible inhibition of platelet COX-1 by aspirin coupled with warfarin’s suppression of vitamin K–dependent clotting factors (Figure f). “Monitoring and management” then distilled monitorable targets and threshold-based rules from existing labels and guidelines (e.g., INR targets and dose limits), effectively translating evidence into actionable clinical recommendations (Figure g). Finally, a comprehensive reference list completed the audit trail, with each superscript linked directly to its source text in PubMed, PubMed Central, or DailyMed, enabling line-by-line verification (Figure h). Discussion and Conclusion

This study established a systematic performance benchmark across 10 state-of-the-art LLMs. This evaluation revealed a fundamental, patient-critical limitation: all native LLMs demonstrated an untenable risk profile for this specialized task. Even the top baseline model, GPT-5, failed to meet clinical reliability thresholds, achieving an F1 score of only 0.784. Characterized by low recall rates and modest F1 scores, these models frequently missed genuine risk combinations, indicating a critical lack of reliability. This deficiency stems from their reliance on generalized and nonvalidated knowledge acquired during pretraining, which lacks the requisite domain depth and accuracy for high-stakes medical applications. Even models purportedly endowed with “reasoning” capabilities, such as o3-mini, Deepseek-R1, and QwQ-plus, generally performed worse than their nonreasoning counterparts without explicit guidance. It was further underscored that raw inferential power alone was insufficient for reliable domain-specific judgments. To overcome this safety barrier, we introduced CoMed, a novel evaluation framework that integrates RAG for knowledge grounding and expert-guided context engineering for structured reasoning. The integration of the knowledge base marked an initial step toward improvement. This step allowed models to access and retrieve relevant medical literature, leading to some enhancement in their ability to identify and locate explicit mentions of drug coadministration. However, this improvement was limited. The LLM required a structured mechanism to effectively process and interpret this complex medical knowledge. The true breakthrough emerged with the introduction of the contextual engineering strategy. By employing predefined multiquestion step templates, this strategy systematically guided LLMs to perform structured reasoning within the retrieved textual context. This critical intervention enabled the models to transcend superficial correlations, fostering a deeper understanding of medical facts and causal relationships pertinent to drugs. The synergistic combination of the specialized knowledge base and innovative contextual engineering strategy achieved a paradigm shift in LLM clinical safety. Models like Qwen2.5-Max, for instance, demonstrated exceptional F1 scores of 0.971, with other leading models, such as GPT-5, also nearing 0.900. These metrics signified an excellent balance between precision and recall, drastically reducing the rates of both false positives and false negatives, which was paramount in medical contexts. It should be noted that our current benchmarking utilized positive drug-risk pairs derived from the FAERS database to rigorously evaluate the models’ sensitivity in scenarios where a missed alert is unacceptable. While the pharmacological complexity of defining a “true negative”the absolute absence of interactionposes challenges for benchmark construction, the CoMed framework addresses potential oversensitivity through its multistep reasoning protocols. Specifically, the “Overinterpretation analysis” serves as a critical safeguard, guiding the system to distinguish between incidental comentions and substantive clinical risks. The significant boost in F1 scores (from below 0.800 to above 0.900) after integrating contextual engineering unequivocally highlighted its indispensable role. Furthermore, the high AUC and AUPRC values demonstrated a superior ability to discriminate between documents explicitly mentioning co-use and those that did not, while effectively balancing misclassification rates even under imbalanced positive/negative sample conditions. The implications of the CoMed framework were profound for both medical research and clinical practice. CoMed offered a promising, accurate, and interpretable tool for managing drug combination risks, directly enhancing patient safety. As demonstrated in the case study on aspirin-warfarin coadministration, CoMed not only accurately summarized increased bleeding risks compared to monotherapy but also provided nuanced guidance on dosage control for specific patient populations (e.g., myocardial infarction patients with additional risk factors) and recommendations for patient monitoring and management during coadministration. Crucially, these analyses were consistently supported by specific, traceable references, providing an evidence-based foundation for experts. This capability transformed LLMs from mere information providers into trustworthy diagnostic and decision-making aids. It is worth noting that the current contextual engineering templates, while effective for alerting drug combinations, may require adaptation for broader applicability across different medical tasks. Overall, when appropriately guided by specialized knowledge and context engineering strategies, LLMs could evolve into indispensable, highly reliable tools, providing accurate and explainable support in specialized domains.
