Abstract

Digital and artificial intelligence (AI) pathology have advanced rapidly and are increasingly embedded in translational research, clinical trials and regulated diagnostic devices. Multiple reviews have addressed the underlying computational methods, oncology applications and clinical translation barriers; however, the operational and regulatory infrastructure required to deploy these technologies across the full drug-development pipeline remains under-reviewed. This structured narrative review synthesises peer-reviewed literature and regulatory documents identified through searches of PubMed, Embase and Web of Science between January 2019 and April 2026 (78 sources retained) on the end-to-end ecosystem connecting AI-ready tissue biobanks, preclinical pathology, AI-assisted patient screening, clinical-trial endpoint adjudication, central pathology review, Contract Research Organization (CRO) workflows and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulatory pathways governing In Vitro Diagnostics (IVD), Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) and Companion Diagnostics (CDx). Unlike prior method-centric or oncology-clinician-centric reviews, we focus on the infrastructure between models and patients, including Laboratory Information Systems (LIS), Image Management Systems (IMS), AI orchestration and structured reporting under Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, College of American Pathologists, Good Laboratory Practice, Good Clinical Laboratory Practice, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and FDA frameworks. We provide a vendor-agnostic comparison of integrated pathology platforms, a mapping of FDA regulatory pathways (510(k), De Novo, premarket approval) to digital-pathology product categories, a stage-by-stage map from each drug-development challenge to its enabling infrastructure and regulatory framework, and a critical appraisal of the unresolved bottlenecks that gate broad adoption: domain shift, interpretability, annotation cost, algorithmic bias, external validation, regulatory uncertainty and reimbursement. A governed, interoperable LIS, IMS and AI ecosystem is a prerequisite for translating digital-pathology research into regulator-grade evidence. Priorities include external validation against reporting standards (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis - Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD-AI), Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM), Development and Early-stage Clinical Identification and Delegation Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence (DECIDE-AI)), harmonisation with the EU AI Act and EU Medical Device Regulation and cost-effectiveness evaluation in real-world deployment.

Introduction

Pathology is undergoing a transition from conventional light microscopy to fully digital workflows. Whole-slide imaging (WSI) scans glass histopathology slides into gigapixel-scale digital images, enabling remote consultation, scalable archiving and quantitative interrogation of tissue.12 Beyond operational efficiencies, digitisation provides the technical substrate for computational and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven inference: digital slides allow reproducible measurement, standardised visualisation and algorithmic interrogation of morphology, addressing long-standing limitations of manual pathology such as inter-observer variability and limited throughput.34 Computational pathology applies mathematical modelling, image processing and statistical learning to extract high-dimensional features from histologic images.4 These approaches enable objective, reproducible and high-throughput quantification of features including tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), mitotic activity, nuclear morphology, glandular architecture and the spatial organisation of the tumour microenvironment.56 By treating each pixel as a measurable data point, computational methods convert tissue sections into structured datasets amenable to statistical analysis and align histomorphology with genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics in modern biomedical research.7 AI, particularly deep learning (DL) using convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has been applied to tissue segmentation, cell detection, grading and subtype classification, with reported performance comparable to expert pathologists on narrow tasks in selected studies.589 AI models trained on H&E-stained slides have been used to predict molecular alterations directly from histology, including microsatellite instability (MSI) and oncogenic mutations.10–12 These observations suggest a learnable relationship between histologic phenotype and molecular genotype, although generalisation across institutions, scanners and staining protocols remains an open problem.1314 Foundation models, pretrained using self-supervised learning on large heterogeneous WSI datasets, generate transferable tissue representations that can be adapted to multiple downstream tasks with reduced reliance on labelled data.15–19 Such models have been reported to improve robustness across scanners and stains, though they do not eliminate domain shift.1314 For routine translational and clinical use, AI models must be embedded within governed digital ecosystems that support traceability, version control, reproducibility and compliance with the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA), College of American Pathologists (CAP), Good Laboratory Practice (GLP), Good Clinical Laboratory Practice (GCLP), Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requirements. These ecosystems include Laboratory Information Systems (LIS) for specimen tracking, Image Management Systems (IMS) for WSI handling and AI orchestration for inference.2021 Such integrated cloud-based platforms have been proposed as a foundation for end-to-end workflows from slide acquisition through AI-assisted analysis to regulator-aligned reporting.22 The application of AI to digital pathology has been reviewed extensively, with recent comprehensive treatments addressing computational methods and oncology applications,5 the translation of deep learning to the clinic,8 biomarker inference from histology,11 multimodal data fusion,23 the technical landscape of foundation models15 and forward-looking perspectives on translational cancer research.2425 Industry-perspective reviews have additionally examined the use of digital pathology in pharmaceutical translational medicine26 and clinical trials.20 The present review complements that literature by adopting a distinct lens: the operational and regulatory ecosystem required to deploy AI-enabled digital pathology across the full drug-development continuum, spanning AI-ready tissue biobanks, through preclinical toxicology and clinical-trial pathology workflows, through central pathology review and Contract Research Organization (CRO) integration, to the regulatory pathways governing In Vitro Diagnostics (IVD), Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) and Companion Diagnostics (CDx) that ultimately determine whether a digital-pathology biomarker reaches patients. Where prior reviews emphasise models or end-user applications, this review emphasises the infrastructure and governance between them. We also depart from the predominantly oncology-focused literature by addressing preclinical animal pathology and CRO workflows, two areas that receive limited treatment elsewhere. We provide a vendor-agnostic comparison of currently available integrated pathology platforms and identify the unresolved challenges that gate adoption at scale: algorithm generalisability, model interpretability, annotation dependence, regulatory uncertainty and cost. To keep the synthesis anchored to drug development rather than cataloguing individual applications, we organise the review around the specific challenge that each stage of the pipeline presents and map that challenge to the digital-pathology solution, the operational infrastructure and the regulatory framework that address it (table 1). Each subsequent section is structured around this challenge-to-solution logic rather than around algorithms or organ systems.

Methods

This is a structured narrative review conducted in accordance with Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles guidance for transparent narrative reviews27; it is narrative rather than systematic and no protocol was registered. Literature was identified through searches of PubMed, Embase and Web of Science between January 2019 and April 2026 (final search 20 April 2026). The search combined two concept blocks with the Boolean operator AND, each block using OR internally: a pathology block comprising (‘digital pathology’ OR ‘computational pathology’ OR ‘whole slide imaging’ OR ‘whole-slide image’ OR histopathology OR immunohistochemistry), combined with a method-or-application block comprising (‘artificial intelligence’ OR ‘deep learning’ OR ‘machine learning’ OR ‘convolutional neural network’ OR ‘foundation model’ OR ‘self-supervised’) OR (‘clinical trial’ OR ‘companion diagnostic’ OR biomarker OR ‘Software as a Medical Device’ OR ‘drug development’ OR toxicolog OR biobank). In PubMed, the strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms (for example, ‘Pathology’ (MeSh), ‘Artificial Intelligence’ (MeSh), ‘Deep Learning’ (MeSh)) with title/abstract free-text terms; in Embase, Emtree equivalents with .ti,ab; and in Web of Science, Topic (TS=) field searches with the same term groups. The full database-specific search strings are provided as Supplementary Material (online supplemental table S1). Filters applied were: publication date 1 January 2019 to 20 April 2026, English language and human studies, except that non-clinical (animal) toxicology studies were retained deliberately for the preclinical-pathology section. Records were exported to a reference manager and de-duplicated. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to the operational, infrastructural or regulatory dimensions of digital and AI pathology across the drug-development pipeline; potentially relevant records were read in full. Reference lists of included articles and of recent reviews were snowballed iteratively to capture sources the database queries missed. Inclusion criteria were: (a) peer-reviewed articles, authoritative regulatory or standards documents, or high-quality preprints addressing digital or AI pathology in translational research, drug or device development, or regulation; and (b) sufficient methodological detail to support the point being cited. Exclusion criteria were: conference abstracts without full text, non-English records, opinion pieces without primary or regulatory grounding, and studies of purely radiological (non-tissue) imaging. Because this is a narrative rather than systematic review, selection favoured representativeness and authority over exhaustive capture; the synthesis is therefore illustrative of the field’s operational and regulatory structure rather than a complete enumeration of every study, and this is a limitation (see Discussion). Priority was given to (a) peer-reviewed articles published within the search window (2019 onward); (b) high-impact reviews and primary studies in Nature Medicine, Nature Cancer, Cancer Cell, Modern Pathology, Journal of Pathology, Annals of Oncology, Lancet Digital Health and equivalent venues; (c) regulatory documents from the US Food and Drug Administration, the European Commission AI Act and Medical Device Regulation and the International Medical Device Regulators Forum; and (d) reporting standards including Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis - Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD-AI),28 Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM),29 Development and Early-stage Clinical Identification and Delegation Evaluation of Artificial Intelligence (DECIDE-AI)30 and Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies – Artificial Intelligence (STARD-AI).31 Sources predating the search window were retained only where they remain foundational (for example, the FDA In Vitro Companion Diagnostic Devices guidance)32 and seminal interobserver-variability studies in prostate pathology.3334 Synthesis was narrative and organised by pipeline stage according to the framework in table 1; findings were grouped by drug-development stage and, within each stage, by the operational or regulatory challenge addressed rather than pooled quantitatively. 78 sources were included in the final synthesis, all of which appear in the reference list. Reporting of evidence syntheses was informed by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) principles where applicable. Construction of the platform comparison (table 2) followed a pre-specified, vendor-agnostic procedure. Platforms were eligible if they were commercially available integrated pathology systems marketed for translational or clinical research that combine at least two of the three core capabilities compared (LIS, IMS, AI orchestration) and had publicly available product documentation. Comparison columns were fixed in advance to these operationally meaningful capabilities. All entries were populated solely from public sources: vendor product documentation, peer-reviewed publications and, for regulatory status, the FDA 510(k), De Novo and premarket approval (PMA) public databases (searched 20 April 2026). No proprietary or non-public information was used. FlexLIS, developed by the authors’ institution (NovinoAI), was assessed against the identical publicly verifiable criteria applied to every comparator. Vendors were not contacted to review or correct the table; because regulatory status changes over time, entries state an as-of date and should be re-confirmed against the FDA device databases at the time of reading.

Digital pathology and AI in translational research › AI-ready tissue biobanks

Tissue biobanks are essential infrastructure for translational research and clinical trials.35 They collect, process, store and distribute biological specimens along with associated clinical and pathology data.36 Traditional biobanks identify tissue by H&E-stained glass slides filed in storage systems; without digitisation, retrieval is slow, cataloguing is inconsistent and ground-truth metadata is sparse. Advances in WSI, software integration and accessible storage have changed how slides are filed, catalogued, stored and retrieved.36 Common practices that make a biobank AI-ready for digital pathology include governance and compliance, pre-analytical control, a quality management system (QMS), traceability, inventory management, structured data linkage and fit-for-purpose services such as standardised slide production, tumour enrichment or microdissection, pathology review workflows and distribution-packaging standards.35 Substantial gaps remain: most biobanks still require investment to deliver consistent image quality, trustworthy ground truth and computable metadata at scale. These gaps are not confined to the biobank stage. The computable metadata, controlled pre-analytics and adjudicated ground truth that define an AI-ready biobank3536 are the same inputs whose absence limits the histomarker, molecular-inference and clinical-trial studies reviewed below, so the biobank determines what downstream work can achieve rather than merely preceding it.

Digital pathology and AI in translational research › Novel histomarkers from morphology

Histomarkers, defined as histopathologic features used in diagnosis, classification, risk stratification and prognostication, have historically been identified by pathologists relying on recognisable morphological patterns. AI-based systems can quantify morphologic features that are difficult to assess reliably by human visual review.37 In prostate pathology, for example, it is important to recognise the cribriform pattern of prostate cancer in needle biopsy, since it is associated with adverse outcomes and is an independent prognostic factor. Interobserver agreement for the cribriform pattern, especially small cribriform foci with fused glands, in prostate cancer needle biopsies is moderate, with kappa values ranging from 0.40 to 0.56 depending on the study and criteria applied.38 Ambrosini and colleagues showed that a deep-learning method to detect cribriform growth patterns in prostate needle biopsies can achieve an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.81 and a sensitivity of 0.9039; of the AI’s false-positive images, pathologists on re-review considered 9% cribriform and 11% possibly cribriform, suggesting that roughly 20% of what the AI ‘incorrectly’ flagged was in fact debatable or correct on a second look. However, although AI models can identify quantitative features that may correlate with diagnosis, prognosis or molecular alterations,40 most such features remain investigational and exploratory; rigorous biological and clinical validation, including prospective external confirmation, is therefore required before these features can be used as clinical histomarkers for clinical trials.1441 Taken together, the cribriform studies illustrate a pattern that recurs throughout this review: the ceiling on demonstrable performance is set less by model capacity than by the reproducibility of the reference standard. Where expert agreement on the feature itself is only moderate,38 a substantial fraction of apparent model error is irreducible label noise, as the re-review of false positives by Ambrosini and colleagues shows,39 and this, rather than algorithmic limitation, is a principal reason such features remain investigational.

Digital pathology and AI in translational research › Molecular and genetic inference from H&E

The integration of histopathology with molecular and genetic data is an active research direction in precision medicine.1112 Deep-learning models applied to WSI have been reported to predict clinically relevant genetic features in breast, prostate, gastrointestinal and lung cancers.1042 Campanella and colleagues demonstrated that an AI model trained on lung-adenocarcinoma WSI (n≈8461) could predict clinically relevant epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations with a mean AUC of 0.89 and could potentially reduce subsequent sequencing in 43% of evaluated samples.43 Multimodal models that integrate histology with spatial genomics enable investigation of the tumour microenvironment, cell composition and spatial relationships; for example, integrative analyses of more than 14 million cells from 10 cancer types and seven spatial-omics platforms identified four conserved spatial cancer-associated fibroblast subtypes with distinct organisational patterns and neighbouring-cell compositions.2344 Two features distinguish the more mature studies in this area from exploratory reports. The first is an orthogonal reference standard, such as sequencing rather than pathologist opinion, which allows performance to be estimated cleanly43; the second is a defined clinical decision, such as triage before sequencing, whose value depends on prevalence and downstream consequences rather than on discrimination alone.1441 The strongest findings are also multimodal, coupling morphology with molecular or spatial data,7234044 which supports treating AI-driven inference from H&E as a complement to, not a replacement for, confirmatory molecular testing.

Digital pathology and AI in translational research › Quantitative morphometry and spatial biology

WSI and AI together enable quantitative morphologic assessment that is measurable, reproducible and amenable to downstream statistical analysis.45 Quantitative measurements support assessment of cell counts (for example, tumour cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes), mitotic activity, necrosis, immunostain expression (for example, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1 (PD-L1)) and tissue segmentation.646 Cancer spatial biology, the study of the location-specific organisation of cells and molecules within tumours, has been advanced by methods that combine H&E with multiplex immunostaining and in-situ molecular profiling.67 AI is used both to analyse individual stains and to integrate morphology with omics data through multimodal fusion, supporting predictive algorithms for treatment response.1123

Digital pathology and AI in translational research › Morphology-driven drug discovery

In phenotypic (morphology-driven) drug discovery, the process begins by observing morphologic changes in disease models or patients before the molecular target is known.47 This approach is particularly useful for complex or poorly understood diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis and certain psychiatric disorders.47 High-throughput imaging of 3D cells, organoids and tissue sections combined with AI-based morphologic analysis allows large-scale phenotypic screens with reclassification of biological responses by computational features. Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development

Digital pathology with image analysis can provide accuracy, reproducibility and standardisation of pathology-based trial entry criteria and endpoints, while enabling new features that go beyond what is practical with manual review.2026 In oncology clinical trials specifically, AI in pathology may accelerate patient selection, improve endpoint assessment, enable deeper biomarker discovery and standardise central review across sites.202526

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Preclinical pathology and toxicology

In preclinical animal studies, digital pathology and AI are being applied to lesion detection, severity grading and tissue classification, streamlining workflows that are otherwise time-consuming and subjective.48 These tools support consistent interpretation of histological slides and automate quantitative assessments relevant to drug development and regulatory submissions. The FDA’s PathologAI Initiative is a notable example, using AI to support consistent and scalable pathology assessment in regulatory toxicology by identifying lesion types and anatomical sites, grading severity and supporting analysis across organs and species.4849 The PathologAI system was developed using weakly supervised classification of rat-liver WSIs without pixel-level lesion annotation, drawing on the Open Toxicogenomics Project-Genomics Assisted Toxicity Evaluation System (TG-GATEs) dataset (170 compounds at three dose levels and four time points). Classification accuracy on the holdout test set was reported as 87% among 87 control slides free of findings and 83% among 120 controls with spontaneous necrosis, with lower accuracy on treated animals with minimal or slight necrosis (59% to 67%).48 Such results illustrate both the promise and the limits of weakly supervised approaches: detection of frank lesions is achievable, while subtler treatment-related findings remain difficult and motivate richer ground truth. The same pattern, reliable performance on unambiguous findings and degradation at the diagnostic margin, recurs in the clinical studies discussed below and points to adjudicated reference standards, more than larger models, as the route to further gains.4146

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Patient screening and enrolment

Efficient patient screening and enrolment remain critical determinants of clinical-trial success, particularly in precision oncology where eligibility depends on histologic subtype, biomarker expression and molecular alterations.50 Traditional enrolment workflows rely on manual pathology-report review, electronic health record (EHR) interrogation and sequential molecular testing, with prolonged timelines, high labour costs and frequent under-enrolment. AI-enabled digital pathology offers a scalable alternative by enabling automated, image-based prescreening directly from WSI.1050 Such capabilities support a two-stage paradigm, comprising AI-based morphological triage followed by targeted confirmatory molecular testing, that may reduce unnecessary sequencing, enrich biomarker-positive cohorts and expand the effective screening pool. AI-based quantitative assessment of biomarkers such as PD-L1 or HER2 may additionally reduce interobserver variability in eligibility-defining criteria. AI-assisted screening must nevertheless be deployed within validated workflows with explicit handling of false positives, prevalence-dependent predictive values and cross-site model performance.1441

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Endpoints and quantitative readouts

Quantitative readouts derived from digitised pathology images via AI provide objective tissue measurements suitable for clinical-trial endpoints.20 Endpoints in trials may be clinical (for example, survival) or biomarker/surrogate (for example, a tissue marker change linked to disease progression). Common AI-derived quantitative readouts include cell counts (lymphocytes, apoptotic cells), area measurements (tumour vs stroma, necrosis, fibrosis), morphometric features (nuclear index, mitotic index), spatial statistics (tumour cells vs lymphocytes) and risk scores for stratification (recurrence risk, response to treatment).645

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Treatment response, resistance and mechanism of action

AI has been applied to predict individualised treatment benefit across multiple solid tumours. In breast cancer, AI-based pathology models incorporating digitised histology, image-derived omics and clinical covariates have been developed to predict pathologic complete response and other measures of neoadjuvant treatment efficacy.42 In hepatocellular carcinoma, AI has been applied to diagnosis, risk stratification, and prediction of response to transarterial chemoembolisation, surgical resection, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and post-transplant management.51 In prostate cancer, multimodal AI models have been developed and validated using large phase III trial cohorts to identify subgroups that derive differential benefit from androgen-deprivation therapy combined with radiotherapy, with biomarker-positive patients showing reduced metastasis risk on therapy and biomarker-negative patients showing no measurable benefit.375253 These examples share a design feature that separates them from most published models, which remain retrospective and single-institution: validation on multi-institutional cohorts against predefined clinical endpoints.5253 The same feature characterises the real-world EGFR deployment described earlier43 and the multi-site evidence underlying the first De Novo authorisation in pathology.54 It is this validation design, rather than algorithmic novelty, that the reporting standards described in the Methods call for283031 and that currently distinguishes regulator-grade tools from promising models.

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Central pathology review

Central pathology review reduces subjective interpretation and standardises assessment of treatment responses.2055 Pathology slide review across multiple institutions exhibits substantial variability; an early sarcoma-trial study reported diagnostic discordance between local and central pathology as high as 45% in that specific setting,56 although this figure is disease-specific and era-specific and should not be generalised. Interobserver studies in prostate pathology have reported moderate-to-substantial agreement for Gleason scoring (weighted kappa 0.56 to 0.70 across pathologists), with consensus (at least 70% agreement) on Gleason score reached in only 38 of 46 cases33; only 67% of cribriform Gleason pattern-4 images reached consensus among 10 genitourinary pathology experts.34 AI-assisted review can reduce such variability: in a multi-reader study, AI-assisted Gleason grading among 16 readers (including three residents) increased the quadratically weighted Cohen’s kappa from 0.799 (unaided) to 0.872 (AI-assisted), with the largest gains for less-experienced readers.57 Large multi-institutional benchmark efforts such as the Prostate cANcer graDe Assessment (PANDA) Challenge confirm that AI-assisted grading can approach expert performance while improving consistency across observers.58 Read together with the interobserver studies above, these results indicate that the benefit of AI most consistently demonstrated in the trial setting is reproducibility rather than accuracy: the historical problem being addressed is discordance between readers3334 and between sites,56 and the gains are largest precisely where reader variance is greatest.5758 The same logic underlies the case for AI in enrolment criteria and endpoint assessment2050 and in quantitative biomarker scoring for therapy selection.26 Cost-effective central review with AI requires the same governance, such as version control, audit trails and validated thresholds, as any regulator-grade system.2155

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › AI-assisted contract research organisations

CROs provide the operational backbone of clinical development, overseeing protocol design, site management, data monitoring and regulatory submission. Conventional CRO workflows are constrained by fragmented data systems and manual oversight, challenges that are amplified in biomarker-driven oncology and rare-disease trials.59 Integration of AI and machine learning is reshaping the CRO role from operational execution toward predictive coordination: by combining historical trial data with real-world evidence, AI models can forecast enrolment trajectories, optimise inclusion criteria and identify high-performing sites, with potential reductions in timeline and financial risk.60 Natural language processing accelerates patient identification by extracting eligibility variables from unstructured clinical documentation, bridging EHR data and trial protocols. In immuno-oncology, AI-assisted spatial analytics quantify cellular interactions within the tumour microenvironment, providing mechanistic insights that single-marker immunohistochemistry alone does not.62346 AI also underpins decentralised clinical trials by enabling remote monitoring and risk-based oversight aligned with regulatory frameworks for clinical-investigation conduct.6162 For CROs, digital and AI pathology promise high throughput, standardisation, expanded service portfolios and a strategic shift from service provider toward co-development partner.

Digital pathology and AI in drug and device development › Medical devices, IVDs, SaMD and companion diagnostics

Digital pathology and AI are increasingly integrated into medical devices to support diagnosis, monitoring and treatment decisions. The FDA classifies such devices as IVDs, SaMD, AI-enabled SaMD (AI-SaMD) or CDx.6364 Examples include Paige Prostate, which received FDA Breakthrough Device designation and subsequently, in 2021, De Novo marketing authorisation (the first FDA-authorised AI-based product in pathology) as an aid to the identification of cancer in prostate needle-biopsy WSIs54; Proscia Concentriq AP-Dx, a 510(k)-cleared SaMD for viewing, interpretation and management of WSI in clinical pathology laboratories; and the Ventana TROP2 RxDx Device, an AI-driven CDx granted FDA Breakthrough Device designation for assessment of TROP2 (Trophoblast Cell Surface Antigen 2) expression in non-small-cell lung cancer to identify candidates for datopotamab deruxtecan therapy.65 Quantitative AI-assisted scoring of biomarker expression (HER2, PD-L1) has been described as more reproducible than manual assessment, supporting standardised therapy selection.26 A schematic of these clinical-trial and device-development applications appears in figure 2.

Operational and regulatory infrastructure › Integrated LIS/IMS/AI ecosystems

Integration of LIS, IMS and AI is essential to ensure data integrity, traceability, scalability, regulatory compliance and accurate biomarker-driven decisions.20 LIS supports specimen acquisition and tracking, test ordering, processing and analysis, and report creation and distribution. In translational and clinical research, LIS supports sample tracking across tissue banks (frozen tissue, blocks, stained and unstained slides, blood, nucleic-acid extracts) with full chain of custody, audit trails for accessioning and result entry, automated and queryable reports, and interfaces to IMS for slide management, electronic data capture (EDC), clinical-trial management systems (CTMS) and biorepository/genomic data warehouses. LIS must meet regulatory requirements including GLP, GCLP and HIPAA. IMS organises, stores and transmits images generated from scanners; it must catalogue WSI by tissue type and stain, track image versions, analysis parameters and user modifications, and version-lock analysis tools that contribute to clinical decision making. Useful additional functions include annotation, region-of-interest measurement and scalability to manage millions of images at terabyte file sizes.221 AI plays an increasing role in translational and clinical research815 and is ideally integrated with LIS and IMS so that data and images can be accessed directly for inference. Operational and regulatory infrastructure › Quality assurance and validation for routine deployment

Beyond governance and regulatory oversight, routine deployment of digital and AI pathology depends on day-to-day quality assurance (QA) that is often underemphasised relative to algorithm development. Before a laboratory can rely on digital slides for diagnosis, the digital workflow itself must be validated: the College of American Pathologists guideline, updated in 2022, recommends validating whole-slide imaging systems against the intended clinical use, using a sample of at least 60 cases spanning the range of specimens encountered, comparing digital with glass-slide diagnoses by the same pathologist after a washout period of at least 2 weeks, and revalidating whenever a significant component of the system changes.3 Validation is therefore workflow-specific rather than a one-time event. Image quality is the foundation of any downstream measurement, and AI models are sensitive to acquisition artefacts that human readers subconsciously discount. Practical QA components include scanner acceptance testing and periodic calibration; per-slide image-quality assessment for focus, colour fidelity and completeness of tissue capture; automated detection of common artefacts such as out-of-focus regions, tissue folds, pen marks, air bubbles and missing tissue; and defined rescanning workflows that route failed slides back for re-imaging before they reach an algorithm or a pathologist.2166 Automated artefact and quality gates are especially important at scale, where manual review of every gigapixel image is infeasible, and they protect AI outputs from the domain shift that degraded input introduces.1467 Once a system is in production, ongoing quality monitoring (tracking scan-failure and rescan rates, monitoring AI output distributions for drift, auditing a sample of AI-assisted cases and revalidating after scanner, stain or software changes) is necessary to sustain regulator-grade performance.368 These operational QA measures complement, and are a precondition for, the regulatory and technical infrastructure discussed above.

Operational and regulatory infrastructure › FDA regulation of digital-image systems

Precise terminology matters when describing device status, and four concepts are distinct. 510(k) clearance is a marketing authorisation granted when a device is shown to be substantially equivalent to a legally marketed predicate. PMA is the most stringent pathway, reserved for high-risk (Class III) devices and requiring independent evidence of safety and effectiveness. De Novo authorisation is a marketing authorisation for a novel low-to-moderate-risk device that has no valid predicate, and it establishes a new device classification. Breakthrough Device designation, by contrast, is not a marketing authorisation at all: it is an expedited-review status intended to speed development and review of devices addressing serious conditions. A device may hold a Breakthrough designation and later be cleared, approved or De Novo authorised; the two should not be conflated. We use these terms consistently throughout this review. Digital-pathology systems and image-analysis tools may be subject to FDA regulatory controls when used as medical devices or as diagnostic tools that support trial endpoints, patient stratification, or safety and efficacy decisions.69 The FDA regulates WSI systems intended for primary diagnosis as Class II medical devices subject to special controls (21 CFR 864.3700), a classification established through the 2017 De Novo authorisation of the first such system. Image-management software may be exempt as Class I if used only for image acquisition, storage, transmission and display; it is regulated as SaMD when used for primary or secondary diagnostic interpretation, quantitative analysis (for example, PD-L1, HER2 scoring), AI-based classification or any analysis that affects patient inclusion or trial outcomes. Regulatory pathways for IMS include 510(k), De Novo or PMA.6364

Operational and regulatory infrastructure › FDA regulation of AI/ML in pathology

The FDA is actively developing a regulatory framework for AI and machine learning in clinical research, including draft guidance on AI use to generate data that support regulatory decision making, expectations for AI-enabled SaMD and risk-based credibility frameworks for AI tools used in clinical trials.6369 The FDA’s Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) framework for adaptive AI is intended to allow pre-specified, controlled post-market updates of machine-learning-enabled device software functions while preserving safety and effectiveness; PCCP submissions must include a description of modifications, a modification protocol and an impact assessment.70 International divergence is real: the EU AI Act introduces a parallel high-risk classification for medical-device AI with conformity-assessment obligations beyond those imposed by the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR),7172 and the International Medical Device Regulators Forum SaMD framework provides a risk-categorisation scaffold harmonising terminology across jurisdictions.73 Sponsors developing pathology AI for global use must plan for multiple, evolving regulatory regimes.

Operational and regulatory infrastructure › Companion diagnostics

A companion diagnostic identifies patients most likely to benefit from a particular therapy, or at increased risk of serious side effects, or monitors response.32 CDx tests are regulated as Class III medical devices, generally requiring a PMA application; co-approval of the CDx and drug or device is encouraged when the test is essential to determine clinical benefit.32 Examples include the PathVysion HER2 DNA Probe Kit (Abbott Molecular) for trastuzumab, the cobas EGFR mutation test v2 (Roche Molecular) for osimertinib, and the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 PharmDx assay (Agilent/Dako) for pembrolizumab. Quantitative AI-assisted scoring of HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2) and PD-L1 (Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1) expression has been described as a route to greater accuracy and efficiency than manual assessment, with implications for biomarker reproducibility and therapy selection.26 An overview of integrated LIS, IMS and AI considerations in translational research and clinical trials is shown in figure 3.

Discussion

The optimistic trajectory of digital and AI pathology must be balanced against substantive technical, operational, regulatory and ethical bottlenecks. We summarise the most consequential below.

Discussion › Scope and recurring themes across the pipeline

This review spans the full drug-development pipeline, from biobank to companion diagnostic, and that breadth is deliberate: our thesis is that the operational and regulatory connective tissue between these stages, rather than any single application, is what gates translation, and this argument cannot be made from within one stage alone. To keep the breadth disciplined rather than encyclopaedic, we organise the synthesis around a single logic, namely the challenge each stage presents mapped to the solution, infrastructure and regulatory framework that address it (table 1), and we map each use case to its specific regulatory pathway rather than treating regulation as a single undifferentiated topic. Preclinical toxicology sits under GLP and non-clinical FDA review; trial-embedded use under Good Clinical Practice (GCP) and GCLP; diagnostic deployment under 510(k), De Novo, or PMA together with CLIA and CAP; and companion diagnostics under PMA with drug-device co-approval. We acknowledge the trade-off this breadth imposes: as a narrative review, the synthesis is illustrative and authority-weighted rather than an exhaustive systematic enumeration, individual applications are treated more briefly than a single-topic review would allow, and selection, despite the pre-specified search and criteria in the Methods, retains a degree of subjectivity. We judge the cross-stage perspective to be the review’s principal contribution and the reason it complements, rather than duplicates, the many excellent stage-specific and method-specific reviews already available. Reading the included sources as a body also reveals a trajectory. Work published from 2019 to 2021 established feasibility with task-specific convolutional networks, showing that narrow diagnostic tasks and even molecular status could be inferred from H&E,581054 while documenting the fragility of those models to stain, scanner and site.131474 Work from roughly 2022 to 2024 responded with self-supervised foundation models that reduce annotation dependence and improve robustness15–19 and with reporting frameworks that formalised expectations for external validation.28–31 The most recent literature is characterised by real-world deployment and phase III-anchored validation435253 and by regulatory instruments, notably the PCCP framework and the EU AI Act, that address how validated models are maintained over time.7071 The centre of gravity has thus shifted from whether AI can recover histologic signal, which is no longer seriously disputed, to whether it generalises and under what governance it can be trusted. On several questions, the sources converge. Every study or review that addresses generalisation concludes that site-specific signatures degrade performance and that stain normalisation, augmentation and foundation-model pretraining mitigate but do not eliminate the effect1314161874; none claims the problem is solved. There is similar agreement that the defensible near-term role for AI is assistive rather than autonomous, reflected both in reader studies showing the largest benefit for less experienced pathologists57 and in the authorisation of the first FDA-authorised pathology AI as an aid to, rather than a replacement for, pathologist interpretation.54 Tension persists on interpretability, where some authors treat attention and saliency maps as adequate for clinical trust23 while others, and increasingly regulators, regard them as descriptive rather than explanatory and look instead to external validation or mechanistic plausibility,697075 and on the pace of adoption, where technical reviews project rapid clinical integration152425 but implementation studies and the Delphi forecast make routine use by 2030 contingent on validation, regulation and reimbursement rather than on further algorithmic advances.2176 An implication for the bottlenecks discussed below is that they are not independent: ground-truth uncertainty, domain shift and uneven reporting are three expressions of one deficit, the absence of governed, multi-institutional, well-annotated data infrastructure, which is why this review places the operational ecosystem rather than model architecture at its centre. A second is that the evidentiary properties requested by reporting standards28–31 and required by regulators636970 are now substantially the same, so a study designed to satisfy TRIPOD-AI or DECIDE-AI is largely a study designed for regulatory submission.

Discussion › Algorithm generalisability and domain shift

Deep-learning models trained on one institution’s scanners, staining protocols and demographic mix frequently lose accuracy when deployed elsewhere.131474 Howard and colleagues demonstrated that institutional origin is recoverable from H&E pixel statistics alone, biasing apparent biomarker associations and inflating reported accuracies.14 Stain normalisation, data augmentation and foundation-model pretraining mitigate but do not eliminate the gap.131618 Robust deployment requires multi-site training data and prospective external validation.41

Discussion › Model interpretability

Attention heatmaps and saliency maps illustrate where a model attended but do not explain why a feature is causally informative.2375 For regulatory submissions, the FDA increasingly expects mechanistic plausibility or strong external validation in lieu of mechanism.6970 Concept-bottleneck models and pathologist-collaborative explanation interfaces are emerging research directions but remain immature for routine clinical use.

Discussion › Dependence on annotated data and ground truth

Pixel-level annotation by trained pathologists is the dominant bottleneck in supervised pipelines. Recent advances in weakly supervised multiple-instance learning, self-supervised learning and foundation-model pretraining16–19 reduce but do not eliminate the requirement. Ground-truth disagreement among pathologists for nuanced histologic decisions (for example, cribriform Gleason pattern, Ki-67 hotspot scoring) caps achievable model accuracy.333457

Discussion › Algorithmic bias and fairness

Models can systematically underperform for demographic groups, scanner types and clinical contexts under-represented in training data.1475 Bias auditing is not yet standard practice in pathology AI development. Equitable performance across age, ancestry and care setting should be reported explicitly with disaggregated metrics.

Discussion › External validation and reporting standards

Many published pathology-AI models report internal cross-validation only. Reporting frameworks for AI in medical research, including TRIPOD-AI,28 CLAIM,29 DECIDE-AI30 and STARD-AI,31 call for prospective external validation, multi-site cohorts and predefined performance thresholds. Compliance remains uneven in the pathology AI literature. We recommend that translational and clinical pathology AI studies report compliance against the applicable framework at submission.

Discussion › Regulatory uncertainty and international divergence

A modest number of AI-enabled digital-pathology devices have achieved FDA marketing authorisation (510(k) clearance or De Novo authorisation) to date, against several hundred AI-enabled medical-imaging devices overall.546364 The 510(k), De Novo and PMA pathways accommodate digital pathology, but the FDA’s Predetermined Change Control Plan framework for adaptive AI is still maturing.70 The EU AI Act71 introduces parallel high-risk classification for medical-device AI, with conformity-assessment obligations layered on top of the EU MDR.72 The International Medical Device Regulators Forum SaMD framework provides terminology harmonisation but does not by itself resolve jurisdictional divergence.73 International divergence is a real cost and time burden for sponsors planning global deployment.

Discussion › Data governance, privacy and security

Whole-slide images are personal health information under HIPAA when linked to patient identifiers and are subject to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU. Federated learning, differential-privacy training and secure-enclave deployment are active research directions but operationally immature for routine pathology-AI development. Sponsors should anticipate substantial data-governance work, including consent provenance for archival specimens, de-identification audits and cross-border data-transfer agreements, in any multi-site deployment.

Discussion › Cost, infrastructure and reimbursement

Scanner capital cost (typically $50 000 to $250 000 per device), storage at terabyte-scale per laboratory per year, network upgrades, integration with LIS/EHR/CTMS and IT (Information Technology) support add up to capital and operating expenses that many community pathology practices cannot internalise.2176 Current Procedural Terminology codes for AI-augmented pathology services are nascent, and reimbursement parity with AI-augmented radiology remains incomplete. Cost-effectiveness evaluation in real-world deployment is a priority for the next round of evidence development.

Discussion › Real-world adoption gap

Despite a decade of academic progress, deployment in routine clinical pathology remains in the low single-digit percentage range in US laboratories by most surveys.76 Integration with diagnostic workflow (rather than parallel research workflow) is uncommon, and adoption is uneven between academic, community and CRO settings. A 2023 Delphi forecast suggested that broad routine use of computational pathology by 2030 will require coordinated progress on validation, regulation and reimbursement.76

Discussion › Professional considerations

Pathologist labour effects, informed consent for AI use on archival specimens, equity in access to AI-augmented diagnostic services and accountability when an AI-derived recommendation is incorrect all require active governance.6277 Professional society guidance (College of American Pathologists, USCAP, Royal College of Pathologists) is evolving but does not yet fully address questions of AI-assisted versus AI-autonomous decision making in routine pathology practice.

Conclusion

The integration of LIS, IMS and AI is reshaping translational research, drug discovery and clinical trials by enhancing data integrity, traceability and regulatory compliance. Platforms that unify LIS, IMS and AI can streamline laboratory workflows, support multimodal analyses and align with evolving regulatory requirements.202126 The changing regulatory landscape, including updated FDA guidance on digital imaging, AI/ML applications and companion diagnostics, alongside the European Union (EU) AI Act and EU MDR, underscores the need for validated, secure and interoperable solutions.69–72 Evidence to date suggests that these technologies may contribute to faster patient stratification, more reproducible endpoint adjudication and earlier go or no-go decisions in drug development, but no part of the pipeline is yet fully automatable, and every meaningful AI claim must be calibrated to the fidelity of its training data, ground truth and external validation.14282941 Realising the potential of AI-enabled digital pathology will require multi-institutional validation against reporting standards, harmonisation of cross-jurisdictional regulatory expectations, cost-effectiveness evidence sufficient to justify reimbursement parity with AI-augmented radiology, and deliberate attention to equity between academic, community and CRO settings. The operational and regulatory backbone described here is not optional. Without governed biobanks, validated central-review pipelines, transparent comparison of platforms and explicit treatment of limitations, AI in pathology will continue to advance academically while struggling to reach patients at scale. With that backbone in place, the same technologies have a credible path to changing how drugs are developed, how clinical trials are run and how diagnostic decisions are made.
