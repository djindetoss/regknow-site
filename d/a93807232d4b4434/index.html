Abstract

Neutralizing antibody (NAb) activity assessment is a regulatory expectation in the clinical development of therapeutic proteins, traditionally addressed through standalone NAb assays. However, evolving regulatory guidance and industry experience increasingly recognize that integrated analyses of anti-drug antibodies (ADA), pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), efficacy, and safety may provide a more clinically meaningful evaluation of neutralizing activity. This perspective examines the scientific and regulatory rationale for moving beyond routine reliance on standalone NAb assays and describes a risk-based, integrated immunogenicity assessment framework. Two case studies involving low-risk monoclonal antibodies-garadacimab and clazakizumab-are presented, in which validated standalone NAb assays were available but not relied upon for primary clinical interpretation due to limited sensitivity and lack of added clinical value. Instead, longitudinal ADA characterization integrated with PK and PD or efficacy data was used to assess clinically meaningful neutralizing activity. In both cases, this approach enabled clear differentiation between detectable but clinically irrelevant immunogenicity and immunogenicity with clinical consequence and was accepted by regulatory authorities. These examples illustrate how integrated immunogenicity assessments can replace standalone NAb assays while remaining scientifically rigorous, clinically informative, and aligned with regulatory expectations.

Introduction

Immunogenicity remains a central consideration in the clinical development of therapeutic proteins, with the potential to influence pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), efficacy, and patient safety. Consequently, immunogenicity strategies should be developed based on the specific safety risks attributable to immunogenicity and on identifying the most appropriate methods to fully characterize any resulting clinical impact. Regulatory guidance from both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) emphasizes that immunogenicity assessment should extend beyond descriptive anti-drug antibody (ADA) incidence to also include neutralizing antibody (NAb) activity reporting because the impact of ADA on pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, safety and efficacy may correlate with NAb activity rather than ADA incidence [1,2]. Historically, NAbs have been viewed as a critical subset of ADA due to their specific potential to directly inhibit the biological activity of the therapeutic and, in certain contexts, the endogenous counterpart of the drug target [3,4]. For products where neutralization poses a meaningful safety or efficacy risk—such as biological mimics or therapies with nonredundant physiological functions—early assessment of neutralizing activity using a standalone NAb assay or PD assay that can directly reflect neutralization of in vivo drug activity might be warranted to derisk clinical development and inform decision making across all phases [5,6]. It should also be noted that, in specific circumstances, standalone NAb assays go beyond the typically bioanalytical definition and serve a clinically defined context, with a clear and justified example being the routine use of NAb assays to detect the presence of inhibitors in bleeding disorders to support patient and physician decision-making [7–9]. Both FDA and EMA guidelines support early, risk-based planning of immunogenicity testing strategies, including consideration of an appropriate NAb monitoring strategy when neutralization is expected to lead to clinically significant consequences [1,2]. Regulatory and industry guidances have evolved to acknowledge that standalone NAb assays are not always the most informative or necessary means to characterize neutralizing activity and assess clinical impact [6,10]. The FDA notes that, “in selected cases, highly sensitive PD markers, appropriately designed PK assays, or a combination of both may be used in lieu of a stand-alone NAb assay, provided these assays reliably inform clinical activity and the approach is discussed with the Agency” [1]. Similarly, the EMA guideline on immunogenicity assessment emphasizes that the primary objective of immunogenicity investigations is to understand clinical consequences of ADAs rather than to generate immunogenicity metrics in isolation [2]. To address regulatory expectations many NAb assays deployed in clinical development are, in practice, reformatted versions of existing assays utilized for potency, PK, or PD readouts already implemented in the program. Cell-based or ligand-binding standalone NAb assays are highly contrived assays designed to mirror the drug’s mechanism of action, effectively recapitulating key elements of bioactivity or target‑engagement assays that are also interrogated by PD biomarkers or functional PK approaches [6,11]. This overlap raises important questions regarding the incremental value of standalone NAb assays, particularly when no safety concerns that require early and active clinical monitoring have been raised and comprehensive ADA, PK, and PD data sets are readily available and are demonstrably sensitive to clinically relevant impact of neutralizing activity. Recent publications have formalized this concept through the proposal and evaluation of alternative clinical assessments to evaluate potential neutralizing activity measurements, such as PK, PD and efficacy. In a recent publication, the authors evaluated historical and contemporary clinical datasets and demonstrated that ADA magnitude and persistence are often highly correlated with neutralizing activity, and that clinically meaningful immunogenicity is more reliably identified through integrated analysis of ADA status, PK alterations, PD effects, and clinical outcomes than through binary NAb assay results alone [6]. The argument is that for molecules with low anticipated immunogenicity and low safety risk attributable to drug neutralization, a stand-alone NAb assay may not enhance data interpretation, whereas for higher-risk products with safety events of interest that are specific to NAb, targeted use of NAb assays remains appropriate. This risk-based, data-integration approach is not in contrast to regulatory expectations and emerging agency experience emphasizing clinical consequence and integrated analyses, which leverages the totality of data available to comprehensively characterize the clinical impact of immunogenicity [2,6,12]. Despite growing scientific and regulatory consensus on the value of utilizing integrated ADA, PK, and PD data sets to characterize clinically relevant NAb activity in lieu of a standalone NAb assay, implementation and alignment remains uneven across the industry. Success depends on several key factors. These include thoughtfully designing and deploying ADA, PK, and PD assays early in clinical development to characterize clinical impact of ADA, internal cross-functional collaboration and alignment among bioanalytical, clinical pharmacology, safety, regulatory, and clinical teams, a scientifically driven communication strategy, clinical trial designs with appropriate sampling and testing strategies, and development of integrated data analysis and reporting approaches (Table 1, references [13,14]). When executed effectively, immunogenicity assessment becomes a scientific narrative rather than a single numerical outcome, integrating multiple data sets to support informed benefit–risk decisions for patients, regulators, and prescribers [5,15]. The goal of this publication is to provide examples of real-world outcomes that have successfully applied the integrated data approach to assess neutralizing activity of ADA in a value-added approach. Here we present two case studies involving low-risk monoclonal antibody therapeutics in which a traditional stand-alone NAb assay was available yet deliberately not relied upon as the primary tool for clinical immunogenicity interpretation. Instead, we applied an integrated, risk-based strategy leveraging longitudinal ADA characterization in conjunction with PK and PD data to identify clinically meaningful immunogenic responses [16]. In both cases, this approach enabled clear differentiation between immunogenicity that was merely detectable and immunogenicity that was clinically relevant [17]. This approach has direct implications for drug exposure, pharmacological activity, and patient benefit–risk. Importantly, the decision to forego routine NAb testing was made despite assay readiness, underscoring a science-driven approach rather than a procedural or “tick-box” exercise [18]. Together, these case studies illustrate how integrated PK/PD-based assessment can replace standalone NAb testing while remaining aligned with regulatory expectations on characterizing neutralizing activity with clinical consequence. Case studies › Case study 1: garadacimab

Garadacimab is a fully human IgG4 monoclonal antibody developed to prevent attacks in patients with hereditary angioedema (HAE) [19]. The molecule has an antagonistic mechanism of action, binding to and inhibiting the activity of soluble activated coagulation factor XII (FXIIa), thereby reducing downstream bradykinin generation. The data from the Phase 3 (Ph3) pivotal clinical study is described elsewhere [20]. Case studies › Case study 1: garadacimab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

To determine the likelihood of developing clinically relevant ADAs, the product attributes of garadacimab and the HAE patient-related factors were evaluated. Garadacimab is an antagonistic fully human monoclonal antibody with no evident structural or formulation attributes expected to increase immunogenicity risk compared with other licensed therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). In addition, the indicated HAE patient population is not associated with known risk factors that would predispose patients to heightened immune responses. No ADA- or NAb-specific safety concerns were identified based on the mechanism of action or target biology.

Case studies › Case study 1: garadacimab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

Based on the data included in the immunogenicity risk assessment, it was determined that if a robust ADA response with neutralizing activity developed, the potential clinical consequence would be an impact on drug exposure, PD activity, and/or therapeutic effect, rather than acute immune-mediated safety events. Taken together, the overall immunogenicity risk of garadacimab was classified as low. The PK and PD assays deployed in the clinical studies were sensitive to NAb activity by design and would inform on in vivo neutralization of the drug.

Case studies › Case study 1: garadacimab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

The development of anti-garadacimab antibodies was closely monitored throughout all phases of clinical development, in healthy subjects in phase 1 studies as well as in subjects with HAE in phase 2 and phase 3 studies. As described in the label for garadacimab, Ctrough was 8.09 to 8.69 μg/mL, and therefore it would be anticipated that only high‑magnitude ADA would result in a clinically significant impact. In accordance with regulatory guidances, ADAs were detected using a highly sensitive and drug-tolerant bridging ADA assay (sensitivity of ≤50 ng/mL and drug tolerance of 50 ng/mL of ADA detected in the presence of 20 μg/mL garadacimab [400:1 drug:ADA molar ratio]). This assay had the capacity to detect both clinically relevant as well as low-level, clinically irrelevant ADA responses. In addition, investigators were instructed to monitor all subjects for any clinical signs of immunogenicity (e.g., hypersensitivity reactions including hives, generalized urticaria, tightness of the chest, wheezing, hypotension, and anaphylaxis).

Case studies › Case study 1: garadacimab › Immunogenicity data across clinical development

The cumulative incidence of treatment-emergent anti-garadacimab antibodies in subjects with HAE was very low (2.9%) with only 5 treatment-emergent ADA-positive subjects identified. In addition to the low incidence of treatment-emergent immunogenicity, the majority of detectable ADA responses were of low magnitude (expressed as titer or signal‑to-noise ratio [S/N]) and typically had non-titratable ADA responses (i.e., minimum titer value of 10). In reviewing the S/N ADA data from the garadacimab clinical studies, it was observed that the majority of ADA-positive samples had signal values that were detected near the screening cut point (CP). Due to the inability to titer ADA-positive samples effectively when the resulting assay signal is close to the CP, the titration tier provided limited information on ADA magnitude in this scenario. Instead, the normalized signal (i.e., [S/N]) values of ADA samples tested in the screening assay provided superior assessment of the magnitude of an ADA response [16]. The utility of assessing ADA magnitude using S/N in lieu of titer has been demonstrated by an industry consortium that demonstrated the applicability of using S/N in 15 independent drug‑development programs [6]. A S/N evaluation of ADA magnitude has the added benefit of being able to monitor ADA magnitude using the S/N responses of the entire time profile of a subject regardless of ADA status to better understand the trajectory of the ADA response. With titration assays, only the ADA-positive samples are titrated, providing a sparser assessment of immunoreactivity in the ADA assay. To ensure a holistic review of immunogenicity data, the complete time profile (i.e., not just ADA-positive samples) of the S/N results from all ADA screening assessments of the 5 subjects with treatment-emergent ADA-positive samples were evaluated and are graphically presented in Figure 1(A). In addition, the S/N of each positive control (PC) level is presented, which provides an assessment of the relative sensitivity of ADA responses detected based on anti-garadacimab surrogate PC antibody. It is clear that 4 out of 5 treatment‑emergent ADA-positive subjects had an ADA magnitude lower than the ultra-low PC (50 ng/mL); this is lower than the expected 100 ng/mL relative sensitivity for ADA assays based on the 2019 FDA immunogenicity assay guidance [1]. The S/N responses were so low that the responses were not titratable. Thus, in the context of the garadacimab exposure levels, the low‑magnitude ADA responses observed in the garadacimab clinical development program are expected to be clinically irrelevant and have no impact on safety, PK, PD, or efficacy. Case studies › Case study 2: clazakizumab

Clazakizumab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to and neutralizes IL6, thereby modulating downstream inflammatory signaling. At the time of submission of this publication clazakizumab was in Phase 2b/3 clinical development for end‑stage kidney disease (ESKD), ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT05485961 with the Phase 2b (Ph2b) data presented elsewhere [21].

Case studies › Case study 2: clazakizumab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

The immunogenicity risk assessment evaluated both product and patient-related risk factors. Clazakizumab is an antagonistic monoclonal antibody with no known structural, formulation, or manufacturing attributes associated with increased immunogenicity risk relative to other therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. There were no patient related factors identified that would be expected to increase the likelihood of treatment-emergent immune responses specifically directed against clazakizumab. No ADA- or NAb-specific safety concerns were identified based on the mechanism of action or target biology.

Case studies › Case study 2: clazakizumab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

Based on the data included in the immunogenicity risk assessment, it was determined that if a robust ADA response with neutralizing activity developed, the potential clinical consequence would be an impact on drug exposure, pharmacodynamic activity, and/or therapeutic effect, rather than acute immune-mediated safety events. Moreover, the PK and PD assay deployed for the clinical studies were sensitive to NAb activity by design and would inform on in vivo neutralization of the drug. Clazakizumab was classified as a low-risk molecule.

Case studies › Case study 2: clazakizumab › Immunogenicity risk assessment

Immunogenicity was evaluated at up to four different timepoints throughout the study. Subjects had assessments at Week 0 (pre-dose), 4, 12 and at end of treatment (EOT) visits. ADAs were assessed in samples using a tiered approach (screening, confirmation and titration) with assays validated in line with regulatory guidances [1,2]. The validated ADA screening assay has a sensitivity of 2.5 ng/mL which was 40-fold lower than that recommended by the regulatory guidelines of 100 ng/mL [1]. The assay was determined to be appropriate for the expected trough drug concentration at the time of ADA sampling whereby, at 100 ng/mL of ADA, the assay could tolerate 10 μg/mL of drug. This highly sensitive assay can detect both clinically relevant and irrelevant ADA responses.

Case studies › Case study 2: clazakizumab › Immunogenicity data from the phase 2b study

Comprehensive review of the Phase 2b dataset showed overall low-magnitude ADA responses. Due to the low titer values in most ADA-positive samples, the titration data provided limited information on magnitude of ADA in this scenario. Therefore, the S/N values of ADA samples were evaluated to determine the magnitude of an ADA response. The S/N was thus calculated to assess the relative sensitivity of ADA responses detected based on anti-CSL300 surrogate positive control (PC) antibody (Figure 1(B)) with S/N values all below 100 ng/mL low positive control (LPC) and more similar to the 2.5 ng/mL ultra-low positive control. In total, ~7% preexisting ADA were detected that were not treatment-boosted over time while treatment-emergent ADA incidence was low (~3% in total), comprising of ~2% transient ADA and ~1% detected at the end of study. The low-magnitude ADAs identified can potentially be attributed to the highly sensitive (2.5 ng/mL) ADA method detecting clinically irrelevant ADAs.

Case studies › Legacy implementation of the NAb assay paradigm

Although recent years have seen a movement away from the stand-alone NAb assay paradigm in favor of an integrated immunogenicity assessment [6], the development of NAb assays for the two studies discussed here began prior to this shift in scientific understanding and at an early stage in clinical development, occurring around the time of initiation of Phase 2 clinical studies. While it was traditionally accepted that NAb assays were not necessary until pivotal Phase 3 studies, industry practices often involved early investment in assay development as a risk‑mitigation strategy given the analytical challenges associated with developing and validating standalone NAb assays. As a result, a significant financial and resource opportunity cost is made in order to fulfill a regulatory “tick-box” even when there is no scientific value. However, such investment may prove unnecessary if clinical programs are discontinued prematurely or if assays are only implemented in late-stage clinical development to meet the standalone NAb assay requirement without yielding clinically meaningful data. The integrated ADA/PK/PD data approach is now being adopted to gain early insight into clinically relevant neutralizing activity before deeper investment in developing and validating cell-based and non-cell-based NAb assays that may not generate conclusive and clinically meaningful data. Over the course of clinical development of garadacimab, two separate attempts were undertaken to develop a NAb assay using an enzymatic assay reflective of the functional activity of garadacimab. Neither of these attempts were able to achieve adequate sensitivity to characterize the impact of low-level ADAs as observed during the study. Therefore, a ligand-binding assay approach was developed which measured neutralization of garadacimab and FXIIa binding. The validated ligand-binding NAb assay sensitivity was 500 ng/mL in plasma from donors with HAE. Given that only low‑magnitude ADA-positive samples in the clinical studies were detected, it was determined that the developed NAb assay was insufficiently sensitive to detect NAbs in these ADA-positive samples. Relative sensitivity in both the ADA and NAb assays could be compared because the assays utilized PC anti-garadacimab antibody clones of comparable activity. Of note, the highest ADA-positive sample was just above the 100 ng/mL low PC and thus would not be expected to be positive in the NAb assay with an assay sensitivity of 500 ng/mL. Similarly, for clazakizumab, the initial NAb assay development focussed on the use of a cell-based assay, using engineered cell lines to evaluate the impact of NAbs on IL6 signaling. However, this cell-based assay was technically challenging and unable to meet the requirements for precision and sensitivity. Therefore, a ligand‑binding ELISA-based assay was developed, where the drug-ligand binding was shown to be disrupted by the presence of NAbs. However, validation data indicated the ligand‑binding NAb assay sensitivity was approximately 200 ng/mL, 80-fold lower sensitivity than the ADA assay when using the same PC across the assays. Given the large difference in the assay sensitivity between the ADA assay and the standalone NAb assay, and low magnitude ADA responses observed, it was likely that the corresponding NAb assay results would be negative or inconclusive and not provide any clinically meaningful information.

Case studies › Assessment of clinically meaningful neutralizing activity

Consistent with the low-risk status of the two drugs, no ADA-mediated safety events were observed in subjects treated with either garadacimab or clazakizumab. For both the case studies presented here, although a stand-alone NAb assay was developed and validated which would satisfy the requirements of regulatory guidances for a standalone NAb assay, on critical review of the assay performance it was clear that the validated NAb assays were not fit for purpose for the intended context of use which is to detect clinically meaningful neutralizing activity. For both case studies, the implementation of the validated NAb assays would have resulted in inconclusive data and not provided clinically meaningful data on presence and impact of neutralizing activity of antibodies. Therefore, to ensure the application of high-quality scientifically rigor to inform regulators, prescribers and patients on clinically meaningful neutralizing activity, the more informative approach was applied to characterize NAb activity of the ADA-positive samples by integrating key data sets including ADA status, drug concentration, and PD/efficacy results. This was intended to detect the presence of clinically meaningful neutralizing activity and to proactively replace the standalone NAb assay. In both cases, rather than generating inconclusive NAb assay data, the more conservative characterization of neutralizing activity of all ADA responses using the integrated ADA/PK/PD data assessment demonstrated unequivocally the lack of clinically relevant neutralizing activity of the ADA responses detected. Case studies › Regulatory outcomes

A detailed scientific narrative describing the application and outcomes of the integrated assessment of NAb activity was included in the submission packages for garadacimab and has been accepted without a postmarketing requirement (PMR) by the FDA, EMA, Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA-Japan) and all other jurisdictions with licensing at time of submission of this publication. Regulatory review focused on the overall immunogenicity strategy and the adequacy of the integrated ADA, PK, PD, efficacy, and safety data package to characterize clinically meaningful neutralizing activity, and the submission was accepted with no further questions. The proposal for the use of an integrated assessment for clazakizumab was submitted to the FDA in the Ph2b to Ph3 transition; as part of this proposal the Ph2b data was assessed and accepted for the Ph3 pivotal studies.

Future perspective

It is a regulatory expectation to characterize the neutralizing potential of ADA responses in pivotal clinical studies, as well as the associated clinical impact of those responses. While stand-alone NAb assays have historically been developed for this purpose, the 2019 FDA guidance [1] has acknowledged that appropriately designed PK and/or PD assays can be used in lieu of a standalone NAb assay to assess the neutralizing impact of ADA responses using the integrated analysis of these data sets–importantly this should be initially discussed and agreed upon with regulatory agencies. As noted in a recent publication [3], integration of data from all clinical evaluations (PK, PD, efficacy, safety) is recommended by health authorities and can provide an overall assessment of NAb activity. Moreover, as stated in the EMA guideline [2] on immunogenicity assessment of therapeutic proteins “The goal of immunogenicity studies is to investigate the presence of an immune response to the therapeutic protein and its clinical impact.” Thus, the evaluation of immunogenicity should be based on integrated analysis of immunological, pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, as well as clinical efficacy and safety data [22]. The two case studies presented here are real world examples of the successful use of an integrated approach to assess neutralizing activity to support drug development. There are several elements that we found critical for the assessment of clinically meaningful neutralizing activity using integrated datasets. Firstly, immunogenicity risk was assessed contemporaneously by integrating knowledge of product- and patient-related factors to evaluate the likelihood and potential impact of immunogenicity and derive a theoretical risk score. Emerging data from completed clinical studies were then incorporated to inform immunogenicity monitoring strategies in subsequent studies. It is important that early in development there is an understanding of the key data sets that may be impacted by the presence of NAbs, therefore enabling thoughtfully designed assays to ensure that they are validated for the specified context of use. Underpinning the success of implementation of the integrated assessment of neutralizing activity is the need for ongoing cross‑functional collaboration and stakeholder engagement. A priori cross‑functional alignment on data analysis and reporting approaches facilitates a common understanding of how the data sets will be integrated. This moves away from the misconception that neutralizing activity status can only be determined by a standalone NAb assay, recognizing instead that neutralizing activity can be reported using surrogate measurements in an integrated assessment by setting clear parameters, namely, the S/N measurements with any clinically meaningful impacts on PK, PD, efficacy, and safety [17,23]. Whilst it is essential to establish a strategy early and to continuously evaluate it in response to emerging data and insights, it is key to utilize opportunities to make strategic updates or course‑correct as needed. This iterative evolution of NAb activity assessments may involve progressive interactions with regulators on the scientific basis for updating the strategy so that it reflects current thinking and iterative datasets, rather than being wedded to preemptive or outdated commitments. As illustrated by the examples provided, evolving perspectives from both industry and regulators must be integrated, with strategies adapted accordingly to ensure that NAb assessments remain scientifically rigorous and clinically relevant. For both case studies presented here, although a validated NAb assay was in place, an intentional strategic decision was made to utilize surrogate markers of neutralizing activity in lieu of a standalone NAb assay. Although the development of the standalone NAb assays for these programs was primarily driven by legacy industry thinking regarding the need for a standalone NAb assays for marketing approval, which at the time of commencement of assay development was relatively current, these examples nicely highlight the challenges in implementing standalone NAb assays both from a technical assay perspective but also from the perspective of clinical utility, in that these assays often generate inconclusive data or inaccurate conclusions [24]. The use of surrogate measures for in vivo NAb activity against garadacimab was included in the regulatory submission packages and accepted without PMR by all receiving regulatory agencies to date including FDA, EMA and access countries. Likewise, the proposal to use surrogate measures for in vivo NAb activity was proposed to the FDA after the completion of the Ph2b clazakizumab ESKD clinical study, with positive feedback received [21]. These two examples serve to build on the collective knowledge within the industry bioanalytical community, supporting strategies outlined in recent publications [25]. The intention of including information on the NAb assays in addition to the integrated assessments within this perspective is not to suggest that both should be in play as was the case in the two case studies, but to highlight the importance of strategic and informed decision-making to ensure clinically meaningful data generation for patients and healthcare providers. Following the framework and decision algorithm presented by Partridge et al. a subset of clinical studies will require the implementation of a standalone NAb assay where there is a clear need and value-add to the holistic data package [6]. While it may be tempting to speculate that both a NAb assay and integrated assessment should be in place to de-risk any regulatory feedback, it should be acknowledged there is a heavy financial and resource investment required for the development of standalone NAb assays. If these efforts are reduced to a mere procedural requirement for submissions, they risk delaying clinical development and regulatory timelines. Additionally, there is the more concerning possibility that the inappropriate implementation of standalone NAb assays may result in inconclusive labeling data, potentially impacting decision-making by regulators, prescribers and patients. Ultimately, the assessment of NAb activity should be guided by a clearly defined and scientifically driven plan and supported by comprehensive and robust datasets to generate clinically meaningful outcomes. To that end, new programs will implement an immunogenicity risk assessment early in development to identify what is the most scientifically driven approach to understanding neutralizing activity. While the two examples provided involve low-risk monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), this framework for assessing and reporting clinically meaningful neutralizing activity can be broadly applied to other molecules, except where specific safety concerns regarding NAbs justify the early implementation of a standalone NAb assay, often for patient monitoring. In cases without direct safety issues related to NAbs, NAb assays are typically utilized post hoc to characterize the impact of ADAs on PK, PD, and efficacy, drawing upon existing data as illustrated by the examples above. Consequently, as previously discussed, the added value and clinical relevance remain ambiguous, and such assessments are often conducted as procedural requirements rather than based on scientific rationale. The use of a standalone NAb assay should therefore be guided by the need to address distinct safety concerns. Label information about ADA should offer clinically relevant data, helping prescribers and patients make informed choices by showing the context and effect of neutralizing activity rather than just reporting results from a standalone NAb assay as a binary value or percentage (e.g., 5% NAb positive) [5,18]. Using an integrated approach, information on the label may outline the included integrated data sets and indicate the number and proportion of patients with clinically meaningful impact of ADA as demonstrated by the impact on efficacy or safety. As outlined in the a priori criteria that was used to assess the clinical impact of ADA, it is important to note that although there may be neutralizing activity that has an impact on PK and/or PD, there may not always be an impact on safety and/or efficacy and therefore such impact should not be considered clinically meaningful. We suggest that the label focuses on providing detailed enumeration only on clinically meaningful immunogenicity responses that would lend insight into patient benefit-risk decision making. Again, this clear distinction between clinically meaningful and not clinically meaningful immunogenicity highlights the advantage of the integrated approach in clearly articulating clinical impact during analysis and ultimately for label inclusion [26]. The current labeling where incidence of ADA and NAb assay results are reported is specific to the assay methods deployed in the clinical studies and cannot be used to inform risks of immunogenicity across therapeutic products. However, if the label provided enumeration of only clinically meaningful immunogenicity responses, that may allow for a more direct comparison of immunogenicity risks across products. In conclusion, the assessment of NAb activity in pivotal clinical studies should be grounded in an integrated analysis of PK, PD, efficacy, and safety data, as supported by recent regulatory guidance and industry experience. While standalone NAb assays have traditionally been used, evolving industry and regulatory perspectives now recognize that appropriately designed surrogate data sets and integrated approaches can provide more robust and clinically meaningful evaluations, often reducing unnecessary complexity and resource use that don’t add scientific value. At present, adoption of these approaches continues to evolve and may vary among regulatory agencies, review divisions, and individual development programs. Early and ongoing engagement with health authorities therefore remains essential to ensure alignment on immunogenicity assessment strategies and expectations for a given product and context of use. Strategic, cross-functional planning and ongoing adaptation to emerging data are essential to ensure that assessment of neutralizing antibody effects remains relevant, scientifically rigorous, and aligned with regulatory expectations, ultimately supporting effective decision-making and patient care.
