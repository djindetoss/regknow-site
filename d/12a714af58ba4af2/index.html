Abstract

Potency (IC50) prediction of small molecules is pivotal for anticancer drug development. This study benchmarked five deep learning (DL) models for IC50 prediction-DeepCDR, DrugCell, PaccMann, Precily, and tCNN-against a simple mean-based Baseline using standardized GDSC datasets and recently published anticancer compounds. To ensure practicality, conventional error metrics were supplemented with percentage error, log error, three-sigma limit, and a newly proposed Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy statistic. The models performed well on randomly split data and unseen cell lines but showed sharply reduced accuracy for unseen compounds. Though all DL models exhibited similar performance trends, DeepCDR, DrugCell, and tCNN held a slight edge in most testing scenarios. Interestingly, several DL algorithms could not significantly outperform the Baseline model in many tests. Assessing prediction error against physicochemical and biological properties of compounds and cell lines revealed weak correlation, highlighting an underexplored aspect of model performance. A user-friendly web server ( https://nlplab1.isical.ac.in/ic50.php ) was also developed for IC50 prediction of new compounds against cancer cell lines.

Introduction

Cancer was responsible for about one in six global deaths (16.8%) in 20221. Despite the pressing need for effective anticancer therapies, conventional experimental screening methods are hindered by significant limitations, including excessive duration, intensive labor requirements, and high cost. Computational approaches, such as Computer-Aided Drug Design (CADD), offer a faster and more efficient route to designing novel anticancer candidates2. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) is a widely used indicator of anticancer drug potency. Accurate prediction of IC50 values can, thus, greatly assist medicinal chemists in designing effective small-molecule therapeutics against cancer. It is well established that phenotypic screening via cell-based assays more precisely represents in vivo conditions than target-based binding assays, as it assesses biological activity, membrane permeability, and cytotoxicity–key factors in the clinical translatability of drugs3–5. Traditional CADD methods like molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, though useful in modeling ligand–target binding, fail to capture the complexity of the whole cellular or tissue environment, with MD being computationally intensive and reliant on experimental data6–8. Classical quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) methods, which posit that the biological activity of a small molecule is solely a function of its structural and physicochemical descriptors, without accounting for cell-line attributes like genetics are inadequate9, as the same drug can have widely varying IC50 across different cell lines (e.g., the drug foretinib has an IC50 of 0.074 μM in the KU-812 cell line, whereas in the SW1417 cell line the same drug has an IC50 of 8.364 μM: > 100-fold difference, according to the GDSC database10). Thus, although QSAR may predict basic attributes like solubility and elementary pharmacokinetics, it struggles to meaningfully model drug efficacy and toxicity11. Machine learning (ML) algorithms, including deep learning (DL), have demonstrated a remarkable ability to analyze and learn large and complex datasets, uncovering patterns and relationships that conventional methods may overlook. ML approaches address have thus found numerous applications in drug discovery, enabling target identification, drug potency prediction, toxicity prediction, and more2,12. In the preponderance of ML models predicting drug response to cancer, the molecular structure is represented by SMILES (simplified molecular input line entry system), molecular fingerprinting, or molecular descriptors, while the disease state is depicted via genomic profiles, encompassing mutational status and copy number variation or transcriptomic profiles (RNA-seq expression data)13, as depicted in Fig. 1. Classical ML methods like classification, regression, and clustering, however, struggle with representing high-dimensional data like genomic or transcriptomic profiles and encoding small molecule information effectively14. Deep learning (DL) models, enabled by neural networks, address high-dimensional data challenges via embeddings that both reduce dimensionality and automatically extract relevant features, eliminating the need for handcrafted descriptors15. While various promising studies have been published, the effectiveness of ML methods for predicting IC50 values in the practical context of drug discovery remains largely unexplored. The models documented in the literature have been assessed across diverse datasets, usually with a few accuracy metrics like root-mean-squared error (RMSE) or correlation coefficients, prevalent mainly in the ML community. Additionally, most reported results rely on publicly available databases like the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC)16, Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)17, or The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA)18, with no analysis for newly reported, unseen drug candidates. These drug response prediction studies13,19–21 primarily focus on bioinformatics or cancer genomics, offering limited insight for medicinal chemists, e.g., how model accuracy depends on small-molecule physicochemical properties. Another significant gap is that, though Chen et al.19 assessed some traditional ML algorithms, there is a lack of systematic, uniform dataset-based comparative evaluation of DL models with a simple mean-based, non-ML “Baseline” model, which we created, to benchmark the performance of the DL models and reveal their comparative accuracy. Our work systematically analyzes five deep learning models for the prediction of anticancer drug potency, namely DeepCDR22, DrugCell23, PaccMann24, Precily25, and tCNN26 (Fig. 1). We selected these five deep learning models based on their methodological diversity, relevance to IC50 prediction, prior recognition in the field, and availability of the code bases. They span a wide range of deep neural architectures, incorporate heterogeneous biological and chemical features, and represent different strategies for handling input complexity and biological interpretability. Their inclusion allows for a comparative evaluation of deep learning paradigms under standardized experimental settings, highlighting trade-offs between predictive accuracy, generalizability, and clinical relevance. To ensure an unbiased comparison of model performance, all DL models, including the simple mean-based Baseline model, were trained and tested using the same data splits derived from a standardized dataset from the GDSC database (following five-fold cross-validation). In contrast to previous studies, alongside conventional performance metrics, we computed the absolute percent error (APE) and the absolute base-10 logarithmic error (ALE) for every prediction and the mean APE (MAPE) and mean ALE (MALE) for each model to present a realistic and undiluted picture of prediction accuracy. Though IC50 values reported in literature and databases are treated as absolute references when computing error metrics, experimental IC50 measurements inherently exhibit considerable variability. Specifically, IC50 values for the same drug–cell line measured using different cell viability or cytotoxicity assay protocols vary markedly (>400%). For example, U373MG and U87MG cells, with the drug etoposide, yielded IC50 < 60 μM using Trypan Blue assay, 240 μM using Acid Phosphatase assay, and >300 μM using Alamar Blue (resazurin) assay27. Even if identical assay protocol is used, numerous intricate factors such as 2D vs 3D cell culture substrates, choice of curve-fitting technique, and differences in cell seeding density may lead to massive IC50 variations–as observed for cisplatin tested using the MTT assay28,29. To capture this inherent inconsistency in the experimentally measured IC50, we formulated the summary statistic Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA) to evaluate model performance. While prior research tested ML models using only existing datasets, we evaluated the models on more than 150 newly designed anticancer small molecules that were reported in recent literature30–39. Beyond assessing model accuracy, our study provides unique insights into the chemical and biological properties of drug candidates, which are particularly significant for medicinal chemists. Furthermore, we have developed a web server for medicinal chemists, based on the aforementioned models, which accepts small molecules in SMILES format and predicts their IC50 values against more than 800 cancer cell lines. Data from public databases (e.g., TCGA, CCLE, GDSC), consisting of cell line omics, compound representations (e.g., SMILES/descriptors), and IC50 values, are embedded into vectors and used to train neural networks. Results and discussion › Evaluation metrics

To assess the performance of the prediction models, we initially use standard evaluation metrics as followed in the ML community: While these metrics provide an overall assessment of a model’s performance, they do not capture the practical acceptability of predictions in laboratory settings. For instance, a model with R2 = 0.7 and RMSE = 1.0 may still leave a lab chemist uncertain about the actual reliability of its IC50 predictions. To address this limitation, we adopt the use of simple percentage error and log base-10 error, which offer improved interpretability for experimentally measured quantities such as IC50. Specifically, we calculated the Absolute Percentage Error (APE) for each prediction and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) as a summary error metric across all predictions. The absolute base-10 logarithmic error (ALE) and its mean, MALE, across all predictions, were also computed. On the ALE scale, a value of 0.3 corresponds to an error by a factor of 2, 1 corresponds to an error by a factor of 10, and a value of 2 corresponds to an error by a factor of 100. Another key consideration in evaluation is normalization and rescaling. ML model outputs are typically normalized before training. For IC50 or ln[IC50] prediction, values are scaled to the [0,1] range, and error metrics are computed on this normalized scale. However, such evaluation does not reflect the true error in IC50 values, making it less meaningful for practitioners. For example, in a dataset where IC50 values range from 0.01 μM to 20 μM, the corresponding lnIC50 values span [−4.6052, 2.9957]. If a model is trained to predict ln[IC50], these values are min-max normalized to [0,1]. Suppose the model predicts 0.2 for a given compound–cell line pair; this corresponds to 0.3158 in the ln[IC50] scale, which converts back to an IC50 of 1.3714 μM. If the experimental IC50 is 0.05 μM (ln[IC50] = −1.9957, mapped as 0.0654 on the [0,1] scale), then APE in normalized scale is 206 (experimental: 0.0654, predicted: 0.2), and APE in actual IC50 values is 2643 (experimental: 0.05 μM, predicted: 1.3714 μM). This drastic difference arises due to the nonlinearity in the scale conversion between IC50 and normalized outputs. Unlike traditional ML approaches, we evaluated the APE and ALE directly on IC50 values, free from any normalization or rescaling, as the ultimate objective of these models is to provide accurate IC50 predictions for real-world applications. While MAPE and MALE give useful ideas in measuring IC50 values, these metrics, along with r, R2, and RMSE, implicitly assume the experimentally derived IC50 to be the absolute ground truth. To date, no popular accuracy metric incorporates any tolerance for the inherent variability associated with IC50 values determined by cell viability assays, which are influenced by a variety of intricate and often uncontrollable factors. Predictions with high APE and ALE may be considered practically very useful in laboratory settings, as IC50 for the same compound–cell line may vary across experiments and laboratories. For example, if the experimental IC50 is 0.05 μM, and the model predicts 0.15 μM, the APE is high, 300%, but the prediction is still relevant as the IC50 is within the error range commonly faced in labs. Given these limitations, we developed a new evaluation measure, Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA), which incorporates empirical estimates of IC50 variability to offer a more practical and context-aware assessment of model performance. a Histogram of ΔpIC50 values (green) with the fitted normal curve (black). Results and discussion › Evaluation metrics › EVAPA

The Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA) is a summary statistic designed to evaluate model performance by accounting for the intrinsic variability in the experimental determination of IC50 values for compounds tested against cancer cell lines. To derive EVAPA, we selected a subset of antineoplastic drugs with the most extensively reported bioactivity measurements across diverse cancer cell lines in the ChEMBL_35 database. Only data derived from cell viability assays consistent with those used in the GDSC database, i.e., Resazurin and CellTiter-Glo assays with 72-h incubation were considered, ensuring assay compatibility. The resulting data was processed to compute ΔpIC50, i.e., the pairwise differences in pIC50 values for the same compound, cell line, and assay, allowing for a unified analysis across different drug–cell line combinations. The normal distribution fitting to ΔpIC50 is shown in Fig. 2a. The goodness of fit, evaluated using D’Agostino’s K-squared test, yielded a high K2 of 9.051 and p > 0.0140. The Q-Q plot, shown in Fig. 2b, along with D’Agostino’s K-squared test, indicates that ΔpIC50 may slightly deviate from normal distribution but is a close enough fit for the purpose of our analysis. The standard deviation of ΔpIC50 (σΔpIC50) and standard deviation of pIC50 (σpIC50) of the fitted normal distribution were 0.734 and 0.519, respectively. This implied that 90% of pIC50 lay within a deviation of 1.645×σpIC50 or 0.853. pIC50 deviation of 0.853 corresponds to a IC50 deviation by a factor of 7.129. This means that 90% of all IC50 measurements agree within a factor of 7.129: if the experimentally measured IC50 value of a drug–cell line pair using the specified assay conditions is 1 μM, there is a 90% chance that the true IC50 value lies in the interval [0.140 μM, 7.129 μM]. The derivation of factor 7.129 represents one of the first systematic efforts to quantify the inherent variability in IC50 measurements from cell viability or cytotoxicity assays—a well-recognized problem in experimental drug discovery. Results and discussion › Benchmarking performance on GDSC dataset

The performance of the five deep learning models and the simple mean-based Baseline model was evaluated under three data partitioning strategies: Random (R), No Common Cell lines (NCC), and No Common Drugs (NCD) splits. In the R split, models are trained and tested on randomly sampled subsets, allowing overlap between compounds and cell lines. This idealized setup offers a representative view of the dataset and yields reliable overall performance estimates but does not evaluate generalization to unseen chemical or biological entities. The NCC split assesses generalization to new cancer cell lines by ensuring that training and test sets contain disjoint cell line subsets, simulating predictions for previously unobserved biological samples. The NCD split evaluates drug generalization by partitioning compounds into non-overlapping sets, testing a model’s ability to predict the efficacy of entirely new molecules in established cell lines: a scenario aligned with real-world drug discovery tasks. The evaluation results, as presented in Fig. 3, include the Pearson correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (R2), and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) computed on ln(IC50). More importantly, Fig. 3 also reports the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean absolute base-10 logarithmic error (MALE), and the Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA) computed directly on IC50 without rescaling or normalization. The mean of the aforementioned error statistics across the five folds for the Baseline model under the R, NCC, and NCD splits is also shown, contextualizing where the DL models stand relative to simple mean-based predictions. For the R split in Baseline, the Pearson correlation coefficient is not available, as the predicted IC50 value is constant across cell lines and compounds in a fold. All the DL models in the R split exhibited high r (0.81–0.88) and R2 values (0.63–0.77), along with low RMSE (0.98–1.23), relatively low MAPE (136%–348%) and MALE (0.31–0.40) values, indicating strong correlation, low error, and overall good predictive performance when the training and test sets were randomly sampled from the same distribution. The consistently high EVAPA scores (>90%) further confirm model reliability in this non-demanding setup. The Baseline model produced a constant IC50 prediction of ~5.3 μM (slightly different for each of the five folds) and achieved quite poor R2 (~0.00), high RMSE (~2.02), and very high MAPE (~8272%) for the R split. The Baseline had a MALE (~0.32) comparable to the DL models but a suboptimal EVAPA of ~67%. Overall, all DL models performed appreciably better than this simple mean-based baseline in the R split. In the NCC split, model performances declined slightly. The correlation coefficient (r) (0.76–0.82), R2 (0.56–0.67), and RMSE (1.12–1.31) reveal reasonable linear agreement between predicted and true values. The MAPE increased to 208–335%, while MALE rose to 0.38–0.42. The EVAPA scores (87%–92%) remained within acceptable bounds, indicating that most models retained reasonable predictive accuracy and a moderate degree of generalizability. Interestingly, the Baseline model, generating drug-wise mean IC50s, performed very well, attaining r of ~0.85, R2 of ~0.71, and RMSE of ~1.07. On this split, the Baseline’s MALE (~0.367) and EVAPA (90%) were comparable and even slightly exceeded those of some DL models, whereas, only MAPE (~376%) was worse than the DL models. This improved Baseline performance for NCC likely reflects the specific data partitioning and/or that a drug-wise mean captures the underlying distribution (i.e., the biological diversity of the cell lines) reasonably well. Among the DL models, tCNN performed best on the NCC split, while PaccMann and DrugCell showed higher MAPE and MALE values and greater variability, suggesting sensitivity to the biological diversity of the cell lines. In the NCD split, the results indicate a sharp decline in the performance of all the models, including the Baseline. The r and R2 dropped significantly across all models, approaching zero or becoming negative, such as with PaccMann, signifying that predictions were worse than simply using the mean of the training data as a baseline. Error metrics also reflected poor prediction–RMSE and MAPE increased substantially with high variance (e.g., DrugCell MAPE was 11,659.29 ± 1,884.90). MALE approximately doubled from the R and NCC splits, reaching 0.699–0.959, corresponding to the predicted IC50 values being up to 9× different from the experimental values. EVAPA scores of the DL models also declined markedly (52–73%), reflecting a poor match between model predictions and pharmacological response patterns. The Baseline, yielding cell line-wise mean IC50, was observed to have an r and R2 of ~0, an RMSE of ~2.23, MAPE >12,500%, MALE of 0.86, and EVAPA of ~60.6%. Hence, for most error metrics, some of the DL models’ performance remained inferior to the Baseline. Overall, the tCNN model showed slightly improved performance on the NCD split than the other models. In essence, the results on the GDSC dataset, employing diverse and rigorous evaluation strategies, reporting performance across multiple scenarios, highlight a critical limitation: although models perform well (much better than the Baseline) under ideal conditions typically used for testing machine learning models, i.e., random split, their extrapolative power to unseen chemical structures is significantly limited. Notably, the R and NCC test sets remain largely in-distribution (ID), whereas the NCD split presents out-of-distribution (OOD) test samples41. The NCC split involves mammalian cell lines that share broadly similar genomic and transcriptomic profiles (with >90% of human DNA being identical and most genes expressed across numerous cell types), whereas the NCD setup which deals with small molecule compounds that are highly heterogeneous, differing widely in structure and physicochemical properties and are comparatively fewer in the GDSC database (only ~200 compounds). This distinction in the nature of data may partly explain the improved DL performance in NCC compared to NCD. However, the apparent ability of DL algorithms to learn cell line–specific patterns for predicting IC50 values in novel cell lines cannot be inferred with certainty, as the Baseline, without any such “learning", performs equally well in NCC. Results and discussion › Influence of compound and cell line properties on performance

We analyzed how chemical and biological properties of compounds and cell lines influence model performance, offering insights for medicinal chemists and pharmacologists. Specifically, we assessed the impact of compounds’ physicochemical properties, target pathways (reflecting modes of action), and cell line tissue types on IC50 prediction accuracy. Based on the average error metrics computed across five-fold random splits, DeepCDR and DrugCell emerged as the best-performing models, and thus further analysis on these is reported. The three other models also exhibited similar trends. The error metric chosen for evaluating compound properties was the log10-scaled drug-wise MAPE(IC50), defined as the MAPE of IC50 values, calculated across all drug–cell line pairs corresponding to each individual compound (or drug). The variability trends were confirmed by considering other metrics like drug-wise r, R2, and log10-scaled drug-wise RMSE (see Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Similarly, for evaluating dependence on cell line property, we employed the log10-scaled cell-wise MAPE(IC50) as the error metric. Results and discussion › Influence of compound and cell line properties on performance › Compound properties

Four Lipinski parameters, namely molecular weight (MW), lipophilicity (clogP), number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD), and number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), along with rotatable bond count (RotB) and the fraction of sp3-hybridized atoms (Fsp3), were selected as physicochemical properties for analysis42. Analysis was conducted on 170 different compounds, with MW ranging from 130 to 672.3 Da and clogP values from −3.2 to 8.7, thus encompassing most drugs and drug-like compounds present in databases such as ZINC 1543. The prediction errors from both models exhibited weak correlations (r ≈ 0.3) with MW and HBA and negligible correlations (r ≈ 0.1) with descriptors HBD, clogP, RotB, and Fsp3 (Fig. 4). Similar correlations were observed for the other models as well. Considering other error metrics, for drug-wise RMSE, only MW and HBA (r ≈ −0.23 for DeepCDR) showed weak negative correlations (Supplementary Fig. 3), whereas for all remaining metrics and properties, no significant correlation was observed (∣r∣ < 0.2) (see Supplementary Figs. 1–3). This finding suggests that the potency prediction accuracy of these models remains consistent across compounds with diverse sizes, polarity, flexibility, and solubility. Such consistency is particularly noteworthy and differentiates these models from molecular docking programs, which typically exhibit decreased accuracy as the number of rotatable bonds and heavy atoms (a proxy for molecular weight) increases44. The variation in prediction error corresponding to the target pathways of compounds annotated in the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/compounds) was also analyzed. Compounds associated with at least two cell lines in the random-split test sets of both DeepCDR and DrugCell models were included in the analysis. A box plot (Fig. 5a) illustrating the distribution of errors across these target pathways revealed the lowest median log10-scaled error to be 1.5 for both models, whereas the highest median errors were 2.2 and 2.4 for DeepCDR and DrugCell, respectively, corresponding to a maximum log10 error variation of 0.9. Given the previously established experimental variability of pIC50 as 0.853, it can be inferred that the predictive accuracy of the models does not show substantial bias across the target pathways of the compounds. Results and discussion › Influence of compound and cell line properties on performance › Cell line properties

We analyzed the variation in prediction error for cell lines originating from different tissue types available in the GDSC database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/celllines). Only tissue types represented by at least two cell lines in the random-split test sets of both DeepCDR and DrugCell models were included, resulting in a total of 490 unique cell lines selected for this analysis. The box plot (Fig. 5b) illustrates the distribution of errors across these tissue types and indicates that the lowest median log10-scaled errors for both DeepCDR and DrugCell were 1.7, while the highest median errors were 2.1 for both models. Therefore, a log10 error variation of 0.4 was observed, substantially smaller than 0.853, i.e., the experimental variability in measurement IC50 values. Thus, we conclude that the tissue type of the cell lines does not influence the drug potency prediction accuracy in any significant manner.

Results and discussion › Performance on novel anticancer compounds

Four DL models—DeepCDR, DrugCell, PaccMann, and Precily—along with the mean-based Baseline model were evaluated using novel anticancer small molecules reported in recent literature. None of these compounds were present in the GDSC database, thereby simulating the application of these models to entirely new experimental data. The IC50 values reported for these novel unseen compounds were obtained using diverse cell viability assay protocols, including sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay with 48- or 72-h incubation periods and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) with 72- or 96-h incubations, which differ from the protocols employed for compounds in the GDSC database. The tCNN model could not be evaluated on these unseen compounds, as it does not support prediction for molecules outside the GDSC database, which served as its training set. For each model, the fold that performed best on the NCD split was used to evaluate the novel compounds. DrugCell and Precily performed better than the other two models across most evaluation metrics, while PaccMann showed a relatively inferior performance to the other three models (Table 1). The Baseline showed lower r and a substantially higher MAPE than all DL framework. However, its R2, EVAPA, and percentage of predictions within 3σ of experimental IC50 were better than PaccMann and only marginally worse than other DL models. When compared with results from the NCD split from the GDSC dataset, RMSE and EVAPA were quite similar (for DrugCell: RMSE = 3.58 for novel compounds vs. 2.91 for NCD; EVAPA = 60.29% for novel compounds vs. 56.25% for NCD). Despite the overall poor predictive performance observed on both the NCD split and the set of novel unseen compounds, the similarity in results suggests that the GDSC-derived training and test sets reasonably capture the chemical and biological diversity of anticancer compounds. Moreover, the comparable results across compounds tested using different assay protocols indicate a potential degree of assay–agnostic behavior, i.e., although trained on IC50 values derived from specific assay types, the models exhibit similar predictive behavior when applied to compounds measured using alternative assay protocols. The r and MAPE(IC50) values, however, were significantly lower for the unseen compounds (for DrugCell: r = 0.32 for novel compounds vs. −0.02 for NCD; MAPE(IC50) = 2095 for novel compounds vs. 11,659 for NCD), likely due to the smaller size of the novel compounds’ test set relative to that of the NCD split. Since the standard deviations of experimentally determined IC50 (σIC50) were given, we applied the three-sigma rule to define an acceptable prediction range–a prediction was considered accurate if it fell within ±(3×σIC50)45,46. As shown in Table 1, the performance of the models under this evaluation criterion was very poor, with Precily achieving the highest accuracy of only 17.32%. It is to be noted, though, that the reported σIC50 was calculated from only three or four repetitions of the cell viability assay experiments and thus may be underestimated. We further examined the impact of stereochemical information, encoded via isomeric SMILES, on IC50 prediction. A subset of unseen compounds was available in both isomeric (chirality-aware) and canonical or generic (chirality-agnostic) SMILES formats (see Supplementary Fig. 4). Supplementary Table 1 shows that DeepCDR and Precily exhibit no consistent trend or substantial difference in IC50 prediction performance between isomeric and canonical SMILES. In contrast, DrugCell performs better with isomeric SMILES than canonical SMILES, while PaccMann shows decreased accuracy when given isomeric SMILES input. The accuracy trend in DrugCell may be attributed to the use of Morgan fingerprints, which more effectively capture stereochemical features—suggesting that increased stereochemical detail in SMILES enhances molecular representation for the model. Conclusions

This study evaluated the performance of five deep learning models for predicting the IC50 values of small-molecule compounds against cancer cell lines. To more effectively benchmark model performance on heterogeneous biological data, we incorporated metrics such as MAPE, MALE, and EVAPA, rarely used in ML evaluations, to yield more interpretable and experimentally meaningful insights. The development of EVAPA, which accounts for inherent experimental variability in IC50 and similar measures like GR50, underscores a fundamental limitation of drug potency data47. Although the heterogeneity of pharmacological databases is well recognized, proposed solutions such as adopting experimentally consistent and less variable parameters have yet to gain widespread acceptance48. Constructing databases around novel, more standardized measures could substantially improve both the performance and evaluation of ML models. The DL models’ IC50 prediction accuracy was analyzed against a simple mean-based Baseline model, on standardized datasets derived from the GDSC database, as well as on recently published anticancer compounds, which serve as a surrogate for experimental validation. The DL models demonstrated strong performance on randomly split data, which they were originally trained to tackle, and on unseen cell lines. However, predictive accuracy dropped substantially when evaluated on unseen compounds, both from the GDSC and the literature. This underscores a critical limitation of existing IC50 prediction models: the inadequate ability to extrapolate to structurally novel compounds, which constitute OOD samples. Since non-ML, simple mean-based predictions unexpectedly match and occasionally exceed the accuracy of several DL models in tests involving unseen cell lines and unseen drugs, incorporating such a Baseline model is proven to be essential when reporting ML performance. This benchmarking further highlights that the simple Baseline offers a competitive alternative to the complex, computationally expensive DL algorithms. The analysis of model performance with respect to the chemical and biological properties of compounds and cell lines indicated that prediction accuracy was largely independent of these factors. In future work, we aim to apply improved deep learning architectures, incorporating OOD learning strategies such as domain generalization and meta-learning41, to enhance IC50 prediction for unseen compounds by enabling better chemical feature representation and leveraging more diverse training data. We also plan to collaborate with industry and academia to assess the real-world utility of these models in drug discovery pipelines. Enhancing model explainability will also be a key focus, as it is vital for guiding lead optimization in small-molecule development.

Comparison of deep learning models for IC50 prediction

Methods › EVAPA

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), experimentally measured in cell viability assays, can vary significantly. To address this issue while evaluating IC50 predictions, we introduced the performance metric Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA), extending the method of Kalliokoski et al.49 from protein–ligand to drug–cell line measurements. Using the ChEMBL_35 web interface (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), we filtered “SMALL MOLECULE” and “L01 – ANTINEOPLASTIC AGENTS” by descending “Bioactivities” and selected the top compounds with the highest number of reported bioactivity measurements. The filters “IC50”, “Homo sapiens”, and “cell lines” were then applied, and the resulting bioactivity data were downloaded. Redundant or null columns were removed, retaining only rows with “<”, “=“, or “≤” standard relations and “nM” standard unit. According to the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/help#t_curve), Syto60, Resazurin, or CellTiter-Glo assays were used to screen compounds with a 72-h drug-cell incubation period. We extracted both assay name and incubation time from the “Assay Description” for each drug–cell line pair. Because Syto60 assays did not appear in ChEMBL data, we focused on Resazurin and CellTiter-Glo assays. AlamarBlue and CellTiter-Blue were treated as equivalent to Resazurin. Notably, the assay name and duration could appear in various formats (e.g., “CellTiter-Glo”, “Celltiter-Glo”, “cell titer glo”), and 72 hr or 3 days were considered identical to 72 h. For each of the compounds with the highest number of anticancer bioactivities reported, the data were filtered to include only these assay names and incubation times. We then collated IC50 measurements for each drug–cell line–assay combination having at least two measurements. All IC50 values (in M) were converted to pIC50 using the relation pIC50 = −log10(IC50), where IC50 is expressed in M. Pairwise differences in pIC50 were calculated as ΔpIC50=(pIC50)i−(pIC50)j, where i < j, for measurements of the same compound, cell line, and assay. Differences with ΔpIC50 ≥ 2.5 were excluded as likely erroneous experimental results. A population of 272 ΔpIC50 values was considered after this aforementioned processing. The standard deviation of ΔpIC50, denoted σΔpIC50, was obtained by fitting a normal (or Gaussian) distribution to ΔpIC50 data. The goodness of fit was evaluated using the D’Agostino-Pearson test and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot50. The corresponding standard deviation of pIC50 (σpIC50) was calculated as σpIC50=σΔpIC502 (see proof in Supplementary Information). According to the property of normal distribution, 90% of pIC50 values lie within 1.645×σpIC5051. Consequently, 90% of IC50 measurements for a given drug–cell line, using Resazurin or CellTiter-Glo assay, with 72 hours incubation of compound with cell line, vary by a factor of 10(1.645×σpIC50). Finally, EVAPA was defined as the percentage of predicted IC50 values that lie within a factor of 10(1.645×σpIC50) of the experimental IC50 values in the test set.

Methods › Datasets › Genomics of drug sensitivity in cancer (GDSC)-derived dataset

The GDSC database is a widely used pharmacogenomic resource representing the IC50 of a drug on a given cancer cell line. It (GDSC2 is an updated version with additional compounds, cell lines, and follows more standardized and precise assays) includes over 1000 cancer cell lines from various tissue types and more than 250 anticancer compounds, including chemotherapeutic agents and targeted inhibitors. For IC50 prediction, GDSC provides genomic features such as mutations, copy number variations, and gene expression data, along with SMILES representations, molecular fingerprints, and chemical descriptors for drugs. The full dataset initially contains approximately 200,000 cell line-compound pairs, with each cell line tested against up to 250 compounds and each compound screened across 500–1000 cell lines. A key consideration is the range of drug concentrations tested. Many IC50 values exceed the maximum tested concentration, leading to extrapolated estimates rather than direct measurements. To ensure reliable training, we filtered out these extrapolated values, reducing the dataset to approximately 51,000 experimentally measured cell line–compound pairs, which were used for training and evaluation. This filtered GDSC dataset provides a strong foundation for evaluating deep learning models in drug response prediction.

Methods › Datasets › Novel anticancer compounds from literature

We conducted a literature review within the ACS Journal of Medicinal Chemistry and the European Journal of Medicinal Chemistry to identify research articles focused on the discovery of anticancer or antitumor small molecules. Specifically, studies reporting IC50 values obtained from cell viability or cytotoxicity assays against cancer cell lines available in GDSC were selected. Nine papers published from 2021 onwards met these inclusion criteria30–39. From each selected paper, chemical structures of molecules tested against cell lines documented in the GDSC database were manually extracted and converted to SMILES representations using ChemDraw Professional v16.0.1.4. Corresponding IC50 values and their reported standard deviations were recorded in μM units. Entries presenting IC50 values expressed only as lower bounds (e.g., >x μM) were excluded from the dataset. Only those compound–cell line pairs for which predictions could be generated by all four models were selected. Consequently, a test set comprising 209 compound–cell line pairs was assembled. Some compounds were represented as generic or canonical SMILES as well as isomeric SMILES, to examine the prediction accuracy variation with SMILES. This test set of previously unseen compounds included data derived from diverse assay protocols and comprised both covalent and non-covalent inhibitors. None of these compounds was present in the GDSC database.

Methods › Models

In this experiment, the task involves predicting IC50 from the disease state (cell line) and therapeutic or drug (small molecule), where cell lines are described by omics profiles (e.g., genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics) and molecules by chemical structures or SMILES. We focus only on the methods that are based on deep learning methodologies, as deep neural models have outperformed traditional machine learning algorithms by a large margin for different decision-making tasks. The key difference between the two paradigms lies in feature representation: traditional machine learning algorithms rely on handcrafted features, whereas deep learning leverages large datasets to learn representations automatically. The main challenge in applying deep learning based models for IC50 prediction is choosing the appropriate input representations for drug and cell line. DL models for the prediction of IC50 mostly rely on embedding techniques that originated in the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP)52. The idea is to generate a semantically meaningful vector of a much lower dimension for a given sequence. Since generation of embedding for a large sequence such as genomics or transcriptomics profiles is still a technical challenge53, different strategies are followed by different methods. To calibrate the DL methods’ performance, we designed a simple mean-based “Baseline" model based on the training and testing sets following the splits used for the DL models. The following sections discuss how the methods addressed here feed inputs to the deep models and what type of DL algorithms are used to predict IC50. Comparison of deep learning models for IC50 prediction

Methods › Models › DeepCDR

DeepCDR22 is a hybrid graph convolutional network (GCN)-based approach that integrates multi-omics profiles of cancer cells with structural drug representations to predict IC50 values. Unlike models that rely on handcrafted chemical descriptors or SMILES-based embeddings, DeepCDR uses a Uniform Graph Convolutional Network (UGCN) to automatically learn the latent topological representation of drug molecules. On the genomic side, it processes multiple layers of biological data, including genomic mutations, transcriptomic expression levels, and epigenomic modifications. These multi-omics features are passed through specialized subnetworks, where high-level feature extraction is performed before merging them with the graph-based drug representations. The integrated features are then processed using a 1D CNN to generate the final IC50 predictions. The distinguishing feature of DeepCDR is its application of GCNs to represent drug structures, enabling direct learning of molecular interactions.

Methods › Models › DrugCell

DrugCell23 is a deep learning framework that predicts IC50 values by modeling cancer cells as a hierarchical system of biological processes. Instead of treating genomic features as independent entities, DrugCell organizes them into a structured Visible Neural Network (VNN) where neurons represent biological subsystems, such as signaling pathways, organelles, and cellular functions. This approach ensures that the model’s decision-making process aligns with known biological structures, enhancing interpretability. The model takes in binary mutation data as input, encoding genomic features based on their hierarchical relationships rather than as a simple flat vector. Drug representations are processed separately using Morgan fingerprints, a well-established method for encoding chemical structures. These two representations-one derived from a biologically structured VNN and the other from a traditional artificial neural network (ANN)-are merged at a late fusion stage to predict IC50 values. DrugCell’s key distinction is its biologically structured VNN, which represents cellular processes in a hierarchical manner.

Methods › Models › PaccMann

PaccMann24 is a multi-modal deep learning model that integrates three essential components for drug sensitivity prediction: the molecular structure of compounds, transcriptomic profiles of cancer cells, and prior knowledge of protein-protein interactions. Unlike traditional models that treat gene expression as a flat feature vector, PaccMann employs an attention-based encoding mechanism to assign higher weights to the most informative genes, thereby reducing the dimensionality of the input space while preserving critical biological signals. The model provides three different encoding strategies for SMILES-based drug representations: a bidirectional recurrent neural network (RNN), a convolutional encoder, and an attention-based encoder.

Methods › Models › Precily

Precily25 is another deep neural network (DNN)-based framework designed to predict IC50 values by leveraging gene expression data. Instead of using raw gene expression profiles, it computes pathway enrichment scores from bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, allowing for a more biologically meaningful representation. On the drug side, Precily processes molecular descriptors derived from SMILES representations using SMILESVec, which converts chemical structures into 100-dimensional vector embeddings. The model integrates these pathway enrichment scores and drug descriptors as input features to train a regression model that predicts log-transformed IC50 values (ln[IC50]). A distinct feature of Precily is the use of pathway-based representation, which groups genes into functional pathways rather than treating them as independent variables.

Methods › Models › tCNN

tCNN, or twin Convolutional Neural Networks26, is designed to improve IC50 prediction by directly extracting features from both drug and cell line data using convolutional architectures. The model processes genetic feature vectors from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database for cell line representation, while drugs are represented using their SMILES notation, which is passed through a dedicated convolutional neural network. These two parallel CNNs independently extract meaningful features from the genetic and molecular input spaces before merging them into a fully connected network that predicts IC50 values. Unlike models that rely on predefined molecular descriptors or pathway scores, tCNN learns from raw sequence-based representations, capturing intricate patterns from high-dimensional genomic and chemical data. Its key novelty lies in its use of CNNs to process SMILES representations, allowing the model to learn spatially relevant molecular interactions in an end-to-end fashion. Methods › Models › Baseline

For each split (random or R, no common cell lines or NCC and no common drugs or NCD), we built a simple mean-based predictor on the training data, excluding the validation and test data, used for the DL models: (i) for R the Baseline prediction is the global mean of IC50 across all training drug–cell pairs (only one IC50 value); (ii) for NCC the prediction is the drug–wise mean IC50 (mean of drug D over all corresponding cell lines: D − C1, D − C2, ...); (iii) for NCD the prediction is the cell line–wise mean IC50 (mean of cell line C over all drugs or compounds: C − D1, C − D2, ...). Evaluation employed the same metrics as for DL models (including EVAPA on IC50) for the test sets derived from GDSC and the “unknown” compounds taken from literature. Following the used five-fold cross-validation for the DL models, the Baseline was recomputed from the training portion of each fold to avoid test leakage. For reporting results, we consider the mean of the error metrics, as the standard deviations are quite low (see supplementary data).

Methods › Implementation details

The five deep learning models—DeepCDR, DrugCell, PaccMann, Precily, and tCNN—were trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU. All models were optimized using the Adam optimizer, with the mean squared error (MSE) employed as the loss function. All other hyperparameters were retained as specified in the respective original implementations. The genomic mutation data, gene expression data, and DNA methylation data used for training the models were downloaded from the DeMap portal (https://depmap.org). We performed five-fold cross-validation on the random, cell line, and drug splits, and the model corresponding to the lowest mean squared error value on the validation set was chosen for evaluation.
