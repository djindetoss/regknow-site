Abstract

Recent advances in protein structure determination and prediction, large-scale structural databases, and artificial intelligence have reshaped structure-based drug discovery. Structure-aware artificial intelligence models integrate molecular representation learning with three-dimensional protein information to model interactions, predict complex structures and binding poses, and generate novel molecules. In this review, we follow this paradigm along a continuum from protein-ligand modeling to the de novo design of biomolecular binders. We first outline the molecular and protein representations that render structures computable, together with the growing collection of structural data resources. We then survey state-of-the-art methods across drug-target interaction prediction, protein-ligand complex modeling and docking, de novo molecular generation, and biomolecule design, examining the convergence of docking, structure prediction, and molecular generation within co-folding and diffusion-based frameworks. Despite these advances, prospective experimental validation remains scarce, and persistent limitations such as biased structural coverage, limited and ambiguous negative supervision, fragmented benchmarking, and insufficient mechanistic interpretability continue to constrain real-world utility. Progress in data quality and supervision design, evaluation rigor, and design-relevant interpretability will be essential to translate methodological innovation into practical impact.

Introduction

Most therapeutic agents act through specific interactions with target proteins, so knowledge of protein structure and ligand binding modes has long been a central pillar of rational drug discovery. Structure-based drug design (SBDD) formalized this principle, using 3D target information to interpret pharmacological observations and to guide the identification and optimization of candidate compounds [1]. Although historically centered on small molecules, the same structure-guided logic has progressively extended to biomolecular modalities, including peptides, antibodies, and de novo-designed proteins, broadening both the targets and the therapeutic formats that structural information can address. In parallel with experimental progress in structural biology, artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly transformed structure-based drug discovery. Advances in X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and AI-based structure prediction have led to an unprecedented accumulation of structural information. AI has also begun to accelerate parts of the experimental workflow itself, such as the automated building of atomic models from cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) density maps [2]. Together with standardized, machine-readable structural resources, these developments have enabled the routine incorporation of 3D protein information into computational pipelines, even for targets that were previously difficult to access experimentally. As a result, structural availability has become less of a bottleneck for many early-stage drug discovery efforts, although gaps in target coverage and biologically relevant conformational states remain [3]. At the same time, advances in machine-learning architectures, particularly graph neural networks, Transformer-based models, and diffusion-based generative models, have enabled the development of structure-aware AI systems. These models incorporate 3D molecular geometry, protein–ligand interactions, and physicochemical constraints to predict drug–target interactions, estimate binding affinities, generate docking poses, and design novel molecules or biomolecular binders. This shift has progressed from small-molecule tasks such as interaction prediction and docking toward de novo molecular design within protein pockets and, more recently, the direct generation of biomolecular binders, such as antibodies, nanobodies, and mini-proteins. Co-folding architectures and diffusion-based generative frameworks are increasingly unifying docking, structure prediction, and molecular generation across these modalities. In benchmark settings, structure-aware AI models often outperform traditional ligand-based or sequence-based approaches, although these gains remain sensitive to dataset construction [4]. Despite these advances, a growing gap has become apparent between benchmark performance and practical experimental success. Many high-performing models on public datasets do not translate reliably into experimentally validated hits, leads, or functional binders [5]. This disconnect arises from structural and chemical biases in available datasets, scarcity of negative or failed interaction data, overfitting to benchmark-specific artifacts, and limited interpretability [6–8]. Consequently, high numerical performance does not always translate into actionable insights for medicinal chemists, structural biologists, or protein engineers. These challenges become more pronounced as the field moves from small-molecule modeling toward biomolecule design, where conformational flexibility, developability, and functional validation introduce additional complexity [9, 10]. Future progress will therefore depend not only on predictive accuracy but also on data quality, physical plausibility, generalization to unseen targets, and interpretability of model outputs. In this review, we provide a comprehensive and critical overview of structure-aware AI for next-generation drug discovery, from small-molecule design to biomolecule generation. We give particular attention to the convergence of docking, structure prediction, and molecular generation within co-folding and diffusion-based frameworks. Complementing recent reviews that have surveyed structure-aware and geometric deep learning for small-molecule drug discovery [11–13], we follow this paradigm beyond small molecules and across modalities, from protein–ligand systems to de novo biomolecule and binder design. We first examine molecular and protein representations that translate structural information into computable formats, alongside the expanding ecosystem of structural data resources. We then survey state-of-the-art AI models across drug–target interaction prediction, docking and pose estimation, molecule design, and de novo biomolecule design. We also analyze their limitations, identify recurring failure points, and discuss strategies to overcome current bottlenecks. Taken together, we aim to clarify where structure-aware AI has delivered genuine advances and where caution, refinement, and new conceptual frameworks are still required for therapeutic development.

Molecular representation paradigms

A central prerequisite for AI models in drug discovery is the availability of representations that translate chemical and biological entities into computable forms. In practice, the first step in in silico drug design is converting molecules into machine-interpretable formats. Researchers have employed description languages to encode chemical compound structures within the field of cheminformatics, because simple chemical formulas or molecular structure depictions are insufficient for systematic and reproducible computations. String-based representations provide an efficient means of storing and processing large-scale chemical libraries and have been widely adopted in many studies due to their compatibility with machine-learning workflows. Nevertheless, chemical compounds are inherently graph-based entities composed of atoms and bonds, and thus linear string formats cannot fully capture their topological and chemical properties. Consequently, new molecular representation paradigms are being developed to overcome these limitations and to better integrate molecular structures into computational and AI-driven discovery pipeline [14]. Molecular representation paradigms › String-based molecular representations

Early computational drug discovery commonly represented molecules as linear strings, enabling efficient storage, search, and large-scale processing. The Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) [15, 16] remains the most widely adopted format for encoding molecular graphs as machine-readable text (Fig. 1A), capturing atom and bond types, ring closures, branching, and stereochemical annotations. Despite its practicality, SMILES has important limitations for AI workflows, including non-unique string representations for the same molecule and syntactic fragility during molecular generation, which can lead to invalid outputs or overfitting to surface grammar rather than chemical constraints. To improve robustness in generative settings, modified string representations have been proposed. DeepSMILES [17] reduces syntactic fragility by reformulating ring and branch syntax, whereas SELFIES [18] enforces validity by design such that virtually any generated string maps to a chemically valid molecule, which is particularly useful for de novo generation and molecular optimization workflows [19]. Complementary to these generative representations, International Chemical Identifier (InChI) and its hashed counterpart, InChIKey [20, 21], function as standardized identifiers for compound registration and cross-database integration, supporting deduplication and consistent linking across heterogeneous sources. In addition, SMARTS remains important for encoding substructure and query patterns in cheminformatics. Recent breakthroughs in natural language processing (NLP) have enabled practical large language models (LLMs) and Transformer-based architectures, and these advances have rapidly expanded into the domain of drug discovery. In this paradigm, molecules are represented as sequences, most commonly SMILES or SELFIES, and treated as tokenized chemical sentences [22, 23]. Through self-attention and large-scale pretraining, these models learn transferable contextual embeddings for property prediction, bioactivity estimation, and molecular optimization. The potential of this approach is significantly amplified when integrated with generative models. The primary objective is to generate molecules with desired pharmacological profiles while ensuring novelty, meaning they should not merely rely on existing molecular scaffolds [24]. However, because string-based language models do not explicitly encode 3D geometry, their outputs often require downstream 3D-aware validation or refinement when structure-based reasoning is essential. Molecular representation paradigms › Graph-based molecular representations

Graph-based molecular representations, increasingly adopted in recent research, offer an intuitive yet mathematically consistent framework by encoding atoms and chemical bonds as nodes and edges, respectively. This approach represents a conceptual shift from string-based representations, which delineate molecules as linear sequences. Unlike linear string encodings, graph representations maintain explicit topological structure, including ring systems and bond connectivity, without relying on predefined hashing rules. Node features typically describe atomic attributes such as element type, formal charge, hybridization, and aromaticity, whereas edge features encode bond type, conjugation, and stereochemical information. In practice, graph representations specify molecular connectivity (often represented as an adjacency matrix) and associated feature matrices that serve as structured inputs to graph neural networks (GNNs) (Fig. 1C). By annotating nodes and edges with chemically meaningful features, graph-based representations retain multilevel information spanning local chemical environments and global structural patterns. The true power of graph-based representations emerges when coupled with GNNs. GNNs operate through iterative message passing, in which node embeddings are updated by aggregating information from neighboring nodes and edges across multiple layers [25]. This process enables the model to learn hierarchical chemical representations without relying on handcrafted descriptors. Importantly, graph formalisms naturally extend beyond isolated molecules to model protein–ligand systems. In structure-aware settings, proteins can be represented as residue-level or atom-level graphs, and heterogeneous graphs can integrate both ligand atoms and protein residues within a unified framework. Geometric GNN architectures further incorporate 3D coordinates and enforce equivariance to rotational and translational transformations, which are critical for modeling binding affinity and pose prediction tasks. These capabilities allow graph-based models to bridge molecular topology and spatial interaction geometry [25, 26], consequently forming the foundation of many state-of-the-art deep learning frameworks for drug design [27].

Molecular representation paradigms › Molecular descriptors

Molecular descriptors convert chemical structures and protein–ligand complexes into compact numerical representations that can be directly used by machine-learning models. Unlike graph-based representations, which preserve atoms and bonds as explicit nodes and edges, descriptors summarize predefined structural, chemical, physicochemical, or interaction-level properties into fixed-length feature vectors. Within this broad category, molecular fingerprints typically encode chemical structures as binary or count-based vectors, supporting fast similarity search, clustering, and scalable learning across large compound libraries (Fig. 1B) [28]. As summarized in Table 1, representative fingerprint types include substructure key-based methods, path-based or topological fingerprints, circular fingerprints such as ECFP/Morgan, and pharmacophore-style variants such as FCFP [29]. Despite their efficiency, classical molecular fingerprints are limited by their reliance on 2D topology and predefined feature patterns, and by their limited capacity to capture biological context, 3D geometry, or explicit protein–ligand interactions. To address these limitations, fingerprint-based representations have been extended to include structure- and interaction-aware encodings. Protein–Ligand Extended Connectivity (PLEC) fingerprints, for example, adapt the concept of circular fingerprints to protein–ligand interfaces by pairing extended-connectivity environments of ligand atoms with those of nearby protein atoms [30]. Thus, PLEC provides a natural transition from ligand-centered fingerprints to local chemical contexts surrounding protein–ligand contacts. This transition also connects molecular fingerprints to interaction descriptors used in structure-based scoring. RF-Score represents protein–ligand complexes as counts of close contacts between predefined protein and ligand atom types, enabling random forest-based prediction of binding affinity from structural interaction patterns [31]. However, simple atom-type contact counts may not fully distinguish local chemical environments. Extended Connectivity Interaction Features (ECIF) address this limitation by defining protein–ligand atom-pair features based on the connectivity environment of each atom, thereby providing a more chemically fine-grained description of intermolecular contacts [32]. More recent rescoring frameworks, such as SCORCH2 [33], further combine multiple interaction-based fingerprints, molecular descriptors, and heterogeneous XGBoost-based models through a consensus strategy to improve virtual screening performance [34]. These interaction-based descriptors are best understood as feature representations rather than predictive models in their own right. They encode protein–ligand interface information into fixed-length inputs that are consumed by downstream scoring functions and structure-aware interaction models. Protein structure revolution and AI

Drugs ultimately exert pharmacological effects through interactions with proteins, and protein structures provide a mechanistic basis for interpreting binding, selectivity, and downstream functions. In practice, small molecules often engage multiple targets, whereas closely related proteins can respond differently to the same ligand, which complicates structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis and lead optimization. These challenges have made accurate 3D protein information increasingly indispensable, particularly as modern structure-aware AI models require explicit structural context that cannot be fully captured by sequence- or string-based representations alone. Recent advances in structural biology and AI have reshaped this landscape. Improvements in experimental structure determination (X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM) have expanded the availability of high-quality protein structures, while AI-based prediction (AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold) has further broadened structural coverage for targets that remain difficult to characterize experimentally. As a result, protein structural data have become a practical foundation for structure-aware pipelines across key tasks, including structure prediction, docking/pose modeling, drug–target interaction prediction, binding affinity estimation, and molecular generation (Fig. 2). Protein structure revolution and AI › Experimental structure repository

High-resolution experimental structures remain the gold standard for structure-aware drug discovery because they directly provide atomic coordinates of proteins under defined experimental conditions. Such data establish the mechanistic foundation for interpreting binding modes, selectivity, and conformational dynamics. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) [35] serves as the central archive for experimentally determined macromolecular structures and has expanded substantially with advances in X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM [36]. As of October 2025, the PDB contains over 240 000 entries, with cryo-EM contributing an increasingly significant fraction of newly resolved structures. This expansion has improved structural coverage for challenging targets, including membrane proteins and large macromolecular assemblies that were historically underrepresented. These experimentally determined structures continue to function as critical references for validating AI-based predictions and for constructing downstream structure–ligand datasets used in training and benchmarking. In this sense, the PDB not only archives structural knowledge but also provides the empirical foundation for developing and evaluating structure-aware AI models.

Protein structure revolution and AI › Protein representation

As drug discovery research expands into in silico environments, proper protein representation has become essential. Similar to small molecules, proteins must have their physical and biochemical properties converted into computable formats. The most fundamental representation is the protein sequence, in which the 20 amino acids are encoded as one-letter symbols. These sequences are used in multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) to compare evolutionary conservation and covariation patterns among homologous proteins, enabling the inference of conserved functional residues, structural constraints, and potential inter-residue contacts. To represent spatial contacts and non-covalent interactions more explicitly, graph-based representations such as contact maps and residue interaction networks (RINs) are widely used. In these representations, residues are treated as nodes, while inter-residue distances, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, hydrophobic interactions, and other contacts are represented as edges [37]. Node features can further include biochemical properties such as charge, hydrophobicity, secondary structure, and solvent accessibility. Because proteins are flexible macromolecules rather than rigid structures, RIN-based representations can capture residue-level spatial relationships, mutation effects, structural movements, and long-range interaction pathways more effectively than sequence-only representations. Recently, representations that directly incorporate 3D geometric information, including Cα distances, bond angles, dihedral angles, local frames, and rotational data, have become increasingly important. Models that process 3D coordinates often employ E(3)- or SE(3)-equivariant architectures to ensure consistent predictions under rotational and translational transformations [38]. EGNN, SE(3)-Transformer [39], and GVP-GNN [40] are representative architectures designed to learn such spatial geometric information, reflecting broader efforts to encode macromolecular structures more realistically. However, proteins and peptide drugs contain far more atoms and degrees of freedom than small molecules, with substantial flexibility in both backbones and side chains. Therefore, representing them in the same manner as small molecules has inherent limitations. To address this, approaches such as Foldseek’s 3Di alphabet convert local 3D structural environments into 1D token sequences, improving the computational efficiency of structure search and representation learning [41].

Protein structure revolution and AI › Protein structure prediction methods and application database

Despite substantial progress in experimental structure determination, many therapeutically relevant proteins remain structurally unresolved, particularly those that are unstable, membrane-associated, or difficult to crystallize. This structural gap has constrained structure-based drug discovery, making protein structure prediction a crucial technology for expanding structural coverage beyond experimentally characterized targets. Recent AI-based models, including AlphaFold [42, 43] and RoseTTAFold [44], have transformed this field by using evolutionary and structural data to predict protein structures with near-experimental accuracy for many targets. By integrating MSAs, attention-based architectures, and end-to-end coordinate refinement, these models infer residue-residue relationships and 3D conformations with unprecedented reliability. RoseTTAFold, developed by the Baker laboratory, introduced a three-track neural network that explicitly couples 1D sequence features, 2D distance/contact maps, and 3D coordinates (Fig. 3B). This design enables iterative refinement of structural hypotheses and efficient structure modeling across diverse settings. RoseTTAFold All-Atom (RFAA) further extends this framework to assemblies containing non-protein components, including nucleic acids, small molecules, and metal ions, making it relevant for structure-aware pipelines involving ligands or cofactors [45]. AlphaFold, widely adopted since its release in 2021, marked a major turning point in protein structure prediction by leveraging MSAs and attention-based architectures to guide end-to-end structure generation (Fig. 3A). AlphaFold3 further expands this paradigm to biomolecular complexes, extending structure prediction beyond single proteins [46]. These advances have made predicted structures broadly accessible for downstream AI-driven drug discovery. AlphaFold DB [47] provides predicted 3D structures for nearly all cataloged proteins in the UniProt knowledgebase [48], covering over 200 million entries [49], and includes confidence metrics such as per-residue pLDDT and Predicted Aligned Error (PAE). These metrics help users assess model reliability and identify regions that require cautious interpretation, especially for targets that remain difficult to characterize experimentally. In parallel, open resources have improved reproducibility and community-driven development. OpenFold [50] is a PyTorch re-implementation of AlphaFold2 that supports retraining and customization, while OpenProteinSet [51] provides large-scale, ready-to-use, high-quality MSAs, including over 16 million alignments derived from resources such as the PDB and UniClust30 [52]. Together, these resources enable efficient prediction of long proteins and integration into diverse research pipelines. Nevertheless, predicted structures still require target-specific quality assessment, particularly for proteins with limited evolutionary information, low-homology regions, or biologically relevant alternative conformations. Therefore, experimental validation remains indispensable in high-stakes decision-making. Together, experimental and predicted structural resources have expanded the availability of protein structural context and enabled downstream datasets and benchmarks for structure-aware AI models. Protein structure revolution and AI › Training sets

Training and benchmarking structure-aware AI models require protein–ligand datasets that provide both interaction labels (e.g. binding affinities or activity readouts) and evaluation standards for generalization. In practice, these resources can be broadly grouped into (i) structure-linked complex datasets that pair 3D coordinates with experimental affinity measurements and (ii) large-scale bioactivity or screening benchmarks that expand coverage across targets and chemical space. PDBbind (reorganized as PDBbind+) is a widely used structure-linked resource that curates protein–ligand complexes from the PDB together with experimentally measured binding affinities, covering a diverse range of protein families [53–57]. The database is further organized into specialized subsets: the “General Set” for broad training, and the high-quality “Refined Set” and “Core Set” for rigorous testing and validation of scoring functions and AI models [58]. Complementary to structure-linked resources, BindingDB [59–61] aggregates experimentally measured protein–small molecule binding data at scale and provides broad target coverage, which is valuable for model training and cross-target evaluation. As of 2024, BindingDB houses over 2.9 million binding measurement values, covering more than 1.3 million compounds and thousands of protein targets. In addition to these experimental datasets, several databases have been developed specifically for virtual screening and machine-learning applications. For instance, LIT-PCBA was constructed to mitigate chemical biases inherent in existing ligand sets [62]. Based on 149 dose–response bioassays from PubChem, this dataset underwent extensive refinement and debiasing processes to achieve a balanced classification of active and inactive compounds for each target. Since its publication in 2020, this dataset has been employed in numerous studies [63–65]. However, recent reports have highlighted potential issues regarding data leakage and redundancy [66]. Recently, several datasets have been constructed to support protein–protein complex prediction. DIPS-Plus, an advanced iteration of the original DIPS dataset, was developed to learn the binding interfaces between proteins extracted from the PDB [67]. It allows complexes to be utilized at both the residue level and as graph representations, incorporating a diverse array of features to facilitate rich data learning. In addition to these advancements, continuous efforts are being made to expand the absolute scale of training data. The OpenFold self-distillation set, which provides ~270 000 pseudo-labeled data points, represents an attempt to overcome the data scale limitations inherent in the PDB by leveraging high-confidence structures predicted by OpenFold as training data for new models [50]. More recently, PINDER was introduced to mitigate data leakage; by accounting for structural similarities at the interface, it serves as a highly suitable dataset for training and benchmarking generalization performance [68]. Although these datasets greatly support model development across diverse settings, it remains essential to consider their biases, assay heterogeneity, and curation assumptions when interpreting benchmark performance and translating predictions to experiments.

Protein structure revolution and AI › Benchmarks

To advance AI-driven drug discovery, the importance of benchmarks capable of evaluating practical applicability is just as critical as having suitable training datasets. Driven by technological advancements, a wide range of docking software has been developed, spanning from classic open-source tools like AutoDock [69, 70] to commercial suites such as Schrödinger Glide [71]. Consequently, there is a growing demand for datasets tailored to evaluate the realistic performance of these diverse tools. As one of the most representative benchmarks, the accompanying Comparative Assessment of Scoring Functions (CASF) benchmark provides standardized evaluation metrics for scoring functions and comprises high-resolution crystal structures paired with experimentally validated binding affinity data such as Kd and Ki. Accordingly, CASF is specifically designed as a scoring benchmark to evaluate functions across diverse metrics, including scoring, ranking, docking, and screening powers [72, 73]. Widely adopted in numerous studies, CrossDocked2020 was developed to overcome the limitations of traditional re-docking benchmarks, which often overestimate generalization by evaluating ligands solely against their cognate receptors [74]. To address this, CrossDocked2020 incorporates ~22.5 million ligand poses generated by cross-docking into diverse protein pockets within the same family. By including non-cognate receptor-ligand pairs, the dataset provides a more realistic framework for training and evaluating docking models in conditions that better mirror actual drug discovery. In parallel, PoseBusters [75] serves as a benchmark focusing on assessing the physical plausibility and chemical consistency of predicted poses. While many AI-based docking models report strong performance under root-mean-square deviation (RMSD)-based evaluations, geometric agreement with a crystallographic reference does not necessarily ensure robustness across chemically diverse systems. Minor steric clashes or geometric distortions may not always be practically critical; however, models that perform well on benchmark sets can still exhibit reduced transferability when applied to ligands or binding contexts outside the training distribution. Comprising ~308 high-quality protein–ligand complexes, PoseBusters offers a comprehensive suite of metrics beyond RMSD, including PLIF recovery, ligand geometric validity, and contact integrity. These criteria enable a precise evaluation of the physical accuracy of AI-generated docking poses. Thus, PoseBusters complements large-scale benchmarks such as CrossDocked2020 by introducing additional criteria that probe physical realism and generalization beyond simple RMSD agreement.

Structure-aware AI models

Building upon advances in molecular representation, large-scale structural resources, and protein structure prediction, structure-aware AI models have emerged as central components of modern computational drug discovery. These models integrate 3D structural context into learning frameworks to address tasks spanning interaction prediction, docking, molecular design, and biomolecule engineering. However, the presence of 3D inputs alone does not guarantee meaningful structure-aware behavior. In practice, structure-awareness depends on whether geometric context constrains prediction or generation, rather than serving as auxiliary features that can be bypassed via dataset-specific shortcuts. Some approaches utilize structural descriptors at the feature level, whereas others enforce geometric consistency or interface-level constraints within the learning objective or architecture. This distinction is important when interpreting benchmark results and assessing real-world robustness. In drug discovery pipelines, these models are most commonly applied in early-stage research to prioritize targets, estimate binding affinity, propose docking configurations, and generate candidate molecules. Depending on the modeling objective, architectures span graph-based predictors of drug–target interactions, geometry-centric docking networks, and generative frameworks for de novo molecular or protein design. At a foundational level, many approaches are built upon core deep learning methods such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and GNNs, which differ in how structural information is encoded and propagated (Fig. 4). Structure-aware AI models › Drug–target interaction and binding affinity prediction

Interactions between drugs and their target proteins provide essential information for therapeutic development and serve as a primary determinant of target engagement and binding potency. However, it is physically infeasible to experimentally determine binding affinities for the vast number of compound–target pairs. Consequently, researchers have developed in silico models to infer drug–target interactions (DTIs) from existing experimental data. Traditional DTI models such as DeepDTA [76], GraphDTA [77], and GanDTI [78] primarily relied on molecular fingerprints of compounds and amino acid sequences of proteins to estimate binding likelihood. Although computationally efficient, these approaches do not fully capture the spatial complementarity that governs molecular recognition. PLANET [79] addresses this limitation by extracting residues within 5 Å of the ligand binding region and constructing contact maps to learn interaction patterns between ligand atoms and pocket residues. By jointly predicting interaction probabilities and binding affinities, PLANET improves interpretability by identifying residue- and ligand-level contributions while enabling rapid virtual screening. However, its accuracy remains comparable to conventional docking programs such as Glide, suggesting that interpretability and speed are achieved with limited improvement in absolute predictive performance [79]. To improve spatial reasoning, more recent models incorporate full 3D complex geometry to learn spatial and interaction features of protein binding pockets [80]. Physics-informed graph neural network (PIGNet) [81, 82] encodes 3D protein–ligand complex coordinates into molecular graphs and integrates physics-informed GNN components to enhance generalization. To address the limited availability of high-quality 3D complex data, PIGNet augments training data by generating random binding poses, partially reducing data scarcity and structural bias. More recently, iNGNN-DTI [83] integrates AlphaFold2 into the DTI framework by generating 3D protein structures from input sequences, calculating distance and contact maps, and applying them within a GNN framework. Although such GNN-based models often achieve strong benchmark performance, they can be computationally demanding for large-scale applications. SS-GNN [84] addresses this issue through a scalability-oriented design that constructs edges as a single undirected graph and simplifies the representation by disregarding intra-protein covalent bonds, while maintaining competitive performance through a hybrid GNN-MLP architecture. EquiScore [85] aims to address two complementary limitations of DTI models, namely training data quality and architectural representational capacity. To increase active-sample diversity, it reconstructs training data to reduce benchmark bias. Architecturally, it introduces interaction fingerprints (IFPs), aromatic virtual nodes for π interactions, and an interpretable attention mechanism that highlights residue- and atom-level contributions. It also adopts an equivariant architecture to ensure consistent predictions under rotation and translation, improving generalization across structurally diverse targets. RTMScore [86] takes a different strategy by learning the probability distribution of residue-ligand distances rather than directly regressing binding affinity, assigning higher scores to poses within physically natural distance ranges. Its equivariant backbone improves rotational invariance and produces nearly identical scores after arbitrary protein rotation, but limitations remain in absolute affinity prediction and generalization across target classes. To overcome these issues, GenScore [87] was developed as a successor to RTMScore. GenScore uses a two-stage training strategy, first learning the natural distribution of pose geometry and then fine-tuning for affinity prediction, thereby combining pose discrimination and affinity prediction within a unified framework.

Structure-aware AI models › Complex-centric docking/pose prediction

The complex-centric approach aims to learn directly from 3D protein–ligand complex structures and to predict binding poses, and in some cases binding affinities, within a unified framework. CarsiDock [88] addresses this task through large-scale data generation by creating ~9 million decoy docking complexes from ChemDiv compounds docked into PDBbind pockets using Glide. The model first learns features of Glide-generated docking poses and is then fine-tuned on experimentally determined crystal structures. Built on a Transformer-based architecture with triangle attention adopted from AlphaFold2, CarsiDock showed strong benchmark performance. However, because it embeds proteins and ligands separately and predicts their distance matrix, the complex is not fully modeled as a unified geometric system. Its reliance on Glide-derived pretraining data may also transmit docking-specific biases. Recent models increasingly treat protein–ligand complexes as single physical systems that jointly encode molecular geometry and interaction context. SE(3)-equivariant graph neural networks [89], as exemplified by EquiBind [90], preserve rotational and translational consistency and improve generalization across diverse complexes. Related approaches include TANKBind [91], which uses trigonometry-aware neural networks, and FABind+ [92], which improves pose prediction through dynamic pocket-radius prediction. DiffDock [93] further reframes docking as a diffusion-based generative problem. It models ligand binding as a probabilistic denoising process from random initial poses to final conformations in a product space of translational, rotational, and torsional degrees of freedom. Because it samples only these ligand degrees of freedom, DiffDock treats the receptor as rigid and does not explicitly model protein side-chain flexibility. This design enables robust pose prediction even with imperfect protein structures and provides confidence scores for pose prioritization. DiffDock and related methods are generally categorized as sampling-based approaches, in contrast to earlier regression-based models such as EquiBind and TANKBind. Although sampling-based methods often improve pose accuracy, they usually require higher computational cost. DynamicBind [94] extends DiffDock’s SE(3)-equivariant diffusion framework to model protein conformational flexibility and large-scale structural rearrangements under more realistic conditions. It has been applied to practical cases such as SETD2 cryptic pocket prediction and has been reported to outperform DiffDock and Glide in benchmark evaluations. However, its accuracy can decrease for novel proteins with limited structural or interaction information. SurfDock [95] was subsequently developed to address these limitations while retaining an SE(3)-equivariant diffusion framework. Unlike DynamicBind, which explicitly assigns flexibility to protein backbones and side chains, SurfDock incorporates implicit flexibility by emphasizing ligand-adjacent protein surface features. This surface-focused design supports rapid inference, physical plausibility, and strong virtual screening performance, although its applicability to very large proteins may remain limited. NeuralPLexer [96] addresses receptor flexibility through a multiscale diffusion model that jointly samples apo- and holo-state ensembles and recovers flexible binding-site conformations rather than assuming a single fixed structure. Recently, co-folding approaches have also attracted increasing attention for supporting next-generation structure-aware drug discovery. Models such as Chai-1 [97], AlphaFold3 [46], and Boltz-2 [98] integrate pose prediction into unified structure prediction frameworks and generate protein–ligand complex structures from sequence and molecular graph inputs without requiring a predefined binding pocket. These models commonly combine a Transformer-based trunk with a diffusion module for 3D coordinate generation and can handle complexes involving not only small molecules but also nucleic acids or peptides. Although these models achieve competitive pose accuracy on standard benchmarks, their performance tends to degrade under more stringent evaluation settings. Because they generate coordinates through learned diffusion processes rather than explicit energy functions, their confidence scores do not necessarily guarantee physically plausible geometries. Accordingly, pose accuracy on physically rigorous and leakage-controlled benchmarks often remains moderate [75, 99]. More importantly, when training–test splits are designed to avoid data leakage, the virtual screening performance of co-folding and deep learning docking tools tends to become considerably more modest, particularly for targets that are underrepresented in the training data [100]. Therefore, beyond the widely discussed speed–accuracy trade-off [101], a more fundamental concern lies in the physical validity of generated poses and, above all, the reliability of prospective virtual screening performance. It should also be noted that, although these approaches show promising technical potential, their prospective performance has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated. In contrast, more established machine-learning scoring functions have shown strong practical performance, as evidenced by several successful applications [102–104]. For example, in the largest prospective virtual screening campaign reported to date, the CNN-based AtomNet identified novel hits for 235 of 318 targets (~74%) spanning diverse protein classes [105]. In particular, established structure-based strategies remain highly effective for exploiting the rapidly expanding synthesis-on-demand chemical space. Chemical space docking has generated prospective hits from billion-scale compound spaces by evaluating reaction-based combinatorial products without exhaustive enumeration [106], while analog-search approaches can bridge generative models with such spaces to efficiently recover synthetically accessible leads [107]. Considering that the ultimate goal is real-world success, it may be advantageous to actively leverage not only the latest technologies but also established approaches with demonstrated prospective performance.

Structure-aware AI models › Molecular generation

One of the key objectives of molecular generation models is to design physically plausible molecules with optimal binding affinity toward target proteins. Beyond learning and prediction from existing data, AI models have increasingly expanded toward the de novo generation of novel chemical entities. These strategies can be broadly organized along two axes: how target context is incorporated, such as structure-conditioned generation using 3D pocket geometry or language- and token-based molecular generation, and how molecules are generated, including string-based outputs such as SMILES or 3D coordinate-based construction. Early structure-conditioned approaches generated molecules directly within the spatial context of protein binding pockets. GraphBP [108, 109] and Pocket2Mol [110] represent protein pocket atoms and ligand atoms as graphs and use autoregressive atom-by-atom sampling to construct molecules that satisfy geometric and chemical complementarity (Fig. 5). Although effective in capturing local pocket shape, these methods face challenges in global molecular validity, drug-likeness, and synthetic accessibility. To incorporate richer structural context, SurfGen [111] directly uses molecular surface topology rather than relying only on pocket atomic coordinates. It employs two equivariant neural networks, Geodesic-GNN and Geoatom-GNN, to learn topological interactions on pocket surfaces and spatial interactions between ligand atoms and surface nodes, thereby capturing surface-shape complementarity more sensitively. PIDiff [112] further introduces dynamic and physical information by using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation data and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [113]. Based on the principle that stable protein–ligand binding occurs where binding free energy is minimized, PIDiff improves performance on the CrossDocked2020 [74] benchmark and highlights the value of physically grounded dynamic information in structure-based generation. Several models have also focused on the generative algorithm itself. DiffSBDD [114] is an SE(3)-equivariant diffusion model that adopts a non-autoregressive strategy, denoising all ligand atoms simultaneously from a noisy initial state rather than generating them sequentially. This enables more coherent global molecular construction and reduces error accumulation. DiffGui [115] extends diffusion-based generation by jointly diffusing atoms and bonds, helping to reduce unrealistic ring formation and distorted bond construction. By incorporating property guidance such as binding affinity and drug-likeness during training and sampling, DiffGui generates molecules that better satisfy both chemical validity and desired drug-like properties. ED2Mol [116] uses electron density (ED) as a generative signal. After predicting pseudo-ligand electron density from the pocket structure, it places core fragments and expands them through a fragment-based strategy, achieving high physical reliability and supporting automated lead optimization. Despite these advances, 3D structure-based generative models still share several limitations, including limited high-quality protein–ligand complex data, reliance on static pocket structures, and the uncertain translation of benchmark improvements into synthetic accessibility or experimentally validated activity. To address data sparsity and improve scalability, GPT-inspired molecular generation models have recently emerged (Fig. 6). 3DSMILES-GPT [117] adopts a GPT-like architecture that tokenizes SMILES together with 3D coordinates of the protein pocket surface, pretrains on 10 million PubChem [118] drug-like molecules, and fine-tunes on selected CrossDocked2020 poses. BindGPT [119] pretrains ligand and pocket information as separate token sequences and combines them during fine-tuning, enabling the generation of molecular graphs and 3D conformations after training on ~90 billion tokens. Token-Mol 1.0 [120] avoids long Cartesian coordinate sequences and equivariance constraints by tokenizing torsion angles, which are invariant to rotation and translation. Pretrained on 8.45 million conformers from the GEOM dataset and fine-tuned on CrossDocked2020, Token-Mol 1.0 supports more efficient and flexible molecular generation. In addition to unconstrained de novo generation, structure-aware generative models are increasingly being used for molecular optimization tasks, including scaffold hopping, fragment growing, linker design, substructure inpainting, and property-guided lead refinement. These tasks differ from de novo generation because they aim to preserve key elements of an existing chemotype or binding mode while improving potency, selectivity, synthetic accessibility, or ADMET-relevant properties. In this context, structure-aware optimization can serve as a bridge between initial molecule generation and lead refinement by incorporating binding-site constraints, ligand geometry, and medicinal chemistry objectives into the design process. Nevertheless, generative molecular design still faces a gap between computational generation and experimental synthesis. Molecules with high predicted binding affinity or favorable objective scores may have low synthetic accessibility or lack feasible retrosynthetic routes, limiting their progression to experimental validation [121]. Generating molecules that are difficult or impossible to synthesize is therefore a recognized failure mode of generative AI in de novo molecular design [122]. To address this limitation, increasing efforts have focused on integrating synthetic accessibility prediction, retrosynthesis planning, and reaction-aware generation into AI-driven design workflows [123–126]. These developments highlight that practical drug design is not a single-step generation task, but a multi-step optimization process that links molecular design, synthetic feasibility, and experimental validation. Structure-aware AI models › Biomolecule design

Recent research has increasingly moved beyond small-molecule design toward the engineering of biomolecules, including proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids, that possess high-dimensional structures and substantial conformational flexibility. These targets introduce large combinatorial search spaces and multiscale interactions, making design computationally demanding and experimentally difficult to validate. Importantly, the structure-aware machinery developed for small-molecule tasks, in particular, the geometric representations and SE(3)-equivariant architectures described in subsection Protein Representation, carries over directly to this setting, with the key conceptual shift that the protein is no longer only a binding target but becomes the object being generated. Current strategies are commonly organized according to what is generated and what is conditioned, spanning backbone generation, sequence design, and joint all-atom co-design under functional, stability, or binding constraints. In practice, many workflows follow an iterative generate-design-filter loop, in which a backbone generator proposes candidate scaffolds, a sequence model assigns amino acids, and a structure predictor evaluates plausibility for filtering and experimental prioritization (Fig. 7). Backbone generation addresses the central question of which 3D scaffold can realize a desired interface or functional geometry. Early approaches repurposed structure prediction networks through activation maximization, hallucinating sequences whose predicted structures matched target constraints [127]. Diffusion-based generation subsequently became the dominant paradigm. RFdiffusion [128, 129] repurposes the denoising capacity of RoseTTAFold [44] to iteratively transform noise into plausible backbones consistent with user-defined constraints such as interface geometry, motif placement, or symmetry. Chroma [130] offers a parallel programmable diffusion framework that jointly generates backbones and sequences while supporting a range of structural and functional conditioning. More specialized extensions target functional precision: RFdiffusion2 [128] scaffolds enzyme active sites by placing catalytic residues without requiring their sequence positions to be specified in advance, addressing a long-standing bottleneck in de novo enzyme design. Given a generated backbone, sequence design, also referred to as inverse folding, assigns an amino acid sequence predicted to fold into that structure. ProteinMPNN [131], a message-passing inverse-folding model, has become the standard component for this step, outperforming physics-based methods in both speed and sequence recovery, and is routinely paired with RFdiffusion in de novo design pipelines. ESM-IF [132] extends inverse folding by training on millions of predicted structures, improving robustness when experimental structures are scarce. Of particular relevance to structure-aware drug discovery, LigandMPNN [133] conditions sequence design on non-protein context, including small-molecule ligands, nucleic acids, and metal cofactors, allowing binding-site residues to be designed in the explicit presence of the molecule they must accommodate. This capability directly connects biomolecule design back to small-molecule recognition, unifying the two halves of the structure-aware design problem. Beyond modular pipelines, integrated frameworks increasingly unify structure generation and conditioning within a single all-atom process, mirroring the convergence already observed in co-folding predictors. RFdiffusion3 [134] performs diffusion directly at the atomic level across backbone and side-chain atoms, generating proteins together with the ligands, nucleic acids, and other non-protein partners they interact with, while reducing computational cost relative to earlier residue-level approaches. BoltzGen [135] provides an all-atom generative framework that jointly models proteins with nucleic acids or ligands and has been evaluated on targets with limited similarity to known complexes to probe generalization, with selected designs advanced to wet-lab validation. Chai-2 [136] is more specifically oriented toward zero-shot de novo design of antibodies and protein binders; given a target structure and a small set of epitope residues, it jointly generates sequences and all-atom structures of scFvs, VHHs, and mini-binders and prioritizes a small number of candidates for testing, reportedly achieving an antibody hit rate of ~16% against novel targets lacking known binders. Conceptually, these models represent the generative counterpart of co-folding predictors such as AlphaFold3, Boltz-2, and Chai-1, indicating that structure prediction and molecular generation are converging into a single all-atom modeling paradigm spanning small molecules, proteins, and their complexes. This convergence is accompanied by a broadening range of design targets. Antibody and nanobody design remains a distinct and difficult subproblem because activity is governed largely by the conformationally variable complementarity-determining regions. Alongside Chai-2, structure-aware approaches such as antigen-conditioned diffusion models generate CDR sequences and conformations directly against a target epitope [137], while end-to-end full-atom models co-design the sequence and structure of the antigen-binding interface [138]. Peptide binder design has emerged as a further frontier, with diffusion-based methods extended to generate short binders against defined protein surfaces [139]. Together with the nucleic acid and ligand-binding capabilities of the all-atom frameworks described above, these developments point to a gradual generalization from designing a single protein toward designing arbitrary biomolecular binders against arbitrary targets. Despite these advances, biomolecule generation faces practical failure modes that are often underrepresented in benchmarks. Generative models may produce hallucinated interfaces that appear plausible in silico but fail to maintain stable binding or specificity under conformational heterogeneity and solvent effects, and reported design success rates remain low unless candidates are aggressively filtered using structure-prediction-based self-consistency checks [140]. Optimization objectives may also overemphasize structural stability while neglecting functional requirements such as catalytic geometry or induced-fit compatibility. Finally, developability factors, including expression yield, aggregation propensity, immunogenicity risk, and manufacturability, are frequently considered only at late filtering stages [141]. These limitations reinforce the broader evaluation and translatability challenges discussed in section Challenges and Future Directions, and they highlight the need to incorporate multi-objective constraints earlier in the design loop and to evaluate candidates under more realistic experimental criteria. Challenges and future directions

Despite rapid advances in structure-aware AI, a persistent gap remains between benchmark performance, in silico predictions, and prospective experimental success. This disconnect is not primarily architectural; rather, it stems from systemic limitations in data, supervision, evaluation, interpretability, and experimental translatability that constrain the practical utility of current models. Below, we examine these challenges and outline strategies for addressing them.

Challenges and future directions › Structural coverage and data quality

Structural repositories such as the PDB remain indispensable, yet coverage is intrinsically skewed toward experimentally tractable proteins, stable conformations, and successful ligand-bound complexes. Difficult targets, transient states, cryptic pockets, and weak or failed binding events are systematically underrepresented. Predicted structures have expanded access but remain less reliable for low-homology folds, flexible regions, and alternative conformations; these uncertainties propagate into structure-conditioned learning and can misdirect downstream inference. Addressing these gaps will require investment in datasets that better reflect the biological and chemical diversity encountered in drug discovery, including expanded representation of alternative conformations, transient states, and targets from underrepresented protein families. Equally important, modeling frameworks should incorporate uncertainty-aware strategies (such as ensemble conditioning, confidence-weighted training, or region-specific masking) to prevent low-confidence structural regions from being absorbed as reliable signals. Routine annotation of structure provenance, confidence metrics (e.g. pLDDT, PAE), and conformational variability should accompany datasets to enable informed model training and evaluation.

Challenges and future directions › Negative supervision and label ambiguity

Available datasets disproportionately report positive or high-affinity interactions, whereas true non-binders, failed cases, and inconclusive outcomes are rarely captured systematically. This imbalance, particularly the lack of reliable negative data, can inflate benchmark performance while reducing real-world discrimination, calibration, and robustness under distribution shift. Future resources should therefore include clearly defined inactive compounds, hard negatives that appear likely to bind but show no actual activity, and uncertain measurements, together with assay-aware labeling and clearer task definitions. Standardized protocols for distinguishing genuine non-binders from untested compounds and for handling assay-dependent variability, such as IC50, Kd, and Ki, would improve the reliability of both training signals and performance interpretation. Without such refinements, models may learn decision boundaries shaped by reporting biases rather than by the biophysics of molecular recognition. Not all negatives are equally informative. Property-matched decoys, such as those in DUD-E, are inexpensive to assemble but can often be separated from active compounds on the basis of gross physicochemical differences alone, which inflates apparent discrimination without testing binding-site recognition. Hard negatives represent the opposite case: compounds that closely resemble true binders in size, shape, and physicochemical profile yet are experimentally confirmed to be inactive, such as inactive analogs within a congeneric series or property-matched decoys that have been verified by assay rather than merely assumed to be inactive. Because they cannot be ruled out by global molecular features, hard negatives force a model to learn the specific geometric and chemical determinants of binding rather than relying on dataset-level shortcuts. Their role is therefore both diagnostic and corrective: discrimination measured against hard negatives is a far stronger indicator of prospective virtual-screening utility than performance against random or property-mismatched decoys, and training on them reduces the risk that a model appears successful on benchmarks for reasons unrelated to molecular recognition.

Challenges and future directions › Evaluation of rigor and benchmarking standards

Reported gains on curated benchmarks do not necessarily translate into reliable prospective performance. No widely accepted standard benchmark currently ensures structural diversity, realistic task formulation, and consistent preprocessing, leaving cross-study comparisons fragmented and vulnerable to dataset-specific artifacts. Evaluation criteria should extend beyond aggregate accuracy to emphasize calibration, robustness under distribution shift, and prospective enrichment. Community convergence on leakage-resistant data splits, standardized preprocessing pipelines, and transparent reporting of training-set overlap and hyperparameter tuning would improve reproducibility and comparability across studies. Furthermore, benchmarks should be designed to test generalization along axes most relevant to drug discovery, including novel protein folds, chemotypes absent from the training set, and clinically motivated endpoints, rather than relying on random splits that allow memorization of dataset-specific patterns.

Challenges and future directions › Mechanistic interpretability

Many deep learning models achieve strong accuracy but provide limited rationale for which structural determinants drive their outputs. Without transparent attribution, predictions offer limited guidance for molecular optimization and experimental prioritization. As a result, AI systems may become high-performing but low-trust tools that support, rather than drive, decision-making in discovery workflows. Interpretability must, therefore, evolve from descriptive attribution to mechanistically grounded insight. Explanations that merely highlight attention weights or feature importance provide limited value unless they generate experimentally testable hypotheses and remain stable across plausible conformations. Structured intermediate representations, such as interaction fingerprints, residue-level contribution analyses, or physics-informed energy decompositions, may help connect predictions to modifiable structural determinants that medicinal chemists can act upon. Closer integration with medicinal chemistry workflows will be necessary to ensure that model outputs inform rational optimization rather than remain analytical artifacts.

Challenges and future directions › In silico-experimental gap

Despite advances in structure-aware AI, a substantial gap remains between in silico prediction and experimental validation. Many models prioritize predicted binding affinity, docking accuracy, or benchmark performance, but these metrics do not guarantee that candidate molecules can actually be synthesized or validated in appropriate biological assays. In particular, de novo molecular generation may produce compounds with favorable predicted binding profiles but low synthetic accessibility or no feasible retrosynthetic route. Reducing this gap will require models that incorporate optimization processes such as synthetic accessibility prediction, retrosynthesis analysis, and ADMET filtering. Through these optimization steps, AI should be applied not only to generate promising molecules, but also to anticipate and mitigate potential experimental failures, such as poor solubility, metabolic instability, off-target effects, and assay interference. Furthermore, within iterative design-make-test-analyze cycles, computational predictions can be continuously improved through experimental feedback. Such integration is essential for ensuring that high-scoring molecules move beyond structural plausibility and become chemically actionable and experimentally testable candidates. Together, these challenges create a systemic barrier to integrating structure-aware AI as a reliable component of practical drug discovery. They span biased structural coverage, incomplete supervision, fragmented evaluation, insufficient mechanistic transparency, and the persistent gap between in silico prediction and experimental validation. Addressing them in concert, rather than in isolation, will determine whether the field can transition from benchmark-driven development to experimentally grounded, chemically actionable, and decision-relevant modeling.

Conclusion

In this review, we have provided a critical overview of structure-aware AI across key stages of drug discovery, from molecular representation and structural data resources to interaction prediction, docking, molecular generation and optimization, and biomolecule design. Graph neural networks, diffusion models, and Transformer-based architectures now routinely incorporate 3D protein context into learning frameworks and perform strongly on established benchmarks. However, practical therapeutic impact requires sustained attention to upstream and downstream challenges that lie beyond model architecture. Data quality, structural coverage, negative supervision, evaluation rigor, mechanistic interpretability, and experimental translatability collectively determine whether AI-driven predictions can guide experimental decision-making. Benchmark performance alone is an insufficient proxy for prospective utility, and the field must develop evaluation paradigms that assess generalization, physical plausibility, synthetic feasibility, and design relevance under realistic conditions. With continued investment in data curation, supervision design, community-driven benchmarking standards, interpretable modeling, and integration with design-make-test-analyze cycles, structure-aware AI can mature from a supportive computational tool into a dependable component of next-generation drug discovery workflows.
