Abstract

Tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase I (TDP1) repairs topoisomerase I (TOP1)-mediated DNA damage and is a promising anticancer target, particularly in combination with TOP1 inhibitors. However, the discovery of potent and drug-like TDP1 inhibitors remains challenging due to the limited structural diversity of known active compounds. Here, we developed an integrated computational framework combining machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and structure-based docking with experimental validation. A curated dataset of 2040 compounds (857 active, 1183 inactive) was assembled and analyzed by scaffold composition. A total of 40 binary classification models were constructed using six ML algorithms and a deep neural network (DNN), each paired with five molecular fingerprint representations, along with five graph neural network architectures (GCN, GAT, MPNN, AttentiveFP, and FPGNN). The SVM::RDKitDes model performed best (AUC = 0.89, F1 = 0.78, BA = 0.80), with robustness confirmed by Y-scrambling and randomized-split analyses, and SHAP analysis identified 20 key descriptors of TDP1 inhibition. The model was deployed as a web application (http://drugpred.top:5050) and standalone desktop applications (.exe) are available at https://github.com/zenghuang8006/TDP1-inhibitor-prediction. The validated model was applied to screen 201 231 compounds, followed by drug-likeness filtering and hierarchical docking, yielding 16 candidates. Biological evaluation identified compound AO65 as a potent TDP1 inhibitor (IC50 = 0.80 ± 0.02 µM), and quantum chemical calculations and docking elucidated its electronic properties and binding within the catalytic domain. This work demonstrates the value of integrating ML-driven prediction with structure-based approaches and identifies AO65 as a promising lead for further TDP1-focused investigation.

Introduction

Tumor drug resistance is one of the key factors responsible for the failure of chemotherapeutic agents.1 Drug resistance can arise through multiple molecular mechanisms, including enhanced DNA damage repair, drug efflux, apoptosis evasion, and epigenetic modifications, ultimately leading to treatment failure and disease relapse.2 Topoisomerase I (TOP1) inhibitors, such as the camptothecin derivatives topotecan and irinotecan, are a clinically important class of anticancer agents widely used for the treatment of colorectal, ovarian, and small cell lung cancers.3 These drugs exert their cytotoxic effects by stabilizing the covalent TOP1–DNA cleavage complexes (TOP1cc), thereby converting the normally transient catalytic intermediates into persistent DNA lesions that interfere with replication and transcription, ultimately triggering cell death.4,5 Despite their demonstrated clinical efficacy, the therapeutic benefit of TOP1 inhibitors is substantially limited by the development of drug resistance, in which the cellular DNA repair machinery plays a critical role.6,7 Tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1) is one of the key repair enzymes responsible for resolving TOP1-mediated DNA damage.8,9 TDP1 is a highly conserved eukaryotic DNA repair enzyme, TDP1 plays a pivotal role in resolving abortive TOP1–DNA covalent complexes by cleaving the phosphodiester bond between the tyrosine residue of trapped TOP1 and the 3′-end of DNA, thereby initiating the repair of TOP1-mediated DNA damage.10,11 TDP1 deficiency or knockout leads to marked hypersensitivity to camptothecin and its derivatives,12,13 whereas TDP1 overexpression in tumor cells counteracts TOP1 inhibitor-induced DNA damage and diminishes drug efficacy.14 These findings establish TDP1 as a key determinant of tumor resistance to TOP1 inhibitor-based chemotherapy. Consequently, pharmacological inhibition of TDP1 has been proposed as a viable strategy to sensitize cancer cells to TOP1 inhibitors, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy in combination regimens.15,16 A diverse array of TDP1 inhibitors has been reported (Fig. 1), largely comprising natural products and their derivatives. Representative examples include the coumarin derivative 1 (ref 17) (IC50 = 1.1 µM), the benzophenanthroline derivative 2 (ref 18) (IC50 = 8.2 µM), aurintricarboxylic acid 3 (ATA)19 (IC50 = 76 nM), suramin 4 (ref 20) (IC50 = 5 µM), and the indenoisoquinoline derivative 5 (ref 21) (IC50 = 1.52 µM). Nevertheless, the development of TDP1 inhibitors remains at an early stage, with no candidate having advanced to clinical trials to date.22 The major limitations of these compounds include suboptimal potency and selectivity, insufficient drug-likeness, and a lack of systematic structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies across diverse chemical scaffolds. Most importantly, no reliable computational models have yet been established to characterize and rationalize the structural features of known inhibitors. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel strategies to accelerate the discovery of structurally diverse and pharmacologically viable TDP1 inhibitors. In recent years, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have emerged as powerful tools in drug discovery, offering unprecedented capabilities for predicting molecular bioactivity, prioritizing lead compounds, and accelerating virtual screening campaigns.23,24 The integration of ML/DL-based predictive models with structure-based molecular docking has proven to be an effective strategy for improving the efficiency and hit rates of virtual screening.25,26 Importantly, model interpretability methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) have been increasingly adopted to elucidate the key molecular features driving model predictions, thereby providing mechanistic insights that can guide rational drug design.27 However, despite the growing application of ML/DL approaches across diverse therapeutic targets, the systematic use of these computational methodologies for the discovery of TDP1 inhibitors remains largely unexplored. This gap highlights the need for an integrated computational framework that combines multiple ML and DL algorithms with diverse molecular representations for TDP1 inhibitor prediction, coupled with experimental verification to confirm the practical utility of the identified compounds. In this study, we developed a comprehensive computational framework integrating ML, DL, and structure-based molecular docking, coupled with experimental validation, for the discovery of novel TDP1 inhibitors. Specifically, multiple ML/DL algorithms were each paired with various molecular fingerprint representations to construct and benchmark a panel of predictive models. The optimal model was then applied to a large-scale virtual screening campaign, followed by hierarchical molecular docking, to prioritize candidate TDP1 inhibitors. Finally, the prioritized compounds were subjected to biological evaluation, leading to the identification of novel TDP1 lead compounds.

Experimental protocols › Data collection and preparation

The data used in this study was sourced from the ChEMBL database (version ChEMBL34), and several filtration steps were applied to ensure the quality and relevance of the compounds. The following selection criteria were implemented: metal-containing compounds were excluded to focus on non-metallic compounds. Duplicate entries were removed to avoid redundancy. Only compounds classified as “Potency” under the “Standard Type” category were retained. Entries with a “Standard Relation” other than “ = ” were excluded to ensure that all potency values corresponded to a direct equality relation. Only compounds associated with Type B assays (defined as experiments measuring compound-target inhibitory activity relevant to TDP1) were included in the analysis. Compounds with a molecular weight exceeding 1000 g mol−1 were excluded. Those annotated with “Inconclusive” comments were removed from consideration. Compounds annotated with the comment “Active” were assigned a class label of 1. After removal of duplicate entries, this yielded 857 unique active compounds. Compounds annotated with the comment “Not Active” initially comprised 5715 entries. The resulting ratio between active and inactive compounds was therefore highly skewed. Such pronounced class imbalance is a well-recognized problem in QSAR and bioactivity classification, because most ML algorithms are designed under the assumption of an approximately equal class distribution and consequently become biased toward the majority (inactive) class, leading to poor sensitivity toward the minority (active) class, distorted decision boundaries, and misleadingly optimistic overall accuracy that masks weak minority-class performance.28,29 To mitigate this imbalance, we adopted a stricter definition of the inactive class: only compounds annotated as “Not Active” and exhibiting activity values greater than 30 µM were retained as inactive compounds. This criterion reduced the inactive set to a final count of 1183 compounds. The combined curation procedure resulted in a balanced, curated dataset comprising 2040 compounds in total (857 active and 1183 inactive), which was subsequently used for binary classification modeling using ML and DL approaches.

Experimental protocols › Dataset splitting

For the six classical ML algorithms—logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), naïve Bayes (NB), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), a nine-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. The full dataset was first split into a training set (1836 compounds) and a test set (204 compounds) at a 9 : 1 ratio. The training set was further partitioned into nine subsets, of which eight were sequentially used as the sub-training set and the remaining one served as the validation set in each fold. Through iterative training and validation across all folds, the optimal hyperparameter combinations were identified for each model. For the six DL models—deep neural network (DNN), graph convolutional network (GCN), graph attention network (GAT), message passing neural network (MPNN), AttentiveFP, and fingerprint-enhanced graph neural network (FPGNN), a fixed validation set was used in place of cross-validation. This choice was motivated by the considerably larger number of trainable parameters in DL architectures compared to classical ML models, which makes repeated cross-validation computationally prohibitive. The fixed validation set served to monitor overfitting and guide hyperparameter tuning throughout the training process. To ensure fair comparison across all models, the test set was kept identical to that used for the ML models (204 compounds). The ML training set (1836 compounds) was further randomly split at a 9 : 1 ratio to yield a DL training set (1632 compounds) and a fixed validation set (204 compounds).

Experimental protocols › Calculation of molecular representations

We selected the following molecular descriptors, molecular fingerprints, and graph-based representations. Molecular fingerprints include: AtomPairs (2048 bits),30 constructed based on atom pair types and topological distances in molecules; MACCS (167 bits),31 containing 166 predefined key molecular structure fragments (with 1 bit serving as a placeholder); Morgan (1024 bits),24,32 which performs hash encoding of atoms based on circular topological environments (typically with a radius of 2–3); PharmacoPFP (39 bits),33 focusing on molecular pharmacophore features; RDKitDes (210 bits), which integrates molecular physicochemical properties (e.g., molecular weight, log P) and structural features. Graph-based representations were constructed using the DeepChem 2.8.0 toolkit, specifically including: GCN representation:34 molecular structures were processed using ConvMolFeaturizer, and GCN were employed to achieve layer-wise propagation and aggregation of local structural features of molecules; GAT representation: molecular features were extracted via MolGraphConvFeaturizer, and a classification model was built based on GAT, which utilizes attention mechanisms to capture important associations between nodes in molecular graphs;35 MPNN representation:36 molecular information was extracted using MolGraphConvFeaturizer with edge features, and MPNN were used to simulate the message passing process between nodes in molecular graphs; AttentiveFP representation:37 an AttentiveFP model was constructed for classification tasks, which combines attention mechanisms and graph neural networks to capture dynamic associations between global and local features of molecules; FP-GNN representation:38 it integrates molecular fingerprints with graph neural network architectures, and achieves representation learning by combining molecular topological features and graph structural information.

Experimental protocols › Construction of ML and DL models

To systematically evaluate the predictive performance of different molecular representations and learning algorithms, a total of 40 classification models were constructed by combining seven ML algorithms and five DL architectures with various molecular feature inputs. Six classical ML algorithms (LR, KNN, NB, RF, SVM, XGBoost)—along with DNN, were each paired with five molecular fingerprint representations: AtomPairs, PharmacoPFP, MACCS keys, Morgan fingerprints, and RDKit descriptors (RDKitDes), yielding 35 fingerprint-based models. In addition, five graph-based DL models (GCN, GAT, MPNN, AttentiveFP, FPGNN) were trained directly on molecular graph representations, producing five additional models. For each model, hyperparameters were optimized via grid search with five-fold cross-validation on the training set. Experimental protocols › SHAP-based model interpretation

To interpret the feature contributions of the optimal SVM model trained on RDKit descriptors (SVM::RDKitDes), SHAP analysis was performed. SHAP decomposes the prediction for each sample into additive feature contributions based on the following principle:yi = ybase + f(xi1) + f(xi2) + ⋯ + f(xik)where ybase is the expected model output over the training set, and f(xik) represents the SHAP value quantifying the contribution of the k-th feature to the prediction of sample i. Due to the kernel-based nature of the SVM model, the model-agnostic KernelExplainer from the SHAP Python library was employed. To reduce computational cost, the training data was summarized into 10 representative clusters using k-means clustering, which served as the background dataset for the explainer. SHAP values were then computed for all training samples with 1000 perturbation samples (nsamples = 1000) to ensure stable approximations. The resulting SHAP values were used to generate summary plots to identify the most influential molecular descriptors driving the classification.

Experimental protocols › Structure-based virtual screening

A total of 201 231 compounds were obtained from the D008 compound library (TargetMol). All structure-based virtual screening was performed using the Schrödinger software suite (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY). The crystal structure of the target protein (PDB ID: 6DJJ) was first preprocessed using the Protein Preparation Wizard module, which included the addition of hydrogen atoms, reconstruction of missing residue side chains, removal of crystallographic water molecules, optimization of the hydrogen-bonding network, and restrained energy minimization under the OPLS_2005 force field. A receptor grid was then generated using the Receptor Grid Generation module, centered on the co-crystallized ligand binding site, with the inner box dimensions set to the default size of 15 Å × 15 Å × 15 Å; all other parameters were kept at their default values. For ligand preparation, the compound library was processed using the LigPrep module to generate three-dimensional conformations and perform energy minimization, with possible ionization states and tautomers enumerated at physiological pH (7.0 ± 2.0). The compound library was pre-filtered according to Lipinski's Rule of Five and further refined using a reactive group filter to exclude molecules containing potentially reactive functional groups, yielding a total of 16 071 compounds for screening. Molecular docking was carried out using the Glide module in a three-tier hierarchical strategy: the first tier employed high-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) mode, retaining the top 10% of compounds ranked by docking score; the second tier applied standard precision (SP) mode to re-dock the hits from the previous stage, again retaining the top 10%; and the third tier utilized extra precision (XP) mode for refined docking and scoring of the remaining compounds. Throughout the docking process, ligands were treated as flexible, allowing free rotation and translation within the binding pocket. This stepwise screening workflow ultimately yielded 16 candidate compounds.

Experimental protocols › TDP1 inhibition assay

The inhibitory activity against TDP1 was evaluated using a fluorescence quenching-based kinetic assay. A single-stranded oligonucleotide substrate labeled with 6-FAM at the 5′ end and BHQ-1 at the 3′ end (5′-/5,6 FAM-aacgtcagggtcttcc-BHQ1/-3′)9,39 was prepared as a 100 nM stock solution in reaction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5). The assay was performed in 384-well black microplates. Each well received 20 µL of the substrate solution, 1 µL of the test compound dissolved in DMSO, and 19 µL of reaction buffer, followed by the addition of 20 µL of TDP1 enzyme solution prepared in dilution buffer (200 nM TDP1, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM DTT, pH 7.5), yielding a final reaction volume of 60 µL. Fluorescence was monitored on a multimode microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm, with readings taken at 1 min intervals over 30 kinetic cycles. ATA (100 nM) served as a positive control. For data analysis, the fluorescence intensity at each time point was normalized to the initial reading, and the rate of fluorescence increase was determined by linear regression. The percent inhibition was calculated by defining 100% inhibition as the slope obtained from substrate-only wells (without TDP1) and 0% inhibition as the slope from wells containing both substrate and TDP1 with an equivalent volume of DMSO vehicle in the absence of test compound. All enzymatic assays were independently repeated three times.

Experimental protocols › Computational chemistry

The geometric structure of the studied ligand was optimized using the HF/6-31G* level of theory with the SCF = tight, Pop = MK, iop(6/33 = 2), iop(6/42 = 6), and opt options in Gaussian 16.40 On the basis of the optimized geometry, the molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) mapped onto the van der Waals surface, frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO), the localized orbital locator (LOL), and the reduced density gradient (RDG) for non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis were calculated and visualized using Multiwfn 3.8 (ref 41 and 42) in conjunction with VMD 1.9.3.43

Experimental protocols › Visualization of the TDP1–AO65 binding mode and post-docking MM-GBSA rescoring

Based on the molecular docking results described in Section 2.7, the binding mode of AO65 within the TDP1 active site was analyzed and visualized. The 2D protein–ligand interaction diagram was generated using the Protein–Ligand Interaction Diagram tool in Schrödinger Maestro, and the 3D binding mode was further rendered using PyMOL software (Schrödinger, New York, NY, USA). For binding free energy estimation, the docked TDP1–AO65 complex was imported into Schrödinger Maestro, and post-docking MM-GBSA rescoring was performed using the Prime MM-GBSA module after local minimization of the complex. The calculated binding free energy and individual energy components were extracted from the MMGBSA ΔGbind terms.

Results and discussion › Scaffold analysis of the compound dataset

This study compiled a curated dataset of 2040 small-molecule TDP1 inhibitors with activity annotations for scaffold analysis and predictive model construction, including 857 active and 1183 inactive compounds. Two-dimensional plots with molecular weight (MW) as the abscissa and logarithm of octanol–water partition coefficient (log P) as the ordinate were used to characterize the dataset scope (Fig. 2A), revealing a molecular weight range of 72.11–927.02 Da and a log P range of −9.51–10.74. To systematically evaluate the structural diversity of the dataset, Bemis-Murcko scaffold analysis was performed, identifying 1311 unique core frameworks, which indicates a high degree of structural heterogeneity among the compounds. Fig. 2B (violin plot) shows that the number of compounds per scaffold has a mean of 1.50, a minimum of 1.00, and a maximum of 78.00. Corresponding density plot (Fig. 2C) exhibits a distinct peak (mode) at 1.50. This distribution indicates that most scaffolds are unique (i.e., associated with only one compound), reflecting the structural preference and enrichment characteristics of the studied system. Fig. 2D, the top 10 scaffolds ranked by abundance were selected for further analysis, with the distribution of active and inactive compounds visualized via a stacked bar chart. Most of these top 10 scaffolds are alicyclic, featuring cycloalkane backbones with diverse substitution patterns or fused ring systems. Notably, no single scaffold shows a significant predominance in active compounds; the majority of scaffolds have low proportions of active compounds, indicating that active compounds are scattered across multiple alicyclic scaffolds rather than concentrated in a single core framework.

Results and discussion › Construction and evaluation of TDP1 inhibitor prediction model

We established a total of 40 predictive models, comprising 30 ML models that combined six algorithms (LR, KNN, NB, RF, SVM, and XGBoost) with five molecular descriptors/fingerprints (RdkitDes, Morgan, MACCS, Atompairs, and PharmacoPFP), along with 10 DL models developed using advanced architectures (DNN, GCN, MPNN, GAT, Attentive FP, and FPGNN). All models underwent hyperparameter optimization (Table S1), with optimal parameters determined through validation set performance (Table S2). The optimized models were subsequently evaluated on the test set, with comprehensive performance metrics provided in Table S3. Key evaluation indices (AUC, F1, and BA) are visualized in Fig. 3A–J. The 40 models demonstrated performance ranges of 0.53–0.89 for AUC, 0.02–0.79 for F1, and 0.50–0.81 for BA. Notably, models employing PharmacoPFP fingerprints exhibited poor discrimination capacity, showing near-diagonal ROC curves with AUC scores of 0.53–0.59. To further investigate the reasons behind this, we performed Local Outlier Factor (LOF) detection on the dataset. We used Morgan, MACCS, Atompairs, RdkitDes, and PharmacoPFP to describe the molecular features, followed by dimensionality reduction using PCA. The outlier factors were then calculated, and points with an outlier factor greater than 5 were considered outliers. As seen from the results (Fig. 3K–P), among the five molecular descriptors or fingerprints, PharmacoPFP identified 145 outliers, indicating that PharmacoPFP fails to effectively capture the latent features of these compounds, leading to poor predictive performance of the TDP1 activity model built using PharmacoPFP. However, no significant outliers were detected using LOF analysis with Morgan, Atompairs, RdkitDes, and MACCS, suggesting that the structural features of these datasets are relatively distinct and there are no major outlier structures. These molecular descriptors are better at capturing the latent features of the datasets. Therefore, LOF analysis helps to explain the poor predictive performance of the PharmacoPFP model. Among these 40 predictive models, the SVM::RdkitDes model demonstrates the highest AUC value of 0.89, with F1 and BA values of 0.78 and 0.80, respectively, which also perform well compared to the other models. Based on the comprehensive analysis above, we select the SVM:: RdkitDes model as our predictive model. The Y-scrambling validation results provide critical evidence for evaluating the robustness of our SVM::RdkitDes model (Fig. 4A, B and Table S4). Notably, when implementing partial scrambling strategies, the model demonstrates expected sensitivity to label randomization (Fig. 4A). Training set scrambling (green markers) reduces performance to near-random levels (AUC-ROC: 0.45–0.57; F1 = 0–0.06; BA = 0.50–0.51), indicating that the model's predictive power strictly depends on valid chemical information, rather than data leakage or spurious correlations. The test set scrambling scenario (red markers) shows intermediate performance degradation (AUC-ROC: 0.41–0.59; F1: 0.38–0.50; BA: 0.44–0.55), suggesting that while the preserved training set structure retains partial generalization capability, the disrupted test labels effectively prevent proper prediction validation. Using 10 random seeds for the training-validation-testing splits, the unscrambled configuration (blue markers) achieves strong predictive performance, with evaluation metrics exhibiting excellent values. Specifically, the AUC-ROC ranges from 0.86 to 0.92, F1 scores range from 0.76 to 0.83, and BA ranges from 0.78 to 0.86 (Fig. 4B), confirming the model's inherent ability to capture meaningful structure-activity relationships. The stark contrast between the scrambled and unscrambled outcomes statistically validates the model's true predictive capability, rather than stochastic success.

Results and discussion › Feature importance analysis

To evaluate the interpretability and rationality of the knowledge learned by the models, the developed models were analyzed and interpreted using the SHAP method. Fig. 5A illustrates the mean absolute SHAP values (|SHAP|) of the top 20 critical molecular descriptors. This metric, calculated by averaging the absolute SHAP values of individual features across all samples, eliminates the cancellation effect between positive and negative contributions, thereby objectively reflecting the average influence of each feature across the entire dataset. Fig. 5B reveals the synergistic regulatory mechanisms of 208 RDKit descriptors on model outputs through a SHAP feature contribution heatmap. Features are sorted in descending order based on the absolute SHAP contribution, with positive and negative contributions represented by red/blue color scales to indicate their enhancing (activity promotion) or inhibitory (activity reduction) effects on predictions. Although the top 20 high-contribution features are prioritized, the “sum of 188 other features” region at the bottom demonstrates that the cumulative contribution of the remaining 188 minor features remains dominant. This indicates that model decision-making relies on both the directional regulation of high-impact features and the synergistic amplification of multidimensional weak features, aligning with the complex structure–activity relationship characteristics of TDP1 inhibitors. Fig. 5C visualizes the correlation between the numerical distributions of the top 20 key descriptors and their SHAP values using density scatter plots. Feature values are represented by a blue-to-red gradient, with high-value samples (red) densely distributed in the positive SHAP region and low-value samples (blue) concentrated in the negative SHAP region. This pattern suggests that increasing the values of these top 20 features systematically enhances the model's predicted probability of TDP1 inhibitory activity. The top 20 critical RDKit molecular descriptors identified in the predictive model can be systematically categorized into four classes of physicochemical properties and structural characteristics. The first class pertains to polar surface characteristics and electrotopological state distributions, including descriptors such as EState_VSA3/7 and VSA_EState2/4/6/7, which integrate polar surface area (VSA) and electrotopological state (E-State) parameters to characterize polar surface distribution and spatial heterogeneity in atomic electronic environments. These features are supported by visualizations of Gasteiger charge-derived atomic surface potential distributions and Crippen atomic contribution-derived atomic log P lipophilicity (Fig. 6A and B). Additionally, BCUT2D_CHGHI/MWHI/MWLOW quantifies topological diversity in molecular charge (CHGHI) and size (MWHI/MWLOW) based on molecular graph topology. The second class focuses on charge polarization and hydrophobic properties, where PEOE_VSA2/3/8 descriptors reflect polar surface area distributions derived from partial charge calculations, indicating significant charge polarization and regional charge separation. The prominence of SlogP_VSA8/10 (polar surface area proportion in high-log P regions) and MolLogP (molecular lipophilicity) further highlights the role of large hydrophobic regions and strong lipophilicity. The third class involves hydrogen bond interactions and structural complexity, with contributions from NumHAcceptors (hydrogen bond acceptors) and fr_amide (amide group count) underscoring the critical role of hydrogen bonding in molecular recognition, while NumAliphaticHeterocycles (aliphatic heterocycle count) reflects structural complexity in molecular frameworks. The fourth class encompasses drug-likeness optimization metrics, where a high qed value (quantitative estimate of drug-likeness, 0.63 ± 0.12) indicates excellent drug-like properties, and the significance of FpDensityMorgan3 (structural density parameter based on Morgan fingerprints with radius 3) suggests compact 3D topological structures that mitigate ADME risks associated with excessive molecular weight or polar surface area. Collectively, the optimal molecules predicted by the model require a synergistic balance of controlled polarity/hydrophobicity, optimized charge distribution, and rational structural complexity, driven by multidimensional physicochemical coordination rather than reliance on a single dominant feature.

Results and discussion › Deployment of TDP1 inhibitor activity prediction model as web and desktop applications

To enhance the accessibility and practical utility of the developed prediction model, we deployed the SVM::RDKitDes model as both an interactive web application and a standalone desktop application. The web application is available at http://www.drugpred.top:5050/. The web interface, illustrated in Fig. 7A and B, is designed to provide a straightforward and user-friendly experience for predicting TDP1 inhibitor activity. The application supports two modes of molecular input. First, users can directly enter a SMILES string in the designated input field (indicated by arrow a in Fig. 7A). Notably, the system incorporates an automatic standardization step that converts any input SMILES into its canonical form with explicit non-polar hydrogens prior to prediction, thereby ensuring consistency regardless of the input format. Once a valid SMILES string is entered, clicking the “Apply” button renders the corresponding two-dimensional molecular structure in the adjacent panel for visual confirmation. Second, users may draw a molecular structure directly on the integrated sketcher (indicated by arrow b in Fig. 7A). After completing the drawing, clicking the “Get Molecular Structure” button automatically generates the corresponding SMILES representation. Upon providing a molecular input through either method, the user can initiate the prediction by clicking the “SUBMIT” button. The application then returns the prediction results generated by the SVM::RDKitDes model. As a demonstrative example, ATA was used as input (Fig. 7B). The output table displays two key columns: the “Predictive Activity” column, which indicates the predicted classification of the compound (e.g., “active”), and the “Probability” column, which provides the associated confidence score for the predicted class (e.g., 0.91 for ATA). These results enable researchers to rapidly assess the potential TDP1 inhibitory activity of candidate compounds and prioritize them for further experimental validation. In addition to the web application described above, a standalone desktop application was developed to enable offline batch screening without requiring internet access or programming expertise. The executable program is publicly available for download at https://github.com/zenghuang8006/TDP1-inhibitor-prediction. Upon launching the application, users can select an input CSV file via the “Browse” button (Fig. 7C), where the CSV file should contain the SMILES representations of the compounds to be predicted under a column named “SMILES”. By clicking the “Run Prediction” button (Fig. 7D), the program automatically performs preprocessing, descriptor calculation, and SVM-based classification. The output CSV file is generated in the same directory as the input file, appending two additional columns: “predict” (binary classification label) and “predict_proba” (predicted probability of TDP1 inhibitory activity), thereby facilitating large-scale virtual screening of candidate compounds in a local environment.

Results and discussion › Virtual screening and bioassays for the identification of new TDP1 inhibitors

To evaluate whether the established SVM::RDKitDesc predictive model could effectively identify novel TDP1 inhibitors, we designed an integrated virtual screening strategy that combines ML-based prediction with structure-based molecular docking (Fig. 8). A total of 201 231 compounds were subjected to the screening workflow. First, all compounds were evaluated using the SVM::RDKitDesc model, and those with a prediction score greater than 0.8 were retained, yielding 23 455 candidates. These molecules were subsequently filtered according to Lipinski's rule of five to assess drug-likeness, and compounds containing potentially reactive functional groups were excluded, resulting in a refined library of 16 071 molecules. Molecular docking was then performed using Schrödinger software through a three-tier hierarchical protocol employing HTVS, SP, and XP docking modes in succession, with the top 10% of molecules by docking score retained at each stage. This stepwise screening process ultimately yielded 16 candidate compounds. The structures of these 16 compounds (Fig. 9), along with their prediction scores and molecular docking results, are summarized in Table 1.

Results and discussion › Biological activity evaluation and computational analysis of the potential TDP1 inhibitor AO65

To further validate the TDP1 inhibitory activity of the 16 computationally identified compounds, we employed a fluorescence-based enzymatic assay in which a fluorescent DNA substrate was used to monitor TDP1 catalytic activity. All 16 compounds were initially screened at a concentration of 100 µM, with aurintricarboxylic acid serving as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 10A, the majority of the tested compounds failed to significantly suppress the time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity, with the exception of compound AO65, which markedly attenuated the fluorescence signal. The rates of fluorescence increase were determined by linear fitting of the kinetic curves, and the corresponding inhibition rates were calculated accordingly (Fig. 10B and C). Among all tested compounds, AO65 exhibited the most pronounced TDP1 inhibitory activity. To further determine the potency of AO65, we assessed its dose-dependent effect on TDP1 activity across a range of concentrations. As illustrated in Fig. 10D, increasing concentrations of AO65 progressively suppressed the rise in fluorescence intensity. Consistently, the slopes of the fluorescence kinetic curves decreased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 10E), confirming the inhibitory effect of AO65 on TDP1. A dose–response curve was constructed from the calculated inhibition rates at each concentration, yielding an IC50 value of 0.80 ± 0.02 µM (Fig. 10F). To gain molecular-level insight into the reactivity and electronic properties underlying this inhibitory activity, a series of quantum-chemical analyses were carried out. The ESP map of AO65 (Fig. 10G) reveals that the surface minimum is −144.1 kcal mol−1, located at the carbonyl oxygen of the glycine carboxylate moiety, where electron density is highly concentrated, representing the strongest nucleophilic site in the molecule. The surface maximum is −4.1 kcal mol−1, situated on a carbon atom of the furan ring, indicating the region of lowest electron density. Frontier molecular orbital analysis (Fig. 10H) shows that AO65 has a HOMO energy of −5.77 eV and a LUMO energy of 4.23 eV, corresponding to a frontier orbital energy gap (ΔE) of 10.00 eV. The LUMO is fully delocalized over the conjugated system of the furanochromone and bromophenyl rings, indicating that these regions serve as the primary electron-accepting sites. In contrast, the HOMO is mainly localized on the glycinyl-acetyl fragment, identifying it as the principal electron-donating site. The spatially differentiated frontier orbital distributions reveal distinct electronic regions within AO65 that may contribute to intermolecular recognition in the TDP1 binding pocket. The LOL analysis of AO65 (Fig. 10I) reveals high LOL values (>0.5) in covalent bond regions and at the lone-pair electrons of heteroatoms (O, N, and Br), indicating pronounced electron localization. In the π-bond regions of the furanochromone fused-ring system and the bromophenyl aromatic ring, the LOL values are comparatively low, reflecting favorable π-electron delocalization that facilitates intramolecular charge transport. The reduced density gradient (RDG) scatter plot from the non-covalent interaction (NCI) analysis (Fig. 10J) shows no deep-blue spikes in the region where sign(λ2)ρ < −0.03, indicating the absence of particularly strong intramolecular hydrogen bonds. Overall, the weak interactions within the molecule are dominated by moderate van der Waals forces and steric effects, consistent with the structural features of a fused aromatic scaffold bearing multiple heteroatom-containing substituents. Molecular docking results demonstrate that AO65 inserts into the HKN catalytic domain of TDP1, forming an extensive network of interactions with key residues (Fig. 10K and L). The carboxylate moiety, which was identified by the ESP analysis as the most electron-rich region, forms hydrogen bonds with Asn516 (2.2 Å) and Ser399 (2.0 Å); the side-chain carbonyl group establishes a hydrogen bond with His263 (2.1 Å); and the chromone carbonyl in the molecular backbone forms a hydrogen bond with Asn283 (2.2 Å). In addition, salt bridges are formed with Lys495, His493, and Lys265, and a water-mediated bridge is observed with Ser459. Post-docking MM-GBSA rescoring further yielded a favorable binding free energy of −24.73 kcal mol−1 for the TDP1–AO65 complex (Fig. 10M). Energy decomposition showed that coulombic, van der Waals, hydrogen-bonding, and lipophilic interactions contributed favorably to binding, although the polar solvation term opposed complex formation. Together, these polar and electrostatic contacts, along with the MM-GBSA results and favorable electronic properties revealed by quantum-chemical analyses, provide a structural and energetic basis for the inhibitory activity of AO65 against TDP1.

Conclusions

In this study, a comprehensive computational framework integrating ML, DL, and structure-based molecular docking approaches was developed and coupled with experimental validation to discover novel TDP1 inhibitors. A total of 40 binary classification models were constructed using ML and DL algorithms combined with various molecular fingerprint representations, and through systematic evaluation, the SVM::RDKitDes model was identified as the optimal predictor (AUC = 0.89, F1 = 0.78, BA = 0.80), with its robustness confirmed through Y-scrambling and randomized split analyses. SHAP-based interpretability analysis revealed 20 critical molecular descriptors governing model predictions, providing mechanistic insights into the structural determinants of TDP1 inhibitory activity. The model is available as a web app at http://drugpred.top:5050 and as standalone.exe desktop apps at https://github.com/zenghuang8006/TDP1-inhibitor-prediction. Applying this model to a virtual screening campaign of 201 231 compounds, followed by drug-likeness filtering and hierarchical molecular docking, 16 candidates were selected for biological evaluation, among which compound AO65 demonstrated potent TDP1 inhibitory activity (IC50 = 0.80 ± 0.02 µM). Quantum chemical calculations and molecular docking analyses further rationalized the potency of AO65 by elucidating its favorable electronic properties and extensive interactions with key residues within the HKN catalytic domain. These findings suggest that integrating ML-driven predictive modeling with structure-based computational strategies provides a useful framework for TDP1 inhibitor discovery, with AO65 emerging as a candidate compound for further biochemical and mechanistic investigation.
