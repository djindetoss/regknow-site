Abstract

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive breast cancer subtype with a poor prognosis. The absence of effective targeted therapies and endocrine treatment options leads to limited therapeutic options, which remains one of the major clinical challenges in TNBC management. Drug discovery is typically a lengthy and costly process that could be significantly improved through drug repurposing. However, the biological complexity and insufficient repurposing strategies hinder the reuse. This study aims to develop a deep learning-based framework to accelerate drug discovery for TNBC, identify novel therapeutic candidates, and uncover potential drug targets. We developed a deep neural network framework to predict the anticancer efficacy, toxicity profiles, and structural similarities of compounds. By applying this platform to screen over 6,000 compounds from the Drug Repurposing Hub, we identified promising candidates with potential therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles against TNBC. The top-predicted compounds were subsequently validated through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo functional assays. Furthermore, we employed transcriptomic sequencing and mass spectrometry-based proteomics to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-TNBC activity. We identified emodepside, a structurally unique molecule diverging from conventional anticancer agents that exhibited potent antitumor efficacy across multiple TNBC cell lines. Significantly, emodepside administration (5 mg/kg) inhibited tumor growth in xenograft models. Integrated multiomics analyses (RNA-seq/CETSA-MS) identified NAMPT as the primary target. This study demonstrates the viability of our deep learning models to discover structurally novel anticancer agents that are distinct from conventional drugs, thereby expanding the therapeutic arsenal for TNBC patients. Emodepside emerges as a promising TNBC therapeutic candidate, with a possible mechanism of promoting TNBC cell apoptosis via NAMPT inhibition.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for 15–20% of breast cancers, causing the highest recurrence and mortality rate among all subtypes. − The absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression renders TNBC unresponsive to both endocrine therapies and HER2-targeted agents, significantly limiting treatment options. , Currently, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment method for TNBC. Therefore, developing new therapeutic drugs to expand treatment options is an urgent unmet clinical need and a major research focus for TNBC. Despite continuous efforts in TNBC drug discovery, de novo drug development remains a high-cost, low-yield, and protracted process with a substantial failure rate. , Identifying new anticancer drugs and targets is becoming increasingly challenging. In recent years, drug repurposing has emerged as an important strategy in modern drug discovery. However, experimentally screening all potential drug candidates is impractical due to practical and economic constraints. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and the availability of large-scale omics data now enable the systematic and intelligent inference of novel drug-disease relationships, facilitating efficient drug development and rational repurposing. AI has been increasingly applied in drug discovery. By leveraging computational approaches, AI can explore vast chemical spaces beyond the reach of conventional experimental methods. While AI has been widely adopted in drug design, with several successful models developed for predicting compound properties, , these models alone have not yet revolutionized drug development paradigms. This study employed deep learning-based pipeline that transforms drug molecular structures into graph representations, which were subsequently processed using graph convolutional neural networks (GCNs) to generate molecular representations (Figure ). These representations enabled simultaneous prediction of three critical pharmacological: (1) anticancer activity, (2) toxicity profiles, and (3) structural similarity to existing anticancer drugs. The computational predictions were validated through experiments. Our computational pipeline nominated emodepsidea veterinary anthelmintic currently in phase II clinical trials for human applications , as a promising candidate. Notably, this compound has never been investigated in the field of oncology, representing a truly novel therapeutic opportunity for TNBC. Through in vitro and in vivo studies, we confirmed emodepside’s anti-TNBC efficacy and safety profile, while conducting preliminary mechanistic investigations. Our study demonstrates that this deep learning-based approach enhances the precision of anticancer drug candidate selection, reduces development costs, and exhibits significant clinical potential. This study adheres to the TITAN Guidelines 2025 for transparent declaration and ethical use of AI in research. Study design and model architecture.

Materials and Methods › Data Acquisition

Efficacy Data Set: The efficacy data set was constructed by collecting IC50 values of over 500 compounds for MDA-MB-231 cells from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. Here, IC50 refers to the drug concentration required to kill 50% of tumor cells after 72 h of compound treatment. Toxicity Data Set: The ClinTox data set was used as the training set for the toxicity model, containing safety data for 1478 drugs with two labels: (1) presence/absence of toxicity and (2) FDA approval status. Similarity Data Set: We collected a set of 531 FDA-approved anticancer drugs to train the compound structural similarity model. Prediction Set: We utilized over 6,000 compounds from the Drug Repurposing Hub as our prediction set. This data set integrates multiple authoritative databases and pharmaceutical catalogs from more than 50 chemical suppliers, encompassing thousands of chemically diverse compounds. The collection spans the full spectrum of drug development stages, including: (1) clinically approved medications, (2) investigational agents currently undergoing clinical trials, and (3) preclinical compounds in early development phases. Canonical SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) strings for all compounds were generated and cross-verified across at least two of the following databases: PubChem, ChemSpider, ZINC15, ChEMBL, and Chemicalize. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tumor Hospital affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (No. SDTHEC2024003040).

Materials and Methods › Model Construction

The modeling architecture is based on the message passing neural network (MPNN) proposed by Gilmer et al., and employs a directed MPNN (D-MPNN) architecture. − The implementation is available at the official Chemprop repository (https://github.com/chemprop/chemprop). In contrast to MPNN architectures, this D-MPNN implementation performs edge-centric (bond-based) message passing instead of node-centric (atom-based) propagation, more accurately reflecting chemical interaction patterns. This design avoids unnecessary cycles and reduces interferences caused by noise in graph representations. This study adopted an end-to-end training paradigm, where the framework compares input molecular structures with predicted outputs, computes loss functions based on their differences, and computes gradients via the backpropagation algorithm while recording loss function values for each iteration. The model performance was optimized through three techniques: (1) feature enhancement using RDKit 2D molecular descriptors to improve compound characterization, (2) Bayesian hyperparameter optimization for precise model tuning, and (3) an ensemble approach employing cluster technology to create 20 structurally identical models with different random initializations, with final predictions determined by averaging the outputs. We employed RMSE and R 2 as the primary evaluation metrics to assess model performance and compared our model with conventional machine learning models, including random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and XGBoost. Ablation experiments were performed to evaluate the contribution of each key component in the D-MPNN architecture. This optimized modeling framework was subsequently applied to generate efficacy predictions for the prediction set. We applied the same modeling framework to develop the toxicity prediction model using the toxicity data set. The architecture maintained the same technical approaches including RDKit-based feature enhancement and ensemble clustering, with the following neural network specifications: 6 hidden layers (each containing 2200 neurons), 3 feed-forward layers, and a 0.35 dropout rate for regularization. ROC-AUC was adopted as the evaluation metric to assess model performance. This optimized model was subsequently employed to conduct systematic toxicity predictions for all compounds in the prediction set. The structural similarity between compounds in the prediction set and reference molecules from the similarity data set was quantitatively analyzed using the Tanimoto coefficient. This coefficient was computed from Morgan fingerprints (radius = 22,048 bits) generated with RDKit, and is defined as the ratio of shared chemical substructures between two molecules. In this study, compounds with a Tanimoto coefficient > 0.4 were considered structurally analogous to existing drugs.

Materials and Methods › Screening Candidate Anti-TNBC Drugs

The canonical SMILES of all compounds in the prediction set were input into the three trained models, with compounds first ranked by efficacy model-predicted IC50 values, then filtered by toxicity predictions and Tanimoto coefficient (<0.4) to select primary candidates, followed by final selection based on mechanism of action, innovativeness, and accessibility to identify anti-TNBC drug candidates.

Materials and Methods › Drug Preparation

Emodepside, oritavancin, bivalirudin, AP1903, and cyclophosphamide were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA). All compounds were reconstituted in purified water or DMSO according to the manufacturer’s instructions and aliquoted as 100 mM stock solutions stored at −80 °C. The compounds were diluted to their final concentrations using culture medium, with all experiments maintaining a DMSO concentration ≤ 0.1%.

Materials and Methods › Cell Culture

MDA-MB-231 cells were maintained in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, while 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Both cell lines (purchased from Procell) were used within 10 passages after thawing to ensure experimental consistency.

Materials and Methods › CCK8 Assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates at 5000 cells/well (100 μL/well). After 24 h incubation, complete medium containing drugs at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, and 500 μM was added (n = 3 replicates per concentration). Following 12/24/36/48 h treatments, media were replaced with 100 μL of fresh complete medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After 2 h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm (OD) was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as [(As – Ab)/(Ac – Ab)] × 100% (where Ab = blank, Ac = control, As = sample). Growth curves were plotted, and IC50 values were determined for each time point.

Materials and Methods › Clonogenic Assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3,000 cells/well (2.5 mL/well) and 4T1 cells at 1,000 cells/well (2.5 mL/well). After 24 h incubation, complete medium containing various drug concentrations was added. The medium was replaced every 3 days until visible cell colonies formed. Cultures were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min), stained with 0.2% crystal violet (30 min), washed, air-dried, and imaged. Colony numbers were quantified using ImageJ (n = 3 replicates).

Materials and Methods › Wound Healing Assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (106 cells/well) with marker lines drawn on the bottom. After 24 h incubation (100% confluency), wounds were created using a 200 μL pipet tip perpendicular to the plate bottom along lines crossing the markers. Following PBS washes, 2 mL serum-free medium containing test drugs was added. Initial images (4× objective) were captured at marked positions. After 24 h, fresh serum-free medium was replaced and identical positions reimaged, with final imaging at 48 h (n = 3 replicates). Wound areas were quantified using ImageJ to calculate healing rates (%) and generate bar graphs.

Materials and Methods › Transwell Migration and Invasion Assays

For the migration assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/mL (2 mL/well) and treated with serum-free medium containing drugs for 24 h. Cells were then collected and resuspended at 5 × 105 cells/mL in serum-free medium supplemented with 5% BSA (final concentration 0.05–0.2%). 100 μL of cell suspension was added to the upper chamber of Transwell inserts, while 500 μL of complete medium was added to the lower chamber. After 15 h incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The membranes were gently washed twice with PBS, and nonmigrated cells on the upper surface were removed with cotton swabs before microscopic observation and imaging. For the invasion assay, Transwell inserts were first coated with 100 μL of 50× diluted Matrigel and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 2 h (all procedures were performed on ice with precooled pipet tips). The subsequent steps were identical to the migration assay except for an extended incubation period of 20 h. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Materials and Methods › Annexin V/PI Apoptosis Assay by Flow Cytometry

Cells were trypsinized and resuspended at 5 × 104 cells/mL, then seeded in 6-well plates (2 mL/well). After 24 h adherence (∼60% confluency), the medium was replaced with drug-containing complete medium (2 mL/well) following PBS washes (3×). Post 24 h treatment, the supernatant was collected into 15 mL tubes, and adherent cells were washed with ice-cold PBS (3×), trypsinized, and pooled with the supernatant. After centrifugation (1,000 rpm, 3 min), the pellet was resuspended in PBS (2 mL) and recentrifuged. Cells were then resuspended in 1× binding buffer (1 × 106 cells/mL), and 100 μL aliquots were stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI (15 min, RT, dark). Finally, 400 μL 1× buffer was added, the suspension was filtered through a 300-mesh sieve, and analyzed within 1 h by flow cytometry (n = 3). Data were processed using FlowJo V10 software.

Materials and Methods › Subcutaneous Xenograft Tumor Experiment

This work has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments). Six-week-old female nude mice were stratified by body weight and randomly divided into four groups using a random number table (n = 6 per group): solvent control, cyclophosphamide, low-dose emodepside, and high-dose emodepside. One day before tumor cell injection, 4T1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were refreshed with complete medium. On inoculation day, cells were trypsinized, washed with PBS, centrifuged (1,000 rpm, 3 min), resuspended in 2 mL ice-cold PBS, and counted to prepare a 1 × 107 cells/mL suspension (kept on ice and injected within 1 h). After shaving and disinfecting (75% alcohol), mice were anesthetized with 0.3% sodium pentobarbital (0.1–0.2 mL/10 g) and inoculated subcutaneously in the axilla (100 μL cell suspension/injection) using aseptic technique. Postinjection, mice were monitored for recovery, daily health status, and tumor formation. Upon detection of palpable nodules (∼100 mm3) at inoculation sites, treatment groups received oral administration of either solvent or varying emodepside concentrations, while the cyclophosphamide group was administered 100 mg/kg cyclophosphamide intraperitoneally. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days using a digital caliper by an investigator blinded to group allocation, with tumor volume calculated as V = (longest diameter × shortest diameter2)/2, and body weight and behavior were monitored concurrently. After 15 days, mice were sacrificed for tumor excision and analysis.

Materials and Methods › Transcriptome Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from ∼90% confluent MDA-MB-231 cells after PBS washing and trypsinization. Cell pellets were lysed in 1 mL TRIzol, homogenized, and stored at −80 °C. For sequencing, triplicate RNA samples per group were processed into libraries via strand-specific cDNA synthesis and PCR amplification. Libraries were sequenced (Illumina platform), with quality control performed using FastQC and data filtering via fastp. Bioinformatics analysis included transcript alignment (TopHat), circRNA identification (find_circ/CIRC explorer2), differential expression (limma/edgeR), pathway enrichment (KOBAS), and miRNA target prediction (miRanda). Differentially expressed genes were defined as those with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p-value ≤ 0.05. In addition, we required that at least 2/3 of the samples in each group showed detectable expression of the gene, with a mean expression value ≥ 1, to ensure the robustness of the results. An FDR (false discovery rate) ≤ 0.05 was also applied as a multiple-testing correction to control the false-positive rate.

Materials and Methods › Cellular Thermal Shift Assay-Mass Spectrometry (CETSA-MS)

CETSA-MS was performed to identify potential drug-target interactions by analyzing thermally stabilized proteins. Triplicate experimental groups were established for both emodepside treatment and control conditions. Drug-treated cells were scraped, heated (48–66 °C, 3 °C increments, 5 min), and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. After centrifugation to remove denatured aggregates, soluble proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and differential bands were excised for MS. Excised gel bands were destained (50% acetonitrile/50 mM NH4HCO3), reduced (10 mM TCEP, 37 °C), alkylated (25 mM iodoacetamide, dark), and digested (2 μg trypsin in 50 mM NH4HCO3, 37 °C, overnight). Peptides were extracted (50% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid), dried, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS (EASY nLC-1200; 400 nL/min, 2–100% B over 60 min) using a Q Exactive HF-X (2.0 kV, 60k MS1/30k MS2 resolution, top20 DDA, 27% NCE, 20 s exclusion). Data were processed in Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (SwissProt 2022_01, trypsin/P, 2 missed cleavages, 10 ppm/0.01 Da tolerances, fixed carbamidomethylation, variable oxidation/acetylation, high-confidence peptides). Differential proteins were identified using the following criteria: FDR (q-value) ≤ 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1, and at least 2 unique peptides. Under these criteria, 23 proteins exhibiting significant thermal stability shifts upon emodepside treatment were identified.

Materials and Methods › Western Blotting and RT-qPCR

MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with emodepside at a concentration of 5 μM for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NAMPT, PARP, SIRT1, ALDH1, CD133, OCT4, SOX2, and GAPDH (loading control). The membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. For RT-qPCR, total RNA was extracted using a commercial RNA extraction kit. cDNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (which includes a genomic DNA removal step), and quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal control. NAD+ content was measured using a commercial NAD+/NADH assay kit. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Materials and Methods › Construction of NAMPT-Overexpressing MDA-MB-231 Cells via Lentiviral Transduction

To further validate whether emodepside exerts its antitumor effects through NAMPT, NAMPT-overexpressing MDA-MB-231 cells were established via lentiviral transduction. The NAMPT-overexpressing lentivirus and the corresponding empty vector control were purchased from GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were transduced with the lentivirus in the presence of HitransG (a proprietary infection enhancer provided by the manufacturer) and selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) to obtain stable clones. NAMPT overexpression was confirmed by Western blotting and RT-qPCR. The established cells were then used in colony formation assays to evaluate their resistance to emodepside-induced growth inhibition.

Materials and Methods › Statistical Analysis

Intergroup differences were analyzed using ANOVA or Student’s t test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (version 16.0) or GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1), and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results › Model Training and Identification of Candidate Anti-TNBC Drugs

From the GDSC database, 414 compounds with 72 h IC50 values for the TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 were initially identified. After excluding duplicates and biologics lacking canonical SMILES representations, 363 compounds with available data were included in the efficacy data set. These comprised clinically approved anticancer drugs (chemotherapeutics, targeted therapies), preclinical compounds, and plant-derived natural products. Notable IC50 values included cyclophosphamide (195.56 μM), paclitaxel (0.09 μM), docetaxel (0.01 μM), doxorubicin (0.44 μM), and epirubicin (0.15 μM). Cyclophosphamide was selected as the positive control for subsequent validation experiments based on its representative anti-TNBC efficacy. The data set was randomly split into training and validation sets at a 9:1 ratio. Combined with ensemble learning (20 independently trained models), we trained a deep learning model for predicting compound efficacy. The model architecture consisted of 5 hidden layers (with 1400 neurons each), 2 feed-forward layers, and a dropout rate of 0.25. The efficacy prediction model achieved an RMSE of 50.36 μM and an R 2 of 0.814 on the validation set, both outperforming the conventional ML models (Supplementary Table S1). We performed ablation studies by progressively removing or modifying the following components: RDKit 2D molecular descriptors, message-passing depth (reduced from 5 to 2), dropout rate (set to 0), and the number of FFN layers (reduced from 2 to 1). The results are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Removal of the RDKit 2D features caused the most pronounced performance degradation (RMSE increased from 50.35 to 128.92 μM, ∼2.56-fold), followed by the removal of dropout (RMSE increased to 117.34 μM, ∼2.33-fold). The toxicity prediction model attained a ROC-AUC of 0.836 on the validation set. Integrated analysis using efficacy, toxicity, and similarity prediction models screened 6,797 compounds from the Drug Repurposing Hub, revealing: 443 compounds with 72 h IC50 < 0.01 μM; 1,804 with low FDA approval likelihood versus 4,993 with high likelihood; 4,902 low-toxicity versus 1,895 high-toxicity compounds; and 514 structurally similar versus 6,284 dissimilar to known anticancer drugs (Table ). Applying selection criteria (IC50 < 0.01 μM, FDA-approval likely, low toxicity, structural novelty) yielded 307 candidates categorized as 1) repurposed clinical drugs (antibiotics, muscle relaxants, thrombin inhibitors), 2) nonclinical chemicals, 3) investigational trial drugs, 4) preclinical compounds, and 5) bioextracts. The 307 candidate compounds were ranked by their predicted IC50 values. The top 60 compounds were individually searched in PubMed using the search terms “[compound name]” AND (“cancer” OR “malignancy” OR “carcinoma” OR “neoplasm” OR “tumor”). Compounds with prior reports in oncology were excluded, along with those lacking commercial availability; among compounds sharing the same mechanism of action, only one was retained. The prediction results for the top 60 compounds have been provided in Supplementary Table S3. PubMed screening was conducted for each compound to exclude those with existing cancer research, while prioritizing clinically approved drugs or preclinical candidates. Four final candidates were selected for experimental validation based on predictive scores, mechanisms, innovation, and accessibility: the antibiotic oritavancin, the thrombin inhibitor bivalirudin, the investigational drug AP1903, and the anthelmintic emodepside (Table ).

Results › Model-Screened Candidate Drugs Exhibit TNBC Cell Growth Inhibition

CCK-8 assays demonstrated that oritavancin, bivalirudin, and emodepside significantly inhibited the proliferation of both MDA-MB-231 (24 h IC50 values were 34.22 ± 0.78, 13.36 ± 0.96, and 6.79 ± 0.17 μM, respectively) and 4T1 cells (24 h IC50 values were 84.11 ± 6.08, 11.53 ± 1.21, and 15.43 ± 0.93 μM, respectively) in a time-dependent manner, with markedly reduced cell viability that progressively intensified with prolonged drug exposure (Figure ). Notably, all three candidates exhibited superior growth-suppressive effects compared to cyclophosphamide, the conventional anti-TNBC reference drug. AP1903 exhibited no cytotoxic effects at concentrations up to 50 μM, while higher doses (100–500 μM) caused insoluble precipitates that interfered with OD measurements, leading to its exclusion. Clonogenic assays at 24 h (IC50-optimized concentrations) revealed marked suppression of colony formation: in MDA-MB-231 cells, emodepside showed superior inhibition versus cyclophosphamide, followed by bivalirudin and oritavancin compared to controls. Parallel results in 4T1 cells confirmed potent efficacyemodepside > oritavancin > bivalirudin > cyclophosphamide versus controls (Figure ). Collectively, emodepside, oritavancin, and bivalirudin demonstrated significant anti-TNBC activity, with emodepside emerging as the most potent agent independent of DMSO cytotoxicity. Discussion

TNBC, with its distinct molecular profile, is the most challenging breast cancer subtype to treat and remains primarily dependent on standard cytotoxic chemotherapy. However, the limited selection of chemotherapeutic agents, their severe adverse effects, and the lack of alternative options upon drug resistance leave TNBC patients with few therapeutic alternatives. Recent studies have explored immunotherapy and targeted therapy approaches for TNBC, but these have demonstrated limited efficacy and are only beneficial in a small subset of patients. , Therefore, novel drug discovery approaches are urgently needed to explore innovative therapeutics and identify new targets for TNBC treatment. The development of novel drugs is often protracted and costly, making drug repurposing an attractive strategy to enhance R&D efficiency. However, the key challenges in drug repurposing lie in identifying suitable candidates from vast drug libraries and determining their appropriate indications. Recent advances in artificial intelligence and the availability of large-scale omics data have established a research foundation for systematically and intelligently analyzing potential drug-disease relationships, significantly improving the feasibility of drug repurposing. , This study presents an efficient and rapid drug discovery system for identifying novel anti-TNBC therapeutics by integrating multiple critical dimensions: drug efficacy, safety profiles, and structural similarity of compounds. The system demonstrates high predictive performance, significantly outperforming conventional machine learning models. We attribute this performance improvement to two main factors. First, the IC50 values in our data set span 5 orders of magnitude (0.002 to 1460 μM), which poses a significant challenge for conventional ML methods relying on fixed fingerprint representations. Second, the molecular graph-based architecture of D-MPNN enables effective learning of complex structure–activity relationships through iterative message passing, capturing both local chemical environments and global molecular features across the molecular graph. The ablation study substantiated that each evaluated componentnamely, the RDKit 2D molecular descriptors, message-passing depth, dropout rate, and FFN layersmakes a meaningful contribution to the overall predictive performance, with the RDKit 2D descriptors and dropout regularization proving to be particularly impactful. The D-MPNN model employed in this study demonstrated satisfactory predictive stability and generalization capability under limited data conditions (363 compounds). To address the challenge of a relatively small data set, we adopted a random split (90% training, 10% validation) combined with ensemble learning (20 independently trained models), which maximized the use of training data while effectively reducing prediction variance through model averaging. During training, the RMSE trends on the training and validation sets remained consistent, with no obvious overfitting observed. Furthermore, the prediction standard deviation across the 20 models on the validation set was relatively small, indicating that the ensemble model’s prediction variability was within an acceptable range and that the predictions were reasonably stable. Furthermore, cellular experiments validated the effectiveness of the identified drug candidates. Notably, emodepside exhibits particularly promising therapeutic potential against TNBC. Emodepside is a novel derivative of N-methylated cyclooctadepsipeptide that was first isolated in 1992 from fungal secretory metabolites. Currently approved as a veterinary anthelmintic, emodepside has successfully completed Phase II clinical trials in humans, demonstrating an acceptable safety profile supported by clinical evidence. ,, This study pioneers the investigation of emodepside in oncology, revealing its significant anti-TNBC effects through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies. In vitro experiments demonstrated emodepside’s superior efficacy over conventional cyclophosphamide, markedly inhibiting TNBC cell migration and invasion while promoting apoptosis. In murine xenograft models, a single 5 mg/kg dose of emodepside produced durable tumor suppression, with significantly reduced tumor growth rates observed even 27 days post-treatment compared to controls. Treatment-related adverse effects were limited to transient weight loss that resolved within 7 days, with no observed neurotoxicity or other systemic toxicities, further corroborating its favorable safety profile. These findings position emodepside as a promising repurposed therapeutic candidate for TNBC, with advantages including established drug accessibility, proven safety, and a novel structure distinct from current therapies. Emodepside exerts its antiparasitic effects through three known targets: the Slo-1 pathway (a calcium-dependent voltage-gated potassium channel), the latrophilin-like receptor (synaptic G protein-coupled receptors in neurons), and GABA receptors. − The primary anthelmintic mechanism involves activating and opening the Slo-1 channel in filarial worms, altering the cell membrane potential and causing electrolyte imbalance. The mammalian homologue of Slo-1 is the calcium-activated potassium channel subunit KCNMA1. This study found no difference in KCNMA1 expression in TNBC cells before and after emodepside treatment. The latrophilin-like receptor is not the primary site of action. While deletion of the GABA receptor gene UNC-49 can moderately affect efficacy, this impact is even less significant than that mediated by latrophilin-like receptors. Since latrophilin is located at neural synapses and UNC-49 belongs to the GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) receptor family, existing research provides insufficient evidence to explain emodepside’s potential anticancer mechanism. This study conducted transcriptome sequencing and CETSA-MS analysis of MDA-MB-231 cells before and after emodepside treatment, identifying NAMPT as a potential target. NAMPT encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD+ salvage synthesis pathway. Initially isolated in 1994 as PBEF from human peripheral blood lymphocyte cDNA libraries, it was later found to possess nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase activity. NAMPT catalyzes the conversion of nicotinamide (NAM) and 5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate (PRPP) to nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), playing crucial roles in metabolism and other biological processes. Intracellularly, NAMPT localizes to the cytoplasm, nucleus, and mitochondria, while extracellularly it functions as a growth factor promoting B-cell maturation. NAMPT drives oncogenesis via NAD+ metabolism in multiple cancers: In colorectal cancer, it activates PARP/SIRT1 to promote stemness and chemoresistance; in ovarian cancer, its inhibition sensitizes tumors to cisplatin; in TNBC, its overexpression correlates with poor prognosis and its suppression inhibits tumor growth. , These findings support NAMPT as a promising therapeutic target for TNBC treatment. Preliminary mechanistic studies showed that emodepside reduced NAMPT expression and NAD+ content in MDA-MB-231 cells. Given that NAMPT is the rate-limiting enzyme in the NAD+ salvage pathway, this reduction likely impairs NAD+ -consuming enzymes PARP and SIRT1, both of which were downregulated by emodepside. Concurrent downregulation of stemness markers (ALDH1, CD133, OCT4, SOX2) suggests attenuated stemness, consistent with previous reports linking NAMPT activation to stemness and chemoresistance via PARP/SIRT1-mediated pathways in colorectal cancer. NAMPT overexpression conferred significant resistance to emodepside-induced growth inhibition, confirming that NAMPT functionally mediates emodepside’s anti-TNBC activity. Additionally, CETSA-MS identified NAMPT with significant thermal stability shifts upon emodepside treatment, suggesting potential direct interaction. Collectively, these results indicate that emodepside exerts its anti-TNBC activity through the NAMPT-PARP/SIRT1 axis by suppressing TNBC cell stemness. The study has certain limitations. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our deep learning models lack interpretability regarding the structural determinants of compound activity. Second, the assessment of model generalizability in the present study was mainly based on internal validation. Owing to the limited data set size, external validation using an independent data set has not yet been performed. In subsequent studies, we will further evaluate the generalization capability of the model through cross-cell-line validation. Finally, although NAMPT was identified as a functional mediator with preliminary mechanistic insights, the deeper regulatory mechanismsparticularly how emodepside downregulates NAMPT expressionremain to be further elucidated.

Conclusion

This study applied deep learning to construct efficacy prediction, toxicity prediction, and Tanimoto similarity models, providing an advanced AI-driven approach for TNBC drug discovery and repurposing. Emodepside, an anthelmintic drug in clinical trials, was identified through this model as having potent TNBC cell-killing activity with low toxicity. It may induce TNBC cell apoptosis by inhibiting NAMPT expression and function, representing a novel potential therapeutic strategy for TNBC.
