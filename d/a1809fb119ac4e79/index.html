Introduction

Pandemics caused by highly infectious viruses, such as human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis C virus, influenza virus, and Ebola virus, have posed persistent and severe threats to global public health, resulting in substantial mortality and economic losses1,2. Therefore, developing effective antiviral therapies is an urgent priority to address pandemics and save people’s lives. Currently, antiviral strategies can be broadly categorized into small-molecule drugs and vaccines3,4. While vaccines are effective in eliciting immune responses and preventing infection, oral small-molecule antivirals offer advantages in flexibility, rapid deployment, and lower production costs, making them indispensable, especially in the early stages of outbreaks5,6. However, conventional antiviral drug discovery remains slow, costly, and labor-intensive, often requiring over a decade and more than $2 billion to bring a new drug to market7. Computer-aided drug design (CADD) methods, such as molecular docking and quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, have been employed to partially accelerate this process8,9. However, these methods heavily rely on handcrafted features and human-derived prior knowledge10, and are limited in their scalability across the vast chemical space of drug-like compounds, which is estimated to range from 10²³ to 1060 molecules11. Recent advances in machine learning and deep learning have revolutionized nearly all stages of drug discovery and development12, including novel targets identification13, molecular design and optimization14, disease mechanism interpretation15,16, and biomarker discovery17,18. Remarkably, large language models (LLMs) designed for the biological domain have achieved state-of-the-art performance across multiple downstream tasks, including protein structure prediction19, molecular property prediction20, and drug response prediction21. Building on these advances, numerous ML/AI approaches have been proposed for biological activity prediction, including DeepCPI22, DeepDTA23, GraphDTA24, DeepConvDTI25, TransformerCPI26, MolTrans27, ColdstartCPI28, DTIAM29, and GraphBAN30. These models typically employ distinct neural network architectures to encode compound features and protein features separately, followed by feature fusion modules and downstream predictors (e.g., MLP) to estimate compound–protein binding affinity. Among these, several methods specifically designed using compound–antiviral target interaction datasets have been developed to predict antiviral activity against predefined targets, including MMFA-DTA31, MT-DTI32,33, GDL34, and COVIDVS35. Collectively, these approaches define biological activity primarily in terms of a compound’s ability to bind to a specific target protein and are therefore commonly referred to as drug–target interaction (DTI) prediction models. In the context of antiviral drug discovery, two principal strategies are widely employed. The first, known as phenotype-based drug discovery (PBDD), seeks to identify compounds with antiviral activity based on phenotypic outcomes observed in cell-based assays, such as inhibition of viral replication or virus-induced cytopathic effects, without requiring prior knowledge of molecular targets. The second, termed target-based drug discovery (TBDD), focuses on discovering compounds that exert antiviral effects through direct interaction with specific viral proteins, thus depending on detailed structural or functional information about the target. However, current computation methods designed for antiviral drug discovery suffer from several critical limitations: (1) Lack of phenotype-based models due to the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality antiviral phenotypic screening datasets; (2) Limited target-based antiviral models, stemming from the absence of well-curated datasets measuring compound activity against antiviral-relevant proteins; (3) Suboptimal feature representation for both compounds and targets, with the potential of LLMs in this domain still underexplored; (4) Poor integration between PBDD and TBDD strategies, where phenotype-based predictions often lack mechanistic interpretability, and target-based predictions suffer from high false-positive rates; (5) Lack of accessible, end-to-end web platforms dedicated to antiviral compound discovery, limiting broader adoption in the research community. To address these challenges and unify both PBDD and TBDD strategies, we propose DeepAVC—a large language model-powered deep learning framework for comprehensive antiviral drug discovery. DeepAVC integrates two complementary models: DeepPAVC, designed for PBDD, and DeepTAVC, tailored for TBDD. This dual-model architecture enables flexible application across diverse antiviral drug discovery scenarios, from phenotypic screening to target-specific compound identification guided by protein information. As a result, DeepPAVC shows superior performance over existing baseline models, with an AUROC improvement of 6.16%. DeepTAVC delivered competitive performance with state-of-the-art methods under the target warm-start setting, while showing a substantial AUROC gain under the more challenging target cold-start setting. To validate DeepAVC’s ability to identify novel antiviral agents, we applied DeepPAVC to perform phenotype-based virtual screening of the MedChemExpress (MCE) library. In vitro assays confirmed that 93.3% of the selected candidates (28 out of 30) showed antiviral activity against at least one virus. We further applied DeepTAVC to the most potent candidate, MNS, for target deconvolution, which suggested integrin beta-1 as a plausible host target. Subsequent mechanistic assays indicated that MNS does not measurably affect viral attachment, internalization, or entry, but instead inhibits viral replication at a post-entry stage and is accompanied by attenuation of virus-induced inflammatory responses. Together, these results support a host-directed mode of action that may contribute to the broad-spectrum antiviral phenotype of MNS. For ease of use, we also developed a user-friendly web platform for antiviral drug discovery (http://www.cuilab.cn/deepavc) and provided example tutorials to facilitate its practical application. Together, these results establish DeepAVC as a powerful and generalizable framework for accelerating the discovery of antiviral compounds.

Results › Overview of DeepAVC

Most pathogenic viruses affecting humans are RNA viruses, owing to their high mutation rates and rapid adaptability, which contribute to their frequent association with acute infections and epidemics. Among them, retroviruses represent a typical subclass, distinguished by their ability to reverse transcribe their RNA genome into DNA during replication. A typical retrovirus life cycle mainly consists of nine stages (Fig. 1a), each of which presents potential intervention points for therapeutic development. Targeting key proteins involved in these stages enables the development of drugs with distinct antiviral mechanisms, an approach referred to as TBDD. In contrast, when target information is unavailable, antiviral activity must be predicted solely from compound information, an approach referred to as PBDD. To support model development and evaluation, we first constructed benchmark datasets for the two scenarios. Specifically, for the PBDD scenario, we manually curated and filtered all cell-based antiviral activity data from the ChEMBL database36,37 to create the PAVC dataset (Supplementary Fig. 1), which serves as the training data for the DeepPAVC model. As for the TBDD scenario, we first integrated compound–protein binding affinity data from ChEMBL and BindingDB38 databases to build the CPI dataset (Supplementary Fig. 2), which was used for the initial stage of DeepTAVC training. We then refined this dataset by selecting antiviral-related interactions to create the TAVC dataset (Supplementary Fig. 3), which supported the second stage of training. The TAVC dataset comprises 234 known antiviral-related targets with distinct antiviral mechanisms, such as viral replication, viral entry, and host immune response (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 2). The DeepPAVC model takes the simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) representation of a compound as input, converts it into a 2D molecular graph, and extracts molecular features using KPGT20, a pretrained large language model trained on large-scale chemical structures. These features are then fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to output a predicted antiviral activity score (Fig. 1c). This architecture does not rely on redundant information, allowing the model to generalize to any valid compound structure. In contrast, the DeepTAVC model requires input from both compounds and proteins. The compound features are extracted using the same procedure as in DeepPAVC. For proteins, features are directly derived from the input amino acid sequences using the pretrained protein language model ESM-219. To capture both intra- and inter-modality interactions, we combine self-attention with cross-attention for feature fusion. The integrated compound–protein representations are then fed into an MLP to output a predicted compound–protein interaction score (Fig. 1d). The combined use of DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC enables a more comprehensive antiviral drug discovery strategy. In the PBDD scenario, for a given compound, DeepPAVC can first be used to predict its antiviral activity, followed by DeepTAVC to identify potential protein targets that may interact with the compound, thereby offering insights into its antiviral mechanism of action. In the TBDD scenario, for a given antiviral-related target, DeepTAVC is first used to prioritize candidate compounds with predicted binding potential, after which DeepPAVC is applied to assess the likelihood that these candidates also exhibit antiviral phenotypic activity. Results › Performance of DeepAVC on antiviral compound prediction

The performance of the DeepPAVC model is closely dependent on the quality of molecular representation. To this end, we evaluated a range of compound encoding strategies on the PAVC dataset to assess their effectiveness in antiviral compound prediction. These strategies included molecular fingerprints and descriptors generated by RDKit39, as well as several pretrained large language model encoders for 1D40–43, 2D20,44,45, and 3D46 molecular representations (Supplementary Note 1). Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Among all tested methods, the KPGT model, which encodes compounds based on their 2D molecular graph structures, achieved the highest performance, with an AUROC of 0.930 and an AUPRC of 0.926 (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary Data 3). The superior performance of KPGT is likely attributable to the inclusion of ChEMBL compounds in its pretraining corpus, enabling it to learn representations that better capture the chemical space covered by our PAVC dataset, which was also curated from ChEMBL. We identified COVIDVS35 as a relevant comparator because it is currently the only open-source model that predicts antiviral activity solely from compound information. However, COVIDVS was originally designed to identify compounds effective specifically against coronaviruses, and therefore lacks the capacity to generalize to broad-spectrum antiviral prediction. More broadly, existing baseline models are generally not designed to predict broad-spectrum antiviral activity using only compound features. In addition, we implemented two graph-based baseline models using graph convolutional networks (GCN) and graph attention networks (GAT), both of which rely on 2D molecular graph representations of compounds. To ensure a fair comparison, we retrained the baseline models on the PAVC dataset and evaluated all models on a unified test set. To further assess training strategies, we evaluated two approaches for utilizing the KPGT molecular encoder: (1) fine-tuning all trainable parameters, and (2) freezing the pretrained encoder and training only a classification head. Previous studies have shown that full fine-tuning generally leads to better performance than frozen encoders with added classifiers47,48, which we confirmed in our setting. The results showed that the fine-tuned DeepPAVC model achieved an AUROC of 0.931, outperforming its non-fine-tuned variant (AUROC = 0.907). Among all evaluated models, DeepPAVC was the only one to achieve an AUROC greater than 0.9, highlighting its superior ability to predict broad-spectrum antiviral activity from compound information alone. Compared to the best-performing baseline, DeepPAVC achieved a 6.16% increase in AUROC and a 6.26% improvement in AUPRC (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Based on these results, we selected the fine-tuned DeepPAVC model as the final version for all subsequent experiments and analyses. The tenfold cross-validation results of the DeepPAVC model across multiple evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary Data 4. For the DeepTAVC model, existing DTI prediction models are generally not designed for antiviral targets but instead focus on a broad spectrum of disease-related proteins. To establish a fair baseline for comparison, we first trained our model on a widely adopted external DTI benchmark dataset49,50. We refer to this general-purpose version as CADTI (cross-attention–based drug–target interaction model), which shares the same architecture as DeepTAVC but is trained on a general-purpose DTI dataset. To improve training efficiency, we froze the pretrained ESM-2 protein sequence encoder during CADTI training, while keeping all other modules trainable. In addition to CADTI, we selected nine widely used DTI prediction models as baselines: MolTrans, TransformerCPI, DeepDTA, DeepConvDTI, DeepCPI, GraphDTA, ColdstartCPI, DTIAM, and GraphBAN. All baseline models, as well as CADTI, were trained and evaluated on the same external dataset50 to ensure a consistent and fair comparison. We partitioned the validation data according to whether the compound and/or protein was observed in the training set, resulting in four evaluation settings: seen compound–seen protein (SS), unseen compound–seen protein (US), seen compound–unseen protein (SU), and unseen compound–unseen protein (UU). The results showed that when targets were seen during training (SS and US), overall performance was high (AUROC >0.9). CADTI achieved the best performance, with AUROCs of 0.967 (SS) and 0.965 (US), followed closely by GraphBAN with AUROCs of 0.961 (SS) and 0.955 (US). In contrast, when targets were unseen during training (SU and UU), all models exhibited a marked performance drop. Nevertheless, CADTI remained the top-performing method, achieving AUROCs of 0.798 (SU) and 0.836 (UU), whereas ColdstartCPI ranked second with AUROCs of 0.759 (SU) and 0.774 (UU). Collectively, these results indicate that CADTI performs competitively with state-of-the-art baselines under target warm-start settings, while providing a clear advantage under the more challenging target cold-start scenarios, highlighting its improved generalization to unseen targets (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 5). To further adapt the model to the antiviral domain, we first trained CADTI on our CPI dataset, which contains a broader range of compound–target affinity data. This pretraining stage enabled the model to capture general compound–protein interaction patterns. We then conducted second-stage fine-tuning using the TAVC dataset, which specifically focuses on antiviral-related targets. The resulting final model, DeepTAVC, showed improved performance compared to the CPI-trained CADTI, achieving an AUROC of 0.9154 versus 0.8962 (Supplementary Fig. 6). This improvement underscores the importance of domain-specific fine-tuning for enhancing model performance in antiviral compound discovery. In addition, we evaluated the predictive performance of DeepTAVC on each antiviral-related target in the TAVC dataset. For each target, we held out 10% of its compound–target pairs as a validation set and fine-tuned the pretrained CADTI model using the remaining 90% of samples; thus, each target was trained and evaluated independently. Because class balance varied substantially across targets, we excluded targets with extreme class imbalance to avoid unreliable performance estimates. For each retained target, we reported the AUPRC on the validation set together with the positive class ratio of that target-specific dataset (used as a baseline). Results › Virtual screening with DeepAVC identifies known and novel antiviral compounds

One of the primary applications of DeepAVC is the rapid virtual screening of antiviral compounds across vast chemical spaces. The DeepPAVC model, which requires only compound information as input, enables efficient high-throughput screening for potential antiviral compounds. Therefore, we selected eight representative compound libraries for virtual screening (Supplementary Data 7), covering a wide range of compound types and library sizes (104 to 108 compounds). For instance, the DrugBank51 database includes mainly FDA-approved drugs, while the MCE database contains commercial bioactive compounds. To visualize the screening results, we extracted molecular features from each library using DeepPAVC and projected them into two dimensions using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)52. Compounds were colored based on their predicted antiviral scores. Compounds with high and low DeepPAVC scores formed distinct clusters with sharp boundaries, indicating that DeepPAVC can effectively differentiate compounds with potential antiviral activity from inactive ones (Fig. 3a). Next, we applied a threshold of 0.9 to classify compounds with high predicted antiviral potential. Across the eight compound libraries, the average proportion of predicted potential antiviral compounds was ~11%, ranging from 2.3 to 26.1% (Fig. 3b). Results › Mutual complementarity of DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC enhances antiviral drug discovery

DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC are designed to capture different but complementary aspects of antiviral compound discovery, and their combined use helps overcome individual model limitations. On one hand, DeepPAVC can serve as an antiviral activity filter to both reduce false positives and recover false negatives from DeepTAVC predictions. This is crucial because not all compounds that bind to a protein target exhibit antiviral efficacy in cellular assays, and conversely, some potent antivirals may act through alternative mechanisms not captured by a specific target-based model54. By incorporating phenotypic-level predictions, DeepPAVC complements DeepTAVC in identifying truly effective compounds and improving the overall accuracy and robustness of virtual screening results. To demonstrate this, we selected influenza hemagglutinin (PDB ID: 8SD2) as a representative antiviral target. Hemagglutinin plays a critical role in the viral entry process of influenza viruses and is a well-established antiviral target. We extracted influenza-related compound bioactivity data from the ChEMBL database and computed both DeepPAVC-predicted antiviral activity scores and DeepTAVC-predicted binding scores to hemagglutinin. Two representative compounds illustrate the complementarity of the models. The compound CHEMBL7004 has a reported EC₅₀ of 47.86 μM against the influenza virus in cell-based assays55, which is generally considered weak or inactive. However, DeepTAVC predicted a high binding score (0.9941) and a favorable docking free energy (−7.7 kcal/mol), suggesting strong binding to hemagglutinin. This is a typical false positive case—good binding but poor antiviral efficacy. In contrast, DeepPAVC predicted a very low antiviral score (0.0002), correctly identifying the compound as inactive. Thus, DeepPAVC can assist in filtering out such false positives from DeepTAVC predictions. Conversely, the compound CHEMBL4103104 has a reported EC₅₀ of 8.32 nM, indicating strong antiviral potency56. Yet, DeepTAVC predicted a low binding score (0.0001) and weak docking affinity (−3.7 kcal/mol), possibly due to the compound acting through a different antiviral mechanism unrelated to hemagglutinin. This represents a false negative from the perspective of target-based screening. Notably, DeepPAVC predicted a high antiviral score (0.9999), correctly identifying it as a potent antiviral compound. Therefore, DeepPAVC can also compensate for DeepTAVC’s inability to capture off-target or alternative mechanisms of antiviral action, thereby reducing the false negative rate (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Together, these results demonstrate how DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC can be used synergistically to enhance the reliability of virtual screening outcomes—DeepPAVC not only eliminates false positives from DeepTAVC but also recovers missed hits, ensuring a more comprehensive and accurate identification of antiviral candidates. On the other hand, DeepTAVC can provide mechanistic interpretability for compounds identified by DeepPAVC by predicting their potential protein targets. To demonstrate this, we first performed virtual screening using DeepPAVC on the ChEMBL database to identify compounds with high predicted antiviral activity. From the top-scoring candidates, we randomly selected CHEMBL585351, which received a DeepPAVC score of 1.0, indicating strong predicted antiviral potential. We then applied DeepTAVC to predict the binding affinities of CHEMBL585351 to a panel of known antiviral targets. The model predicted the highest binding score (1.0) for HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (UniProt ID: Q72547), suggesting this protein as a likely functional target of the compound. We further performed molecular docking using AutoDock Vina to model the binding of CHEMBL585351 to HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (PDB ID: 2JLE) and to gain preliminary structural insights at the in silico level. Notably, the co-crystallized ligand in 2JLE was also assigned a DeepTAVC score of 1.0 and yielded a Vina-predicted binding energy of −9.6 kcal/mol under our docking settings. In comparison, CHEMBL585351 achieved a more favorable Vina score (−11.7 kcal/mol) under our docking settings, suggesting that it may adopt a compatible binding pose in this docking model. Inspection of the predicted poses indicated that CHEMBL585351 and the co-crystallized ligand adopt broadly similar binding modes within the NNRTI binding pocket of HIV-1 reverse transcriptase. Specifically, both compounds were predicted to engage in π–π stacking interactions with TYR188 and TRP-229, and to form hydrogen bond contacts with LYS101. In addition, CHEMBL585351 was predicted to form an additional hydrogen bond with ASP-186, which may contribute to the favorable docking result observed in this model. (Supplementary Fig. 10b). According to previous literature57, the co-crystallized ligand in 2JLE exhibits an IC₅₀ of 384 nM against HIV-1 reverse transcriptase and also received a DeepPAVC score of 1.0, indicating that it is both target-active and phenotypically antiviral. Given that CHEMBL585351 shares these predictive characteristics and shows a favorable docking profile under the applied computational settings, it represents a plausible candidate for further experimental evaluation. However, these in silico results do not permit inference of its actual IC50. These findings underscore the synergistic utility of DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC—not only enabling accurate compound prioritization but also providing mechanistic insights—thereby advancing a more interpretable, comprehensive, and effective framework for antiviral drug discovery.

Results › Drug repurposing via a similarity network constructed from DeepPAVC-derived molecular representations

Drug repurposing, also known as drug repositioning, refers to the strategy of identifying new therapeutic uses for existing or approved drugs. It has emerged as a cost-effective and time-efficient approach to drug discovery, particularly in rapidly evolving fields such as antiviral research. To enable antiviral drug repurposing, we constructed a drug similarity network based on molecular representations generated by the DeepPAVC model. Specifically, we used the DeepPAVC model to extract molecular embeddings for ~10,000 compounds from the DrugBank database. We then computed the pairwise cosine similarity between these embeddings. In the resulting similarity network, each compound is represented as a node, and edges are formed between nodes with cosine similarity values greater than 0.2. To maintain network sparsity and interpretability, each node was limited to a maximum of 20 nearest neighbors. To demonstrate the utility of this network, we selected Dolutegravir (DTG)—an antiretroviral drug approved for the treatment of HIV/AIDS—as a reference compound and extracted its local subnetwork (Supplementary Fig. 11). As expected, DTG was closely connected to several other known anti-HIV agents, including Rilpivirine, Delavirdine, Etravirine, Nevirapine, and Zidovudine, demonstrating that compounds with similar antiviral pharmacological profiles are naturally clustered in the DeepPAVC-derived embedding space. Interestingly, the subnetwork also included drugs originally indicated for non-viral diseases, such as the anticancer agents Trametinib and Pazopanib, which showed high similarity to DTG. Recent studies have reported that Trametinib exhibits antiviral activity against lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV)58, while Pazopanib was found to inhibit Human enterovirus 71 (EV-A71) replication in a dose-dependent manner59. These findings suggest that such drugs may have unrecognized antiviral potential and could serve as promising candidates for drug repurposing. Together, these results indicate that the DeepPAVC-based drug similarity network can effectively group compounds by their antiviral mechanisms and offers a practical tool for identifying antiviral agents from existing non-antiviral drugs.

Results › DeepTAVC reveals interpretability by identifying key functional groups and binding residues

The DeepTAVC model incorporates a cross-attention mechanism to capture cross-modal interactions between compounds and protein targets. This architecture allows us to extract attention scores that reflect the relative importance of specific compound atoms and protein residues during binding. To illustrate this capability, we selected the co-crystal structure of Doravirine, an orally bioavailable NNRTI, bound to HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (PDB ID: 4NCG60). Doravirine forms multiple stabilizing interactions within the target’s active pocket, contributing to its high binding affinity (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Using DeepTAVC, we extracted the attention map of compound–protein interactions and normalized the attention scores of doravirine atoms to a scale of 0–1. These scores revealed several highly attended atoms, including the third and fourth nitrogen (N) atoms, the 24th chlorine (Cl) atom, and the 25th carbon (C) atom (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Further inspection of the crystal structure showed that the third and fourth nitrogen atoms form hydrogen bonds with LYS-103 in HIV-1 reverse transcriptase. The 24th Cl atom and the 25th C atom are part of a trifluoromethyl group, which enhances Doravirine’s hydrophobicity, allowing better access to the hydrophobic binding pocket, and thus improving binding affinity. These findings suggest that DeepTAVC’s attention maps effectively highlight key functional groups and atoms involved in compound–protein interaction. In addition to attention map analysis, we performed an explicit perturbation masking analysis to identify functionally important chemical substructures. Specifically, we extracted the atom-level embeddings from KPGT for each input compound and iteratively masked each atom by replacing its embedding with an all-zero vector. The masked embeddings were then fed into DeepTAVC, and we quantified the change in predicted binding score relative to the unmasked input. Atoms whose masking caused larger decreases in the predicted score were considered more influential for the model’s prediction. Overall, the perturbation masking results were largely consistent with the attention map analysis. For example, both methods highlighted the N atom at position 3 in the representative compound as highly important. We also observed discrepancies between the two approaches. Attention maps tended to assign higher importance to certain substituents, such as the chloro group at position 24 and the trifluoromethyl group at position 25, whereas perturbation masking more strongly emphasized atoms within the aromatic core, such as the carbon at position 10 (Supplementary Fig. 12c). We attribute these differences to the inherent characteristics of the two interpretability methods. Perturbation masking directly alters the input representation and can therefore preferentially highlight scaffold atoms whose removal induces larger global changes in the model output. In contrast, attention maps reflect learned weighting patterns within the model’s attention mechanism without explicitly perturbing the molecular structure. Together, these analyses suggest that perturbation masking and attention-based attribution provide complementary, rather than redundant, interpretability signals. We note, however, that attention weights alone should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causal importance. We then assessed whether DeepTAVC could provide interpretability from the protein perspective. For this, we computed the relative activity change score for each residue in the HIV-1 reverse transcriptase sequence. This score reflects the impact of residue-level perturbations on compound–protein binding affinity, with higher absolute values indicating greater functional importance. The results revealed that residues within the RT_Rtv domain (residues 44–234), which is functionally essential for HIV-1 reverse transcriptase activity, exhibited markedly higher scores than other regions (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Moreover, residues K101, T106, V188, and M229, located in proximity to the Doravirine binding site, showed significant sensitivity to mutation, indicating their key roles in binding (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Notably, the phenyl ring of Doravirine engages in π–π stacking interactions with TYR188 and TRP-229, consistent with the mutation-sensitivity profile and further validating the interpretability of the model (Fig. 4g). Taken together, these results demonstrate that DeepTAVC enables atomic-level and residue-level interpretability of drug–target interactions, offering valuable mechanistic insights in antiviral drug discovery.

Discussion

Antiviral drug discovery continues to face substantial challenges, particularly in the context of rapidly evolving viral pathogens such as the influenza virus, HIV, and coronaviruses. Traditionally, antiviral drug discovery has followed two major paradigms: phenotype-based drug discovery (PBDD) and target-based drug discovery (TBDD), each with distinct advantages and inherent limitations. PBDD identifies compounds based on their ability to elicit antiviral phenotypes in cell-based systems without requiring prior knowledge of molecular targets, thereby offering high biological relevance. However, this strategy often lacks mechanistic interpretability. In contrast, TBDD focuses on identifying compounds that bind to predefined viral or host targets, providing mechanistic clarity but often suffering from high false-positive rates, as strong binding affinity does not necessarily translate into antiviral efficacy. Despite these limitations, most existing computational approaches for antiviral drug discovery predominantly adopt the TBDD paradigm and frame the problem as a drug–target interaction (DTI) prediction task, emphasizing physicochemical binding while often overlooking phenotypic relevance. To overcome these challenges, we developed DeepAVC, a novel deep learning framework that integrates both PBDD and TBDD strategies for antiviral drug discovery. By combining DeepPAVC for phenotype-based antiviral activity predictions and DeepTAVC for target-based interaction inference predictions, DeepAVC provides a comprehensive and complementary solution for antiviral compound discovery. The synergy between these two models is a central strength of the framework: DeepPAVC effectively filters out phenotypically inactive compounds, thereby reducing false positives arising from target-based screening, while DeepTAVC links active compounds to potential molecular targets and offers mechanistic insights. Through large-scale virtual screening and experimental validation, DeepAVC successfully identified both known and novel antiviral compounds. Notably, the compound MNS exhibited robust broad-spectrum antiviral activity and outperformed the clinically advanced antiviral agent Sisunatovir in multiple in vitro and in vivo assays, underscoring the practical utility of DeepAVC in discovering potent antiviral candidates while simultaneously enabling mechanistic interpretation. Despite these advantages, several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the performance of both DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC is inherently dependent on the quality and coverage of available training data. Publicly curated resources such as ChEMBL, while extensive, may introduce dataset-specific biases that could affect model generalizability. Second, to improve computational efficiency during model training, compounds with more than 90 heavy atoms were excluded from the training datasets. Although this decision substantially reduced training time, it may have led to the exclusion of certain clinically relevant antiviral agents, such as large protease inhibitors. Third, while we demonstrated the utility of DeepTAVC through a target-based virtual screening case study on HIV-1 reverse transcriptase, experimental validation of these candidates was not performed due to the stringent biosafety requirements associated with HIV research. Consequently, this part of the study remains limited to in silico analysis. In the future, DeepAVC can be improved by expanding training datasets to include more diverse antiviral phenotypic data and incorporating structural data to refine target-based predictions. Further exploration of the model’s ability to integrate other critical factors, such as compound dosage and treatment duration, will also enhance its predictive power. Despite these challenges, the DeepAVC framework represents a significant advance in antiviral drug discovery by providing a comprehensive platform that integrates PBDD and TBDD strategies.

Methods › Dataset construction › The PAVC dataset

In this study, we select the ChEMBL_34 database to construct the PAVC dataset for training the DeepPAVC model. The ChEMBL_34 database was released in March 2024 and contains 2,496,335 distinct compounds, 16,003 targets, 1644390 assays, 21,123,501 activities, and 92,121 source publications.

Methods › Data filtering

We first downloaded all assays available in ChEMBL and filtered out virus-related assays using three strategies: (1) Organism-based filtering: we selected assays with the organism term categorized as “virus”, resulting in 43,843 virus-related assays. Next, we combined the assay sets obtained from these three approaches by taking the union based on their unique assay IDs, resulting in 64,524 assays. After excluding assays unrelated to antiviral activity measurements, we finalized a dataset comprising 50,019 virus-related assays.

Methods › Training strategy › DeepPAVC

We split the PAVC dataset into training, validation, and test sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. The model parameters of the molecular encoder, KPGT, were set to be fully trainable. An MLP was used to output the probability that the input compound is antiviral, and the model was trained by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between the predicted probabilities and the true labels. The hyperparameters of the DeepPAVC were determined through hyperparameter optimization.

Methods › Training strategy › DeepTAVC

For the DeepTAVC model, we applied a two-stage training strategy. In the first stage, the model was trained on the CPI dataset to learn broader compound–protein interaction patterns. Subsequently, in the second stage, the model was fine-tuned using the TAVC dataset to achieve optimal performance in the antiviral context. During training, all parameters of the compound encoder (KPGT) were set to be trainable, while the parameters of the protein encoder (ESM-2) were frozen to ensure that the model could be trained within GPU memory constraints. The training objective of the DeepTAVC model was to minimize the cross-entropy loss between the predicted interaction probabilities and the true labels. The hyperparameters of the DeepTAVC were determined through hyperparameter optimization.

Methods › Training details

All models in this study were implemented in PyTorch66 version 1.10.0 with CUDA version 11.3 and Python 3.7. An Adam optimizer with a weight decay of 1e-6 and a learning rate of 3e-5 was used to optimize the model. The model was trained with a batch size of 32 for a total of ten epochs. Both DeepPAVC and DeepTAVC had around 100 million trainable parameters. Training was performed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

Methods › Model performance comparison

All the baseline models for DeepPAVC comparison were trained on the PAVC dataset, while the performance comparison between DeepTAVC and its baseline models was conducted on the external CPI dataset. The dataset was split into training and test sets at a ratio of 9:1, followed by tenfold cross-validation.

Methods › Molecular docking

In this study, the crystal structure of the HIV-1 reverse transcriptase was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank database (PDB ID: 2JLE) in PDB format, and prepared using AutoDockTools 1.5.6. The protein was preprocessed by removing crystallographic water molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, and performing residue optimization and patching as needed. The compounds used for docking were downloaded from the PubChem database67 in SDF format. The compounds were imported into AutoDockTools, and all rotatable bonds were kept flexible by default. The protein receptor and ligands were then converted to PDBQT format. The docking grid was centered at the centroid of the co-crystallized ligand in the 2JLE structure. The grid box size was set to 25 × 25 × 25 Å in the x, y, and z dimensions. Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2 with num_modes set to 50. The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed in PyMOL v3.1.

MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations were performed using the Uni-GBSA pipeline68 to further evaluate the interactions between HIV-1 reverse transcriptase and the screened candidates. The docked protein–ligand complexes obtained from AutoDock Vina were used as the input structures for MM/GBSA analysis. In brief, the protein–ligand complexes were first prepared by removing crystallographic water molecules and assigning appropriate protonation states. Protein parameters were assigned using the AMBER ff14SB force field, while ligand parameters were generated using the GAFF force field with AM1-BCC partial charges. Each complex was subjected to energy minimization to remove steric clashes prior to binding free energy calculation. Methods › Reagents and antibodies

A mouse antibody against GFP (catalog no. 66002-1-Ig), and a rabbit antibody against beta-actin (catalog no. 66009-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech. Methods › Cell culture and viral infection

RAW264.7 and HeLa cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). RAW264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; EallBio, 03.1002 C), whereas HeLa cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (EallBio, 03.4001 C). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin. After the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were infected with vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV; MOI = 0.1), herpes simplex virus type 1, F strain (HSV-1 F strain; MOI = 0.5), encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV; MOI = 0.1), mouse hepatitis virus, A59 strain (MHV-A59, MOI = 0.1), influenza A virus PR8 strain (IAV-PR8; MOI = 0.1), or VSV expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP; MOI = 0.1). After 1 h of infection, the inoculum was removed, and fresh medium was added for subsequent analysis.

Methods › RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and real-time quantitative PCR

RNA extraction from cells following various infections was performed using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN, A0123A01). Purified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript II RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R223-01). Target gene expression was quantified using SYBR Green qMix (Vazyme, Q311) in a quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) assay. The relative expression levels of target mRNAs were normalized to the housekeeping gene Actb. The viral primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Data 14.

Methods › Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Strong) (MedChemExpress, HY-K1001), which was supplemented with protease inhibitors, including an EDTA-Free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail I (100× in DMSO), and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail III (100× in DMSO). Clarified cell extracts (10 to 30 μg) were resolved on SDS-polyacrylamide gels and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Beyotime, FFN08). After blocking, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies. The bound secondary antibodies were detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) method (EallBio, 07.10009-50).

Methods › Flow cytometry analysis

After infection, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual virus, followed by trypsinization to generate a single-cell suspension. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1600 rpm for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and transferred to flow cytometry tubes for subsequent analysis. To evaluate infection efficiency, the percentage of GFP-positive cells was quantified using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Methods › Fluorescence assay

Cells were infected with VSV-GFP at an MOI of 0.1 for 12 h. After treatment with small molecules, cells were visualized under a Nikon fluorescence microscope equipped with a 488 nm excitation filter and a 510 nm emission filter. Images were captured at a magnification of 10×.

Methods › Cell cytotoxicity assay

Target cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL of complete medium. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of the compound (1, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, and 150 μM) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (YEASEN, 40203ES60) was added to each well, and the plate was gently shaken to mix. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Methods › Time-of-addition assay

To determine the stage of the viral life cycle affected by MNS, time-of-addition assays were performed in both RAW264.7 and HeLa cells using VSV or HSV-1. Cells were infected with the virus at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by extensive washing to remove unbound virions. MNS was then added at final concentrations of 10, 20, or 40 µM at 0, 12, or 24 h post-infection (hpi). At the indicated time points, total RNA was extracted and viral mRNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR. This assay was used to determine whether MNS exerts antiviral activity during viral entry or at post-entry stages of the viral replication cycle.

Methods › 4 °C attachment assay

To assess the effect of MNS on viral attachment, assays were performed in both RAW264.7 and HeLa cells. Cells were pre-chilled at 4 °C for 30 min and subsequently incubated with VSV or HSV-1 at 4 °C for 1 h to allow virus binding without internalization. Cells were then washed extensively with ice-cold PBS to remove unbound virions. Cell-associated viral genomes, representing surface-bound viruses, were quantified by RT-qPCR. This assay was performed to determine whether MNS affects viral attachment to the host-cell surface.

Methods › 37 °C internalization assay

To examine whether MNS interferes with viral internalization, synchronized internalization assays were conducted in both RAW264.7 and HeLa cells. Cells were first incubated with the virus at 4 °C for 1 h to synchronize viral binding. After removal of unbound virus by cold PBS washes, cells were shifted to 37 °C for 60 min to allow viral internalization. Subsequently, cells were briefly treated with trypsin to remove residual surface-bound virions, followed by additional PBS washes. Intracellular viral RNA was then extracted and quantified by RT-qPCR. This assay specifically evaluated the effect of MNS on post-attachment viral uptake.
