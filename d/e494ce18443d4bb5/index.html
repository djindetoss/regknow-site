Abstract

Research and development in drug discovery will need to find significant efficiency gains if the industry is to continue generating novel drugs. There is great expectation for machine learning (ML) to provide this boost in R&D productivity, but to harness the full potential of ML, the generation of new, high-quality datasets will be necessary. Here, the authors present a platform that combines high-throughput display and selection data generation with ML. More specifically, deep learning is used to inform the directed evolution of novel biotherapeutics using DNA library synthesis, ultra-high throughput selections, and next generation sequencing. By combining the learnings of multiple in silico models, their platform enables multi-parameter optimisation across multiple important protein characteristics. They also present a model for benchmarking these ML-driven drug discovery platforms according to the accuracy of their underlying in silico models, in conjunction with the throughput of their empirical experimentation.

Introduction

Our current model for drug discovery is inefficient. Despite our understanding of disease constantly improving, our efficiency in generating new therapies has continuously fallen over the past 60 years. Adjusting for inflation, in the 1960s, it cost the global pharmaceutical industry an average of $100 million to take a drug from discovery to the market, today it costs over $2.5 billion [1, 2]. There is disagreement over the root cause of this decline, but broadly it is driven by a high failure rate, with only around 1‐in‐10 drugs that enter clinical trials reaching the market [3, 4]. The integration of machine learning (ML) into the drug discovery process provides an opportunity to reverse this trend. ML is ideally suited to accelerating drug discovery since it is able to accurately capture and model the complexity of biological systems [5]. Capturing this complexity could help unravel the mechanisms that drive the success and failure of different drug candidates, thereby helping us design drugs with better chances of being successful. To do this, most ML algorithms, particularly deep learning, require large volumes of high‐quality data. To provide them this training data, many researchers and companies have focused on the use of publicly available data, hoping to avoid expensive experimental data generation [6]. Unfortunately, public datasets were not produced with ML in mind. The data often contains significant experimental biases. Without the requisite controls for these biases, models will fail to pick up on the underlying parameter, and will instead simply be a model of the experiment. In addition, these experiments are frequently not performed in rigorously controlled conditions, without sufficient replicates to prevent algorithms from overfitting to biological noise. The application of ML in drug discovery is more likely to be successful if backed by an underlying experimental workflow that provides high‐quality training data. To provide an example of such an ML‐driven drug discovery engine, here we summarise LabGenius’ approach to protein engineering in biotherapeutics.

Application of ML to protein engineering

Any new biological therapeutic needs to satisfy a range of biochemical and biophysical requirements in order to qualify as a potential drug candidate. These characteristics are derived from a protein's underlying amino acid sequence. The ability to computationally predict protein fitness from its underlying sequence would be a powerful tool, drastically simplifying and rationalising the design of novel biotherapeutics. Unfortunately, this goal has not yet been achieved because the relationship between sequence and fitness is complex and difficult to model [7]. The traditional approach to engineering novel biological therapeutics uses iterative rounds of directed evolution, taking small steps through sequence space towards variants with higher fitness. These variants are selected from large libraries based on their performance along one particular axis — typically their ability to bind to a target [8, 9, 10]. The resulting pool of variants is then expressed individually and tested according to other important parameters: potency, solubility, expressibility, the propensity to aggregate, stability, immunogenicity, and so on [11]. With luck, a few variants will emerge from the funnel that satisfies all of these criteria. However, because none of these other parameters are factored into the design of the original library, finding these high performing variants fundamentally comes down to chance. Even if a functional molecule is identified, it is incredibly unlikely to be the optimal solution. Often molecules that actually fail some of these tests are pushed through to animal testing and clinical trials anyway in the hope that these red flags will not prove to be ‘show‐stopping’ issues. In reality, these flags are strongly correlated with an antibody's chances of success during clinical trials [12]. An optimal approach may be to co‐optimise for as many of these parameters as possible at the start of a programme. This would increase the flux of high‐performing molecules through in vitro characterisation, delivering only molecules with excellent biochemical and biophysical properties to animal studies and clinical trials. To achieve this, accurate computational models are necessary to predict a protein's performance for multiple biochemical and biophysical properties. To build these models using deep learning, large volumes of high‐quality empirical data linking sequence to fitness are required. Finally, a protein library synthesis method is necessary to faithfully reproduce the predictions recommended by the sequence‐fitness models. Based on these requirements, we present our approach to ML‐driven protein engineering.

LabGenius’ ML‐driven protein engineering platform › Library construction

Conventional antibody engineering relies on antibody libraries derived from animal immune repertoires, followed by optimisation via random mutagenesis [9]. To efficiently explore sequence space, and to enable ML informed library design, it is necessary to be able to precisely control which amino acids are mutated and what they are mutated to. To facilitate this, we construct diverse combinatorial synthetic DNA libraries of between 106 and 1013 variants (depending on the specifics of the library design). This allows us to enrich for functional proteins prior to the application of any selective pressure.

LabGenius’ ML‐driven protein engineering platform › Selection

Next, this library is exposed to various selective pressures during a phage display and selection step. This allows us to bin variants into a range of high‐performing and low‐performing pools. Once selected, these pools are sequenced with next generation sequencing, providing large volumes of DNA sequences linked to their fitness for the screened parameter. Selections performed as part of a platform such as this one are different from traditional selection campaigns. In classic phage display, selection conditions are performed with the express goal of finding the best binders to a given target. This means performing multiple rounds of biopanning and iteratively increasing the stringency of selection to enrich for the tightest binders [8]. Our goal is to map the fitness landscape to enable multi‐parameter optimisation. As such, selection stringency must be carefully optimised — too high, and there will be insufficient data to build a map of the sequence‐fitness landscape, too low and the signal will not be evident through the noise.

LabGenius’ ML‐driven protein engineering platform › Data analysis and ML

The data generated in selections are then used to train ML models, allowing us to build fitness landscape maps based on the library of sequences that we have selected. These maps are iteratively improved and refined as more experimental data is accumulated. This approach allows extrapolation to greater numbers of variants and therefore an exploration of an even greater proportion of possible sequence space. It also enables quicker and cheaper identification of high‐fitness variants and novel proteins that were not present in the starting library. It is important to note that these models are in fact models of the screening method, not a direct model of the underlying parameter of interest. As a result, it is critical that the screen maps as closely as possible to the underlying parameter. A good example of this issue is in phage display: in producing a phage library, Escherichia coli is used as a chassis for phage particle expression. Each variant in the library will be expressed at differing rates, based on the bacteria's ability to do so. This means that before selection, the phage library will be biased towards variants that have been expressed to a higher titre. Training a model on the results of this selection without controlling for this bias will result in the conflation of two properties — expressibility and binding. To truly understand sequence‐fitness landscapes for certain properties then, it is necessary to design assays carefully, factoring in any way in which signal from the screen could be masking the signal from the property of interest.

LabGenius’ ML‐driven protein engineering platform › Data analysis and ML › Modelling sequence‐fitness landscapes

ML is a particularly powerful technique in modelling sequence‐fitness landscapes since it is able to capture interactions between amino acid or nucleotide positions, also known as epistasis. If each position in a sequence had a linear input into the underlying fitness function, the fitness landscape would be smooth and easily predictable. However, protein fitness landscapes are inherently hazardous, and functional proteins are often only a single mutation away from either getting trapped at local maxima or falling into death valleys [13]. The ruggedness of the fitness landscape can, therefore, be characterised by the complexity of the underlying epistatic interactions. Putinseva et al. demonstrate that feed forward neural networks are an excellent tool for inferring genotype‐to‐phenotype relationships [14]. If the sequences are represented in a binary matrix, where each row corresponds to a sequence and each column corresponds to a mutation, then using feed forward neural networks allows co‐optimisation of the impact of each of these mutations, together with the non‐linear function that maps genotype to phenotype. Increasing the complexity of the neural network architecture improved the accuracy of the model. This illustrates that ML is capable of capturing the multidimensional epistatic interactions that govern the shape of the fitness landscape and predicting them with high accuracy.

LabGenius’ ML‐driven protein engineering platform › Data analysis and ML › Multi‐objective optimisation

For each parameter that we test, a new sequence‐fitness landscape is constructed in silico. This allows us to find shared fitness peaks across all of the empirically tested parameters. Using this information, we can construct new DNA libraries to interrogate these high‐performing regions of sequence space. For example, in engineering protease stable binding molecules we would build in silico genotype–phenotype models of protease stability and target binding based on selection data. Next, we would rationalise the search for high performers across these parameters through Pareto‐front optimisation.

Benchmarking ML‐driven biologics discovery platforms

Drug discovery platforms are evaluated based mostly on the success of the molecules that they output. However, this approach to valuing a discovery platform misses the compounding value of ML‐driven discovery platforms. As more data is produced, and as the underlying models become more accurate, these platforms should provide increasing numbers of high‐quality assets. As such, it is important to establish benchmarks that allow us to directly measure the performance of these platforms as opposed to their clinical outputs. Benchmarking ML‐driven biologics discovery platforms › Sequence‐fitness model accuracy

A useful benchmark for sequence‐fitness models is accuracy. Model accuracy quantifies the difference between a model's predictions of fitness and experimentally measured fitness. With an accurate model, less empirical exploration of sequence space is required to find high‐performing variants. To quantify this, the model is asked to classify a number of experimentally characterised DNA sequences that it has not seen before. Its accuracy is defined as the proportion of these sequences that the model calls correctly.

Benchmarking ML‐driven biologics discovery platforms › Effective platform throughput

Since there is a significant amount of noise in biological systems, we can assume that the model's accuracy will be significantly below 100%. As such, we will continue to rely on empirical experimentation to explore the solution space, validate the model's predictions and identify the strongest candidates. Given that the model is identifying potential high‐performing areas of sequence space, it is important that we are able to precisely specify which mutations are tested at each round of empirical screening, as opposed to randomly sampling the space. To capture this, as well as total throughput, we define ‘effective platform throughput’ as the number of planned sequence variants that the platform can produce and screen per unit time (assuming that any screen returns at least one ‘hit’). Each function that the platform optimises for would be benchmarked according to these two factors (see Fig. 1). For example, when searching for binding molecules to a new target, an in silico model is unlikely to be accurate. However, since we can select for new binders at extremely high throughput, the platform is likely to be able to generate novel binding molecules. For a parameter such as immunogenicity, a reliable high‐throughput screen does not currently exist. This means that a more accurate model is required to produce high‐fitness variants. This framework not only allows comparisons between different ML‐driven drug discovery platforms but also enables data‐driven decision‐making within an organisation. Where platform throughput and model accuracy are low, it will be difficult to find high performers. In this case, investment in these weaker areas of a platform would be necessary. Another important aspect of such ML‐driven drug discovery platforms is their ‘generalisability’. For example, to what extent could a model trained on immunogenicity data from immunoglobulin G be extended to predict the immunogenicity of single‐chain fragment variables or immunoglobulinE? More generalisability allows a platform to address a wider array of protein categories, thus providing greater value. While not included as a core aspect of our benchmarking system, generalisability should also be considered when evaluating these systems. One way to do this is to track the model's accuracy when it is asked to predict the fitness of a number of test datasets, each of which having varying degrees of sequence similarity to the training set (see Fig. 2). For example, this would allow us to assess the platform when test data is only 50% similar to the data it was trained with, and compare this to its accuracy when test data is 80% similar to its training data. Conclusions

There is great promise in the application of ML and computation in drug discovery. However, to extract the most value from these technologies, we suggest that drug discovery platforms should be built from the ground up with ML in mind. We have presented LabGenius’ approach to building such a platform, focusing on protein engineering for biotherapeutic drug discovery. This platform enables multi‐parameter optimisation across multiple biochemical and biophysical characteristics from the very beginning of a drug discovery campaign. This has the potential to accelerate the discovery of high‐performing leads, reduce the failure rate, and increase the efficiency of the discovery process by enabling projects to be cancelled earlier. We have also presented a framework for objectively evaluating ML‐driven drug discovery platforms that captures their compounding value.
