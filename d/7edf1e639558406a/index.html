Abstract

Machine learning is increasingly used to explore structure-property relationships in dendrimer-drug systems; however, its integration with topology-based structural compatibility analysis remains limited. This study presents a learning-driven framework to evaluate the topology-based structural compatibility of perylene diimide core dendrimers with antiviral, antioxidant, and imaging agents. Within a quantitative structure-property relationship framework, linear regression, Gaussian process regression (GPR), and artificial neural networks are employed to identify dominant graph-theoretic topological indices and to capture linear and nonlinear structure-property relationships. These insights are combined with a structure-based compatibility analysis that ranks dendrimer generations without altering drug identity or ordering. Generation-wise compatibility is computed using standardized Euclidean distance after global normalization, and ranking stability is assessed through monotonic and magnitude analysis. Results show class-dependent descriptor relevance, with GPR achieving test-set R2 values above 0.94 for molecular weight, exact mass, monoisotopic mass, and heavy atom count. The generation-wise compatibility analysis further reveals distinct topology-based structural compatibility patterns across the investigated drug classes, highlighting the value of integrating machine learning with graph-theoretic analysis for rational nanocarrier design and the computational assessment of potential encapsulation suitability.

Introduction

Advances in nanotechnology have significantly transformed modern pharmaceutical and biomedical sciences by enabling the development of nanoscale delivery platforms capable of improving drug solubility, stability, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy. Among the wide range of nanocarriers investigated for biomedical applications, dendrimers have emerged as one of the most structurally versatile and controllable nanoscale systems [1, 2]. These highly branched macromolecules typically possess diameters ranging from ≈1 to 100 nm and are constructed through iterative branching processes that generate well‐defined generational architectures [3, 4, 5]. Structurally, dendrimers consist of three key components: a multifunctional central core, repetitive branching units forming successive generations, and numerous terminal functional groups that determine the physicochemical behavior of the molecular surface. The term dendrimer originates from the Greek word dendron, meaning tree, reflecting the characteristic tree‐like topology of these macromolecules [6, 7]. A schematic representation of the general dendrimer architecture is presented in Figure 1, highlighting the central core, branching layers, terminal functional groups, and internal cavities capable of hosting guest molecules [8]. Dendrimers have a unique architecture, endowing a number of characteristics that are beneficial when compared to those of traditional linear or randomly‐branched polymers. Their monodisperse nature gives strict control of molecular size and weight during synthesis, and the large quantity of surface functional groups increases their solubility, reactivity, and molecular recognition. Moreover, the internal cavities created inside the branched structure allow the encapsulation of guest molecules by means of hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, or electrostatic interactions. Such structural features have prompted the widespread use of dendrimers in drug delivery, gene transfection, diagnostic imaging, catalysis, and antimicrobial therapy applications, among others, as demonstrated in [9, 10]. The growing biomedical importance of dendrimers is also reflected in the increasing number of dendrimer‐based formulations that have progressed toward clinical translation. There are a number of dendrimer‐based systems which are already commercialized, and others are at different stages of clinical trials to be used in oncology, antiviral therapy, and inflammatory diseases. Representative examples of commercially available dendrimer products and therapeutic candidates in clinical development are tabulated in Tables 1 and 2. In addition to experimental and clinical advances, dendrimers also offer a desirable structure in terms of theoretical and computational analyses because of their very regular and hierarchical patterns of branches. Such systems can be represented by a natural mathematical form of chemical graph theory, with molecules represented as graphs with various vertices corresponding to atoms and edges corresponding to chemical bonds [21, 22]. In this framework, topological indices are graph invariants that give compact numerical indicators that can be used to describe key structural features like branching pattern, connectivity, and molecular complexity of a system [23]. Among the numerous topological descriptors proposed in the literature, the Leap Zagreb indices and the Wiener polarity index have proven particularly useful for analyzing highly branched macromolecular systems. These descriptors quantify different aspects of molecular connectivity and branching architecture and have been widely applied in quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) studies [24, 25, 26, 27]. The first Leap Zagreb index is defined as where d2(v) represents the number of vertices located at topological distance two from vertex v in graph G. This index captures second‐order branching characteristics and has been associated with structural compactness and molecular stability [25, 26]. The second leap Zagreb index is expressed as which describes interactions between second‐order neighborhoods of adjacent vertices and therefore reflects higher‐order connectivity patterns within the molecular graph [25]. The third Leap Zagreb index combines first‐ and second‐order degree information and is defined as where d1(v) denotes the degree of vertex v. This descriptor captures both local and extended branching features and is useful for analyzing dendrimer size, connectivity, and encapsulation potential [26]. Another widely used topological descriptor is the Wiener polarity index which counts the number of vertex pairs separated by a topological distance of three in the molecular graph and provides insight into the complexity of branching architecture [21, 27]. Because dendrimers possess well‐defined generational architectures, these graph‐theoretical descriptors provide a powerful framework for analyzing how structural variations influence physicochemical behavior and drug–carrier interactions. Understanding the relationship between dendrimer generation and drug compatibility is therefore essential for the rational design of nanoscale delivery systems. Despite the growing use of dendrimers as nanocarriers and the increasing application of machine learning in drug‐related structure–property studies, an integrated computational framework that combines graph‐theoretical characterization of perylene diimide (PDI) dendrimer generations with topology‐based compatibility analysis and machine‐learning‐driven QSPR interpretation remains limited. In particular, the generation‐wise structural compatibility of PDI core dendrimers with chemically diverse drug and imaging agents has not been systematically investigated using standardized topological descriptors. Moreover, the extent to which these descriptors encode physicochemical information relevant to drug‐dendrimer matching across different compound classes remains insufficiently explored. This gap motivates the present study, which integrates topological indexing, standardized structural compatibility analysis, generation‐wise ranking, and QSPR modeling to provide a quantitative computational framework for evaluating potential dendrimer‐drug compatibility. In this work, we present a systematic structural analysis of dendrimer‐drug interactions by integrating chemical graph theory, similarity‐based compatibility analysis, and machine learning‐driven QSPR modeling. Specifically, PDI core dendrimers across multiple generations are investigated in order to evaluate their compatibility with a diverse set of therapeutic and imaging drugs. Four topological descriptors of the dendrimer architecture, namely, the Leap Zagreb indices (LM1, LM2, and LM3) and the Wiener polarity index (W p), are computed to characterize generational branching patterns. These descriptors are combined with physicochemical properties of drug molecules to construct a multidimensional descriptor space in which generation‐wise compatibility between dendrimers and drugs is quantified using normalized Euclidean similarity metrics. The resulting compatibility scores allow a systematic ranking of dendrimer generations with respect to predicted encapsulation suitability. Furthermore, machine learning‐based QSPR models are employed to analyze how dendrimer topological descriptors correlate with drug physicochemical properties. This integrated framework provides a quantitative and reproducible approach for identifying structurally compatible dendrimer generations and offers insights into the rational design of dendrimer‐based nanocarriers for drug delivery and biomedical applications. Machine Learning Framework for QSPR Interpretation › Computation of Graph Theoretic Topological Indices

To quantitatively encode the structural characteristics of PDI core dendrimers, a set of graph‐theoretic topological indices is computed from the molecular graph representation of each dendrimer generation. These indices provide compact numerical descriptors that capture connectivity patterns, branching behavior, and global structural organization without relying on 3D conformational information. In this study, four topological indices, namely, LM1, LM2, LM3, and W p, are employed. The Leap Zagreb indices are degree‐based measures derived from vertex degree distributions and their interactions within the dendrimer graph. They reflect variations in branching intensity and local connectivity as the dendrimer generation increases. By comparison, the w p index is a distance‐based measure, which includes path length information and measures global structural compactness of the dendrimer. One of the most vital elements to compute these indices is called the second neighbour degree, and it defines the number of nodes directly two edges away from a particular node. Accurately determining this quantity allows for the precise calculation of distance‐ and degree‐based descriptors, which in turn reflect the dendrimer's branching complexity and overall topology. There is also a significant linear tendency in molecular complexity. Conversely, the polarity and hydrogen‐bonding descriptors, including TPSA and the number of hydrogen bond donors, exhibit a weak or statistically nonsignificant correlation, and those of XLogP, hydrogen bond acceptors, and rotatable bonds have moderate predictive capability. The same is manifested in the antiviral compounds, where the mass‐ and connectivity‐driven properties (molecular weight, exact mass, monoisotopic mass, the count of heavy atoms, and complexity) have statistically significant and strong linear relationships with W p (maximum R2 of 0.78). Overall, the single‐descriptor QSPR analysis establishes a reliable baseline for interpreting linear structure–property relationships and highlights the necessity of incorporating multivariate or nonlinear modeling approaches to adequately describe more complex physicochemical properties. Machine Learning Framework for QSPR Interpretation › Nonlinear Machine Learning Models › Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)

GPR was employed to model nonlinear dependencies between the selected topological indices and the molecular properties. GPR is a nonparametric Bayesian regression technique that places a Gaussian process prior over the space of possible functions and learns the relationship directly from the observed data. This framework is particularly suitable for small‐ to medium‐sized datasets and provides both predictive estimates and uncertainty quantification. Mathematically, a Gaussian process is defined as where m(x) denotes the mean function and k(x,x′) represents the covariance kernel describing the similarity between data points. The radial basis function (RBF) kernel was used in this work due to its capability of modeling nonlinear, smooth relations in the multidimensional space of descriptors. The kernel‐based formulation enables complex interactions of structural properties to be learnt by GPR with no explicit functional structure being assumed, and also gives confidence limits around values predicted. The comparative predictions between GPR and linear regression models (based on high coefficients of determination, R 2) showed better predictive performance of the GPR as compared to the linear regression models, as well as a better prediction error. For reproducibility, a fixed random seed of 123 was used throughout the analysis. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training and independent test subsets using an 80:20 split implemented through the createDataPartition function in the caret package. The four topological descriptors (LM1, LM2, LM3, and W p) were standardized using centering and scaling parameters estimated exclusively from the training subset; the same transformation was subsequently applied to the test subset. Separate GPR models were fitted for each physicochemical property using the RBF kernel implemented through the kernlab package. The variance parameter was fixed at 0.01 in the GPR implementation. No separate k‐fold cross‐validation or LOOCV procedure was used for GPR model selection; instead, predictive performance was assessed using the independent 20% test subset. The model performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination (R 2), RMSE, and mean absolute error (MAE). In addition, 200 bootstrap resamples of the training data were generated to estimate the uncertainty bands around the GPR prediction curves.

Machine Learning Framework for QSPR Interpretation › Nonlinear Machine Learning Models › Comparative Evaluation of Model Performance Across Therapeutic Classes

To systematically assess model robustness and generalization, GPR was evaluated across all therapeutic classes using consistent statistical criteria. Single‐descriptor linear regression models provide an interpretable baseline for understanding structure–property relationships; however, the statistical results summarized in Tables 13–15 demonstrate that only a subset of physicochemical properties can be reliably predicted using linear mappings of individual topological descriptors. Specifically, the measurements of size‐dependent properties like molecular weight, exact mass, monoisotopic mass, number of heavy atoms, and molecular complexity are all strongly correlated (R 2 ≈ 0.69–0.89, p<10−4) with each other, indicating that the properties are largely determined by global structural topology as captured in graph‐based indices. Conversely, the relationships between polarity‐based descriptors, hydrogen bonding parameters, lipophilicity, and conformational flexibility tend to show weak correlation (R2<0.30) associated with low statistical significance in antioxidant, antiviral, and imaging datasets. These findings suggest that these properties are nonlinear structural effects that are not well explained by linear relationships using a single descriptor. In order to address this shortcoming, GPR was used as a nonlinear model structure that can learn complex functional relationships as well as measure predictive uncertainty at the same time. Even though GPR significantly enhanced the prediction of various nonlinear structure–property relations, some physicochemical descriptors were still poorly modeled. This brought about a significant question: are these limitations due to a lack of model flexibility, or are the limitations inherent to the chosen set of graph‐based descriptors due to a lack of information? To examine this difference, an ANN model was used as a nonlinear learning framework with a higher capacity. Structural Compatibility and Generation‐Wise Structural Selectivity

In this section, a structure‐based discussion of the suitability of encapsulation in PDI core dendrimers is conducted. Although the above learning‐based QSPR model establishes relationships between descriptor properties, the aspect of encapsulation is assessed here in terms of direct structural compatibility between dendrimer generations and drug molecules. The following section outlines the entire computation pipeline to determine the extent to which each generation of PDI dendrimer can be compatible with a wide range of drug molecules. The workflow integrates graph‐based topological descriptors, distance‐based compatibility assessment, and generation‐wise ranking analysis to identify structurally suitable dendrimer generations for downstream investigation. The computational process is initiated by the generation‐wise computations of the topological indices in terms of graphs of the PDI core dendrimer family. Simultaneously, topological descriptors were computed on each drug molecule so as to obtain a consistent space of structural features. The sets of these descriptors are then assembled together to create a single dataset with which it becomes possible to directly compare structures between dendrimer generations and drug molecules. In the present study, the term compatibility refers specifically to the structural proximity between PDI dendrimer generation and a drug molecule within the standardized space of the selected topological descriptors. The compatibility score is therefore a computational similarity measure and does not represent a direct experimental measurement of encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity, binding affinity, or release behavior. Accordingly, the generation‐wise results should be interpreted as identifying dendrimer generations with comparatively similar topological profiles to the investigated drug molecules, thereby providing a computational indication of potential suitability for further encapsulation studies. The present analysis is based exclusively on graph‐theoretical topological descriptors and standardized structural compatibility and therefore does not directly account for the energetic, conformational, electronic, or intermolecular factors governing drug‐dendrimer interactions. Consequently, the compatibility scores should not be interpreted as direct evidence of binding affinity or experimental encapsulation efficiency. Molecular docking, binding energy calculations, molecular dynamics simulations, and experimental encapsulation studies could provide complementary information for validating the topology‐based predictions. Such investigations are beyond the scope of the present work and represent important directions for future research. Encapsulation compatibility has then been measured by computing standardized Euclidean distances among dendrimer and drug descriptor vectors. Depending on these scores, generation‐wise ranking vectors are built to assess the potential of encapsulation with the same drug identity and ordering not changed. The obtained distance values are converted to compatibility scores, indicating the comparative structural proximity of each dendrimer generation to the investigated drug molecules within the selected topological descriptor space. To make sure that the resulting rankings are representative of systematic structural behavior but not random fluctuations, both monotonic rank consistency and magnitude‐based compatibility tendencies are used to conduct stability analysis. Lastly, the analysis identifies representative dendrimer generations exhibiting the highest topology‐based compatibility with the investigated drug classes, providing a computational basis for further encapsulation studies. Conclusion

To examine drug–dendrimer interactions in PDI‐based dendritic structures with graph‐theoretic topological indices, we developed a computationally grounded, chemically based strategy in the present study. The Leap Zagreb indices (LM1, LM2, and LM3) and the Wiener polarity index (W p) offered a strict structural description of both dendrimers and drug molecules, which could then be compared chemically, having undergone global standardization of descriptors. Standardized Euclidean distance gave rise to compatibility scoring, which showed generation‐specific encapsulation patterns, with the least PDI dendrimer generations (D0−D2) showing the most desirable structural correspondence of most therapeutic agents. Generation stability diagnostics also ensured that these trends are systematic and based on true topological differences and not stochastic effects. The complementary use of QSPR‐based neural regression analysis showed that the topological indices are strongly related to major physicochemical properties, molecular size, polarity, hydrogen‐bonding potential, conformational flexibility, and complexity of the π‐systems, making them valid, chemically interpretable descriptors. The results indicate that the investigated PDI dendrimer generations exhibit distinct topology‐based compatibility profiles with the studied drug molecules. These computational patterns provide structural indications that may support the selection of dendrimer generations for further investigation, while direct encapsulation behavior requires complementary molecular‐level and experimental validation. The integrated methodology presented here provides a robust platform for rational dendrimer design, predictive drug carrier matching, and future development of chemically informed nanocarrier optimization strategies. Future studies should extend the present analysis to chemically modified PDI dendrimers, including terminal group functionalization and heteroatom‐substituted π‐cores, to examine how electronic and steric perturbations alter host‐guest selectivity. Incorporating solvent explicit simulations and quantum chemical descriptors may further clarify the mechanistic basis of π‐driven encapsulation. Experimental studies measuring encapsulation efficiency, loading capacity, and release behavior would provide complementary validation of the topology‐based compatibility predictions. On the predictive side, expanding the neural QSPR model with additional molecular features or generative architectures could enable inverse design of dendrimer carriers optimized for specific drug classes. Together, these directions will help establish a more comprehensive and chemically interpretable framework for rational engineering of PDI‐based nanocarriers.
