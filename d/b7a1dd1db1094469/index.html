Abstract

Cardiotoxicity is a critical issue in drug development that poses serious health risks, including potentially fatal arrhythmias. The human ether-à-go-go related gene (hERG) potassium channel, as one of the primary targets of cardiotoxicity, has garnered widespread attention. Traditional cardiotoxicity testing methods are expensive and time-consuming, making computational virtual screening a suitable alternative. In this study, we employed machine learning techniques utilizing molecular fingerprints and descriptors to predict the cardiotoxicity of compounds, with the aim of improving prediction accuracy and efficiency. We used four types of molecular fingerprints and descriptors combined with machine learning and deep learning algorithms, including Gaussian naive Bayes (NB), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and Transformer models, to build predictive models. Our models demonstrated advanced predictive performance. The best machine learning model, XGBoost Morgan, achieved an accuracy (ACC) value of 0.84, and the deep learning model, Transformer_Morgan, achieved the best ACC value of 0.85, showing a high ability to distinguish between toxic and non-toxic compounds. On an external independent validation set, it achieved the best area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.93, surpassing ADMETlab3.0, Cardpred, and CardioDPi. In addition, we explored the integration of molecular descriptors and fingerprints to enhance model performance and found that ensemble methods, such as voting and stacking, provided slight improvements in model stability. Furthermore, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) explanations revealed the relationship between benzene rings, fluorine-containing groups, NH groups, oxygen in ether groups, and cardiotoxicity, highlighting the importance of these features. This study not only improved the predictive accuracy of cardiotoxicity models but also promoted a more reliable and scientifically interpretable method for drug safety assessment. Using computational methods, this study facilitates a more efficient drug development process, reduces costs, and improves the safety of new drug candidates, ultimately benefiting medical and public health.

Introduction

Cardiotoxicity is a critical issue in drug development because of its potential to cause severe health risks, including fatal arrhythmias [1,2]. The voltage-gated potassium channel, human ether-à-go-go related gene (hERG), plays a key role in the repolarization of cardiac action potentials, and its blockade can lead to prolonged Q- to T-wave (QT) intervals, potentially triggering fatal cardiac arrhythmias [[3], [4], [5]]. Therefore, the inhibition of the hERG channel is an important marker for assessing cardiotoxicity [6,7]. In addition, other ion channels, such as Nav1.5 and Cav1.2 also play significant roles in cardiac electrophysiology, and their blockade can lead to severe cardiotoxicity [8,9]. Traditional experimental methods effective in detecting cardiotoxicity have significant limitations [10,11]. First, these bioassays are generally expensive and time-consuming, limiting the feasibility of large-scale screening [12]. Second, traditional experimental approaches often focus on endpoint toxicity and concentration, overlooking the relationship between a compound's molecular structure and toxicity [13]. This makes it challenging for researchers to understand the mechanisms of compound toxicity and predict the toxicity of new compounds. In recent years, machine learning methods based on molecular fingerprints and descriptors have shown great potential for predicting the cardiotoxicity of compounds [[14], [15], [16]]. Molecular fingerprints capture the structural features of the compounds, whereas molecular descriptors provide quantitative descriptions of their physicochemical properties [17,18]. Research using these two types of input features can more accurately predict compound toxicity. For instance, Lee et al. [19] developed a deep learning model using molecular fingerprints to target hERG channel blockade and achieved an accuracy of 61.4% on an external validation set. Wang et al. [20] developed a deep learning-based DMFGAM model by combining molecular fingerprints with molecular graph features, which achieved an accuracy of 74.0% on an external validation set. In addition, Transformer models have exhibited exceptional performance in processing sequence data, making them potentially effective for predicting molecular properties [21]. In this study, we utilized four types of molecular fingerprints and one molecular descriptor, combined with five machine learning models and Transformer deep learning models, to construct various combination models, achieving an optimal area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93 and accuracy (ACC) of 0.85. In addition, we explored whether integrating molecular descriptors and fingerprints could enhance the performance of cardiotoxicity prediction models. We further investigated the potential of model fusion to predict cardiotoxicity by constructing eight ensemble models. Our results indicated that while the overall AUC values did not change significantly with the addition of molecular descriptors, the models showed slight improvements in stability through multiple training based on voting and stacking. Specifically, based on Morgan fingerprints, the interpretation of structural features of molecular toxicity revealed that phenyl rings, NH groups, oxygen-containing groups, and fluorine-containing groups significantly influenced the prediction of molecular cardiotoxicity. Materials and methods › Data collection and standardization

Datasets for hERG channel blockers were collected from PubChem [25], ChEMBL [26], BindingDB [27], and PubMed [28]. The integrated dataset included over 20,000 different compounds with simplified molecular input line entry system (SMILES) structures [29] and negative logarithm of the half maximal inhibitory concentration (pIC50) values. The molecular structures within the dataset were processed using the standardized package (version 0.1.9, https://github.com/flatkinson/standardiser), which involved the removal of counterions, solvent fractions, and salts, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms, to obtain a standardized set of 22,247 small-molecule SMILES data. We normalized the entire data set, all compounds were classified based on their pIC50 values according to the standard used by researchers in this field: compounds with pIC50 ≥ 5 were labeled as 1 half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 10 μM or below, and those with pIC50 < 5 (IC50 values higher than 10 μM) were labeled as 0. The dataset was then randomly split into training and testing sets in an 8:2 ratio. The training set contains 17,797 molecules and the test set contains 4,461 molecules. Specifically, for the positive class (label 1) and negative class (label 0), the training set contained 7,055 positive samples and 10,742 negative samples, whereas the test set contained 1,775 positive samples and 2,686 negative samples.

Materials and methods › Molecular feature evaluation of the dataset

We used the RDKit cheminformatics library [30] to parse the SMILES strings of each molecule in the dataset and converted them into molecular objects. Using the descriptors module and the MoleculeDescriptors module in RDKit [31], a series of molecular descriptors were calculated, including molecular weight (MolWt) [32], topological polar surface area (TPSA) [33], number of hydrogen bond donors (NumHDonors) [34], number of hydrogen bond acceptors (NumHAcceptors) [34], Molecular LogP [35], and molecular volume (MolMR) [36]. After calculating the properties of all molecules, all molecular descriptors were normalized using StandardScaler [37]. Distribution plots for each property were created using the histplot function from the Seaborn Library [38], with molecules categorized as toxic or non-toxic based on their classification.

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation

In this study, five types of molecular fingerprints were used to construct fingerprint-based predictive models: Morgan fingerprints (extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP), 2048 bits) [39], Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) keys (166 bits) [40], AtomPairs fingerprints (2048 bits) [41], and fingerprint 2 (FP2) fingerprints (1024 bits) [42]. In addition, a set of 208 RDKit molecular descriptors (RDKitDes) were selected to develop descriptor-based predictive models [43]. RDKit offers a variety of descriptors, including physicochemical properties, topological indices, geometric descriptors, and electronic descriptors, that comprehensively characterize the features of molecules. In practice, we used Descriptors.descList to generate all available descriptors and calculated them for each molecule using the get_rdkit_descriptor function. The results for all the generated molecular descriptors are listed in Table S1. These fingerprints and descriptors were calculated using the open-source RDKit software (version 2020.03.1; http://www.rdkit.org/). For the machine learning models, we employed the Gaussian naive Bayes (NB) [44], random forest (RF) [45], support vector machine (SVM) [46], K-nearest neighbors (KNN) [47], and eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) [48] models to optimize the hyperparameters for each model to enhance predictive performance.

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › Gaussian NB

The Gaussian NB classifier is based on Bayes' theorem and is widely used in molecular property predictions and virtual screening projects. To optimize the NB model, we adjusted two hyperparameters: alpha (0.01–1) and binarize (0, 0.5, and 0.8).

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › RF

The RF model, developed by Svetnik et al. [49], is an ensemble recursive partitioning method where each recursive partition “tree” is constructed from a bootstrapped sample of compounds, and each branch of the tree uses a random subset of descriptors. We optimized the following five hyperparameters to achieve the best RF model: n_estimators (100, 200, and 300), criterion (“gini” and “entropy”), max_depth (5, 10, 20, 30, and none), min_samples_leaf (1–10), and max_features (“log2,” “auto,” and “sqrt”).

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › SVM

The SVM was formally developed in 1999 [46] and has quickly become a mainstream machine learning method owing to its excellent performance in text classification tasks. The SVM determines the optimal hyperplane in the feature space by maximizing the margin between classes, thereby distinguishing objects with different labels. We optimized two hyperparameters for the SVM model: the kernel coefficient (gamma, “auto”, and 0.1–0.2) and the penalty parameter for error terms (C, the regularization parameter, with values of 0.1, 1, 10, and 100).

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › KNN

KNN is a commonly used supervised learning method with a simple mechanism. For a given test sample, it finds the K-nearest training samples based on distance metrics (such as Manhattan, Euclidean, and Jaccard distances) and then predicts based on the information of these K “neighbors”. We optimized three hyperparameters for the KNN model: n_neighbors (3, 5, 7, 10, 15, and 20).

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › XGBoost

XGBoost is one of the most representative ensemble machine learning algorithms under the gradient boosting framework. It has achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on many standard classification benchmark datasets. We optimized seven hyperparameters for the XGBoost model: learning_rate (0.01–0.1), n_estimators (100, 200, and 300), max_depth (3, 5, 7, 10, 15, and 20), min_child_weight (1–3), gamma (0–0.1), subsample (0.8–1.0), and colsample_bytree (0.8–1.0).

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › Transformer models

We designed and implemented a deep learning model based on the Transformer architecture [50] for the binary classification of molecular fingerprint features. The model was built using the PyTorch library [51] and deployed on a compute unified device architecture (CUDA) device to leverage the graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration. The core of the model consists of a module comprising four Transformer encoder layers [50]. Each Transformer encoder contains a multihead self-attention mechanism and a feedforward neural network, with each input feature dimension [52] set to 2048 to match the molecular fingerprint feature dimensions. The number of heads in the multi-head attention mechanism [53] was set to 64 to allow the model to learn more fine-grained feature representations, and the feedforward neural network dimension [54] was set to 2048 to ensure that the features maintained the same dimensions as they passed through the feedforward network. The first dimension of the input data was set as the batch dimension (batch_first = true) to accommodate the batch input. The model comprises multiple stacked Transformer encoder layers that extract higher-level feature representations through multilayer self-attention mechanisms and feedforward neural networks. The final classification layer was a linear layer (in_features = 2048 and out_features = 1) that mapped the features extracted by the Transformer encoder to the output space. To simplify the training process, we use the binary cross-entropy loss function (BCEWithLogitsLoss) [55], which combines sigmoid activation and binary cross-entropy loss. During forward propagation of the model, the input data first pass through the Transformer encoder layers for feature extraction, and the output features are then passed through the linear classification layer to generate the classification results. If labeled data are provided, the model calculates the training loss. With this design, our model can extract rich higher-level features from molecular fingerprints for classification tasks.

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › Ensemble models

In addition, to improve model performance, we employed two ensemble learning methods: stacking and voting. The stacking model consists of base learners and meta-learners. A meta-learner can be a RF classifier or an XGBoost classifier. The base learners were first trained on the original data to generate preliminary predictions, from which the meta-learner learned to produce the final prediction. The voting model is divided into hard and soft voting. Hard voting makes majority decisions based on predictions of the base models, whereas soft voting [56] uses the predicted probabilities of the base models to compute a weighted average. In this study, we used the soft voting method, in which the predicted probabilities of several different models were weighted and averaged to generate the final prediction.

Materials and methods › Model construction and evaluation › Performance evaluation indicators

We used several key performance indicators during model training and evaluation: the AUC, sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), ACC, precision, F1 score (F1), and balanced accuracy (BA). The AUC provides a comprehensive measure of model classification performance, summarizing it across all possible thresholds. SE measures the ability of a model to correctly identify true positive cases, whereas SP measures its ability to correctly identify true negative cases. MCC assesses the quality of binary classifications, considering all four confusion matrix categories, ranging from −1 (complete misclassification) to 1 (perfect classification). ACC is the proportion of correctly classified samples, and precision is the proportion of true positives among the predicted positives. The F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, making it useful for imbalanced datasets. BA is the average of the SE and SP, providing a fair evaluation of the imbalanced classes. These indicators enabled us to comprehensively assess the performance of each model in predicting cardiotoxicity, ensuring that both positive and negative samples were accurately identified. In the context of cardiotoxicity prediction, the balance between false positive and false negative rates is crucial for optimizing model utility across different stages of drug development. False positive results, where a non-cardiotoxic compound is incorrectly classified as cardiotoxic, can lead to the unnecessary exclusion of promising drug candidates. This, in turn, increases costs and delays drug development. On the other hand, false negative results, where a truly cardiotoxic compound is missed, pose a severe risk to patient safety, potentially resulting in adverse effects during clinical trials or post-market use. Given these considerations, decision preferences are often stage-specific: during early screening, minimizing false negatives is prioritized to ensure that no harmful compounds are overlooked, even if this increases the false positive rate (FPR). Conversely, in later validation stages, reducing false positives becomes more important to avoid wasting resources on compounds ultimately deemed safe.

Materials and methods › Methods comparison

To validate the optimal model and compare it with SOTA methods, we used an external validation set of 100 molecules (50 toxic and 50 non-toxic) as an external independent verification set, which was different from the molecules in the training set. We have provided the distribution of pIC50 values for the 100 molecules used in our comparison. These molecules were evenly split, with 50 classified as toxic and 50 classified as non-toxic. A distribution plot is shown in Fig. S1. We predicted their toxicity using three online platforms, ADMETlab3.0 [57], CardioDPi [58], and Cardpred [19], which provided scores ranging 0–1. We classified scores ranging 0–0.5 as non-toxic (label 0) and scores ranging 0.5–1 as toxic (label 1). To evaluate the models on an external validation set, we calculated the AUCs for the three additional models. Using the prediction scores (cardpred, admet3, and CardioDPi) and the True label column, we applied the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC)_curve function to compute the FPR and true positive rate (TPR) for each model, and used the area under the ROC curve (AUC) to evaluate overall classification performance (Fig. S2). In addition, we performed analysis of variance (ANOVA) analysis on three independent external validation sets for the five models (Fig. S3).

Materials and methods › SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis

To interpret the predictions of the XGBoost_Morgan model, we employed SHAP [59], which provide consistent and locally accurate feature attributions. The explainer computed the SHAP values and quantified the contribution of each feature to the predictions of the model. Then, we calculated the SHAP values for the test dataset, which indicated the impact of each feature on the model output for each sample. We generated several plots to visualize the importance and effects of these features. A SHAP summary plot was created to show the overall importance of each feature and its impact distribution across all samples. This plot highlights the most influential features and their positive and negative contributions to predictions. In addition, we produced SHAP dependence plots for the top 20 features. These plots illustrate the relationship between each feature and the model output, providing insights into how changes in feature values affect predictions. To further investigate the specific molecular substructures associated with these important features, we identified the top 20 features based on mean absolute SHAP values. For each of these features, a substructure search was conducted within the molecules in the dataset to identify those containing specific substructures. The identified molecules were visualized, and images of both molecules and their key fragments were saved using the RDKit library.

Materials and methods › Batch molecular docking to investigate the impact of important substructures on protein-molecule binding

To further investigate the key substructures identified by the SHAP analysis, we performed molecular modifications using Avogadro. For features 175, 429, 121, and 322, we selected ten toxic molecules containing these substructures for modification. The benzene ring was removed for feature 175; for feature 429, we removed the fluorine atom for feature 121, the NH group for feature 322, and the oxygen atom from the ether group connected to the benzene ring. Modifications were performed without introducing new groups and replacing atoms that could be substituted with carbon wherever possible. After modification, we optimized the molecular conformations using a general amber force field (GAFF) [60]. Subsequently, we conducted batch docking using AutoDock Vina [61] to compare the binding energies of the original and modified molecules with those of hERG. This approach enabled us to quantify the impact of removing specific substructures on the binding affinity, providing insights into the role of these substructures in molecular interactions with the target protein. The results were analyzed by comparing the binding energies before and after modification, highlighting the significance of the identified substructures in the context of cardiotoxicity.

Results and discussion › Performance of Transformer models

Table 1 and Fig. 4A present the performances of the different Transformer models using four types of fingerprints as inputs (Morgan, MACCS, FP2, and AtomPairs). Results and discussion › Analysis of key features identified by SHAP and their impact on hERG binding

Model interpretability is crucial in drug design and SHAP analysis provides valuable insights into the influence of molecular features on model predictions. Because the Transformer model is a black box with poor interpretability, we used the best-performing machine learning model, XGBoost_Morgan, for the SHAP analysis. The SHAP beeswarm plot (Fig. 7A) illustrates the effect of each feature on the output of the XGBoost model, with colors indicating the feature values (red for high and blue for low). Feature 175, representing the benzene ring, had the greatest impact on the model predictions, followed by feature 429 (fluorine-containing group) and feature 121 (NH group). High values of these features generally increase the model output, whereas low values decrease it. The mean SHAP value bar plot (Fig. 7B) further confirmed the importance of feature 175, showing that it had the highest average SHAP value, indicating its significant role in predicting hERG binding affinity. Fig. 7C shows the 20 most important substructures, highlighting their key roles in predicting cardiotoxicity and providing valuable molecular structure references for drug design. Feature 175, the benzene ring, significantly contributed to the lipophilicity (LogP or LogD) of the molecule. Since the central cavity of the hERG protein is a long hydrophobic pocket, reducing the lipophilicity of drug molecules in this region can effectively hinder hydrophobic interactions with hERG. Strategies to achieve this include increasing the polar surface area (PSA), introducing electron-withdrawing groups on the aromatic rings, and replacing low-LogP-ring systems with bioisosteres. Feature 429, which represents fluorine-containing groups, influences the electronic properties and metabolic stability of molecules and is often associated with increased binding affinity. The removal of fluorine can reduce these effects, potentially lowering the toxicity of the compound. Feature 322, which involves oxygen in the ether groups, affects the hydrophilicity and hydrogen-bonding capabilities of the molecule. Altering these features can modify the interaction of the molecule with hERG, although the impact is generally smaller than that of benzene and fluorine groups.Fig. 7Interpretability SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) graph based on Morgan fingerprints. Conclusion

This study presents a detailed analysis of the cardiotoxicity prediction of small molecules using machine learning models, with a focus on hERG channel toxicity. Various molecular fingerprints and descriptors have been used for the models, such as Gaussian NB, RF, SVM, KNN, XGBoost, and Transformer. The Transformer_Morgan model demonstrated the highest performance, particularly in terms of AUC-ROC, SE, ACC, and F1, indicating its strong predictive power and robustness. SHAP analysis of the XGBoost_Morgan model highlighted key molecular features such as the benzene ring (feature 175), fluorine-containing groups (feature 429), NH groups (feature 121), and oxygen in ether groups (feature 322) as significant contributors to the predictions of the model. These features were validated by molecular docking studies, which showed that removing these substructures decreased their binding affinity to the hERG protein, emphasizing their importance in hERG toxicity. Ensemble methods such as voting and stacking models were also explored with the stacking strategy, particularly combining XGBoost models with descriptors, showing superior performance. However, the Transformer models did not show significant improvement over the ensemble strategy. A comparative analysis with other SOTA models indicated that the Transformer_Morgan model consistently outperformed other models in predicting hERG toxicity. Overall, this study highlights the efficacy of combining molecular descriptors and fingerprints, and provides a reliable framework for predicting cardiotoxicity, aiding in safer drug design and development.
