Abstract

Existing evaluations rely heavily on retrospective testing and algorithm-centric metrics, while current guidelines emphasize reporting standards rather than specifying validation across stages of model maturity. This study proposes a five-phase evaluation framework for medical AI, supported by a dynamic evaluation architecture reflecting the nonlinear, iterative nature of AI systems. The framework integrates technical validation, operational robustness validation, controlled interaction validation, clinical evidence validation, and real-world integration validation, while incorporating phase-gating criteria and local and systemic fall-back triggers. These mechanisms enable re-entry into earlier phases based on drift, version updates, or safety signals, and accommodate parallel activities such as implementation research informing clinical trials. By systematically mapping multicenter external validation, shadow-mode testing, human-AI comparison and cooperation studies, randomized controlled trials, real-world evaluations, and adaptive designs into a coherent lifecycle pathway, the framework addresses persistent gaps between laboratory performance and clinical benefit. It provides researchers, clinical institutions, and regulators with an operational, scalable approach aligned with evolving regulatory expectations, supporting trustworthy, ethically aligned, and lifecycle-based evidence generation for medical AI systems.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has advanced rapidly across diagnostic, prognostic, and clinical decision-support applications, 1–3 including imaging4–6 and pathology7–9, yet the pathway from laboratory performance to demonstrable clinical benefit remains methodologically underdeveloped10–12. Most current studies rely on retrospective testing13, focusing on algorithmic metrics under controlled conditions. However, clinical practice is primarily concerned with whether AI improves diagnostic workflows and patient outcomes14,15. Meanwhile, existing reporting guidelines such as DECIDE‑AI16, TRIPOD + AI17, and STARD‑AI18 mainly address documentation standards, whereas broader translation frameworks and technology-readiness scales operate at macro-level engineering or regulatory milestones19–21. Neither provides prescriptive guidance on which clinical study designs to use at different maturity stages or how validation should progress in a structured manner. This methodological gap has led to a persistent “evaluation mismatch”22,23: on one hand, many AI systems proceed to costly clinical trials before achieving sufficient stability; on the other hand, some systems remain confined to offline comparisons even when clinical benefit needs to be demonstrated. To address this gap, we propose the concept of a medical AI clinical evaluation gradient framework, which views validation as a continuous, nonlinear lifecycle rather than a one-off performance assessment. The framework encompasses data robustness, engineering stability, human–AI interaction, clinical evidence, and real‑world integration. The model development stage serves as the foundation, establishing initial performance boundaries before formal evaluation begins. Crucially, although the framework depicts model development as its foundational layer, it presupposes an upstream, problem-led clinical-need assessment to ensure that an AI intervention is epidemiologically and operationally justified. Building on this principle, we develop a lifecycle-based evaluation framework consisting of five-phase evaluation framework supported by an overarching dynamic evaluation architecture that explicitly accommodates nonlinear parallelism (Fig. 1). The framework systematically integrates eight core methodological approaches and eighteen study designs (Figs. 1 and 2), enabling researchers to identify the maturity stage of their system and select appropriate study designs accordingly. Although conceptually general, the framework is primarily applicable to diagnostic and prognostic medical AI systems, particularly those entering clinical use through the SaMD pathway. These systems typically adopt a frozen‑model paradigm during evaluation, and their evidence‑generation trajectory aligns with that of traditional diagnostic devices. At the same time, the five-phase framework is intended as a flexible evaluation gradient rather than a rigid sequence; the appropriate pathway depends on intended use, risk class, regulatory designation, and clinical context. The dynamic evaluation architecture further outlines how continually learning models and non‑diagnostic AI applications may selectively implement or adapt components of the framework. The framework also aligns with emerging global governance expectations, including robustness, fairness, transparency, and lifecycle risk management emphasized in the OECD AI Principles24 and the EU AI Act25. Importantly, the framework accommodates medical AI systems with different levels of algorithmic autonomy. Assistive or human‑in‑the‑loop systems require explicit evaluation of human–AI cognitive coupling, which is the central focus of Phase 3. Fully autonomous systems may have limited need for the human–AI interaction components of Phase 3 but require higher evidentiary standards in Phases 1, 2, and 4, as they operate without a clinician safety net. Results › Phase 1. Technical validation: establishing data robustness › Rationale and positioning

Technical validation aims to assess whether a frozen medical AI model can maintain stable performance when applied to fully independent and clinically heterogeneous datasets. Rather than further optimizing model architecture or parameters, this stage assesses generalizability across institutions, data‑generation or acquisition processes, and patient populations. The goal is to establish a data robustness baseline under idealized data flows, thereby creating the prerequisite conditions for subsequent operational robustness and clinical evaluation. Multi‑center retrospective external validation (Method 1) represents the first methodological gateway for medical AI to enter the clinical evaluation pathway26. Its core question focuses on whether a model can remain reliable once decoupled from its development environment and tested on independent, clinically representative data. To ensure methodological transparency, external validation studies in this phase must align with task-specific international standards: studies evaluating the standalone diagnostic accuracy of an AI tool should adhere to the STARD-AI statement18, whereas those validating multivariable individual risk prediction models should strictly follow the TRIPOD + AI guidelines17.

Results › Phase 1. Technical validation: establishing data robustness › Key design elements

Rigorous technical validation requires strict governance of both the study population and the data pipeline. Crucially, prior to any data access or analysis, a formal study protocol and a detailed statistical analysis plan (SAP) must be explicitly prespecified. This phase pre-specification ensures that primary endpoints, subgroup stratifications, and success criteria are not retroactively fitted to the data. Before evaluation, the model version, parameters, and decision thresholds must be locked, and no post‑hoc tuning is permitted27; any modification constitutes a new model and must be re‑evaluated on an entirely new validation set. Dataset selection should follow a diagnostic cohort design, using consecutive enrollment or random sampling to preserve low‑quality images and complex comorbidities rather than selecting “typical cases”, which is essential for detecting and avoiding spectrum bias but remains challenging in practice28,29. The validation set must be fully independent from the development data in temporal (different acquisition periods), geographic (different institutions), or both30,31. Inclusion of multiple centers (typically ≥3) is recommended to capture natural heterogeneity in data sources, acquisition processes, and populations, thereby probing the model’s generalizability limits27,32,33. To ensure that technical validation reflects genuine robustness rather than overfitting to a single-site distribution, Phase 1 must explicitly characterize and address structural sources of heterogeneity across participating centers. This includes documenting and mitigating case-mix differences, acquisition-process variation, prevalence shifts, and any site-level differences in data-generation workflows. Reference standard construction should maintain strict separation between the algorithm team and the annotation team. For retrospective studies, pathology confirmation or expert consensus should be used34, and delayed follow‑up (e.g., 1–3 years) may be necessary to correct verification bias35,36 and decouple the ground truth from initial clinical diagnosis35,37. To combat structural validity problems caused by site heterogeneity, rigorous reference standard harmonization is mandatory to ensure uniform ground-truth definition across diverse sites. Furthermore, if a single global threshold yields suboptimal calibration across varying clinical environments, guidance for local threshold recalibration should be established and documented. If validation results fall short of expectations, in‑place parameter adjustments are prohibited. Instead, systematic failure analysis should be conducted to identify vulnerable scenarios, such as specific devices, pathological severity, or data-quality anomalies, and these findings should be fed back into model development. Any updated version must be treated as a new model and re‑evaluated on a new, independent third‑party dataset to preserve the integrity and independence of the evaluation process. Finally, the evaluation must include formal AI-by-site interaction testing alongside stratified robustness checks across key heterogeneity factors. These measures ensure that technical validation probes the model’s true stability across heterogeneous inputs rather than relying on a single dominant data source. Sample size determination should be based on the target sensitivity or specificity, desired statistical power, and significance level. Additional inflation is often required to account for center‑level clustering and potential data drift, ensuring that statistical inference remains robust across heterogeneous multi‑center settings (Supplementary Material 1)38,39.

Results › Phase 1. Technical validation: establishing data robustness › Evaluation components

In this phase, overall discrimination metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) or the area under the precision-recall curve should be reported alongside sensitivity at a prespecified specificity, as well as the corresponding positive and negative predictive values (PPV/NPV)40. Beyond discrimination, probabilistic models must report calibration metrics (e.g., Brier score, expected calibration error [ECE], and reliability plots) together with validated uncertainty estimates to evaluate prediction reliability, particularly for heterogeneous data, edge cases, and out-of-distribution inputs. Additionally, decision-curve analysis (DCA) should be incorporated to estimate the potential net clinical benefit across relevant risk thresholds41. Key subgroup analyzes, stratified by pathological severity, data‑source heterogeneity, acquisition processes, and demographics, alongside formal AI-by-site interaction testing, are essential to avoid masking failure modes that may be hidden by “average performance”40,42. Subgroup specification must be driven by clinical relevance rather than modality‑specific conventions. The imaging‑related subgroup examples provided here are illustrative rather than prescriptive; actual subgroup definitions should be determined by clinical experts based on disease context, patient heterogeneity, and the intended clinical use.

Results › Phase 1. Technical validation: establishing data robustness › Strengths and limitations

The core value of method lies in using heterogeneous clinical data from independent multiple sites to test the upper bounds of model performance and the limits of robustness across settings43–45, and to identify overfitting, spectrum bias, and inter-institutional variability that single‑center studies cannot reveal43,44. The approach provides the safety boundary required for follow-up research. However, because it relies on historical and often “cleaned” data, 28, 29 it cannot assess human–AI interaction effects, may not reflect contemporary clinical practice, and is susceptible to variability in data quality, annotation practices, and selection bias across centers40,42. Consequently, it should serve as the starting point rather than the endpoint of the evidence chain, establishing the model’s data robustness baseline under idealized data flows, rather than demonstrating clinical effectiveness.

Results › Phase 2. Operational robustness validation: engineering stability and stream stress testing › Rationale and positioning

The operational robustness validation phase evaluates the engineering stability and data‑stream adaptability of a medical AI system within real hospital workflows. Unlike technical validation, which focuses on data robustness in static datasets, this phase examines how the model performs when exposed to raw clinical data streams. The core question is whether the system can process reliably and stably on real‑world data within a clinical environment, before influencing clinical decision‑making. Shadow-mode or silent trial (Method 2) represents the core and most practical study design for this phase. The model runs in real time in the background without displaying outputs to clinicians, allowing comprehensive assessment of its behavior under authentic data flow conditions, including data preprocessing, inference latency, failure rates, and engineering bottlenecks46–48. The purpose of this phase is to establish an engineering safety boundary before human–AI interaction studies or clinical trials, ensuring that the system can “operate, operate stably, and operate end‑to‑end” in real clinical environments.

Results › Phase 2. Operational robustness validation: engineering stability and stream stress testing › Key design elements

Crucially, prior to initiating shadow-mode deployment, a formal monitoring protocol or a versioned analysis charter must be explicitly prespecified. The model must be deployed within clinical data pathways to ensure full alignment of data formats, acquisition processes, and metadata structures with routine clinical workflows46. Shadow‑mode deployment should encompass all clinical cases, rather than only “readable” or “typical” cases, to expose potential failure modes arising from low‑quality images, atypical protocols, or rare findings. At the engineering level, key indicators of the data pipeline, including data ingestion, preprocessing, inference latency, resource utilization, and batch‑processing stability, should be systematically monitored, with all abnormal events logged together with their triggers and frequencies46–48. In settings involving multiple data sources, acquisition processes, or site-level workflows, the model’s stability and agreement under varying input distributions must be evaluated to identify risks of data drift and model drift26,46. A comprehensive logging and error‑tracking system is required to ensure that every failure can be located, reproduced, and explained46. Organizationally, the algorithm team and clinical team should remain separated, and clinicians must not be exposed to model outputs to avoid automation bias49. Issues identified during this phase should be addressed through engineering fixes or data governance rather than parameter tuning or retraining; if model updates are necessary, they should be treated as a new version and re‑evaluated through a new shadow‑mode cycle. Sample size estimation for shadow-mode testing prioritizes the confirmation of engineering robustness over conventional hypothesis testing. Consequently, the scale is determined by a synthesis of agreement, stability, and safety (Supplementary Material 2). First, Cohen’s kappa or intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC)50,51 can be used to estimate the number of cases required to ensure that AI-clinician agreement meets predefined safety thresholds. Second, confidence interval width method or single‑proportion formulas52–54 are used to estimate system failure rates to ensure engineering robustness attains the targeted precision. Third, the “rule of three”55–57 or binomial distribution58,59 can be applied to estimate the upper bound of rare critical errors or to determine the sample size needed to capture at least one such event. Given the dynamic nature of real‑world data streams and potential data loss46,60, a 10–20% inflation over the theoretical estimate is typically recommended to ensure adequate retention, rather than to compensate for underlying structural site heterogeneity.

Results › Phase 2. Operational robustness validation: engineering stability and stream stress testing › Evaluation components

The focus of this phase has expanded from single-dimension diagnostic performance to a multidimensional evaluation framework encompassing model performance, system robustness, diagnostic agreement, and data-stream adaptability46,47,61–63. In addition to Phase 1 metrics (e.g., AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and calibration), Phase 2 emphasizes real-time operational capability within hospital environments. This includes engineering stability indicators such as failure rate, inference latency, system throughput, and service availability, alongside resource-load characteristics such as compute cost, energy consumption, network bandwidth, and performance under concurrent load (e.g., latency at scale, batch throughput, and compute-resource ceilings). These factors jointly determine whether the system can be deployed sustainably across departments and devices in real-time, high-volume clinical workflows while accounting for long-term infrastructure and environmental impact. Diagnostic agreement between AI outputs and the reference standard should be quantified using measures such as Cohen’s kappa or ICC. In addition, Phase 2 requires explicit monitoring of calibration and predictive uncertainty under real-world data streams. Calibration drift should be assessed using reliability plots, Brier scores, and ECE41,64, while uncertainty estimates should be evaluated for their ability to characterize prediction reliability and identify potential out-of-distribution inputs without influencing clinical decisions. Furthermore, data drift, device heterogeneity, and potential biases should be evaluated through drift-detection metrics, bias audits, and fairness assessments across predefined clinical subgroups. However, these assessments represent baseline characterization rather than longitudinal monitoring; the objective is to establish the system’s initial robustness profile when first exposed to real-world data streams without influencing clinical decisions. Continuous, time-varying drift and fairness monitoring is reserved for Phase 5, where post-market surveillance governs long-term performance stability.

Results › Phase 2. Operational robustness validation: engineering stability and stream stress testing › Strengths and limitations

The primary value of the phase lies in prospectively and non‑interventionally validating the engineering stability and data‑stream adaptability of a medical AI system under clinical workflows46–48. By receiving raw clinical inputs in real time, the model avoids the selection bias inherent to retrospectively curated datasets and more accurately reflects performance under authentic input distributions. This phase also enables high‑fidelity engineering validation by fully reproducing hospital data pathways, including clinical data-transmission pipelines, network latency, protocol variability, and metadata heterogeneity, thereby revealing engineering vulnerabilities. Because model outputs do not influence clinical decisions, the ethical risk is low26,49, and the frozen outputs can be compared with clinician diagnoses or reference standards at study completion to generate high‑quality performance evidence49. However, this phase does not involve clinician behavior48 and therefore cannot assess human–AI interaction effects, automation bias, or workflow changes, nor can it directly reflect impacts on diagnostic efficiency or patient outcomes. Furthermore, implementation is resource-intensive, demanding robust hospital information technology infrastructure, data governance, and privacy protection. As an engineering assessment, this phase should be regarded as an engineering threshold within the evidence chain, establishing system stability under real data flows rather than demonstrating clinical effectiveness46–48.

Results › Phase 3. Controlled interaction validation: human–AI interaction and cognitive assessment

The controlled interaction phase represents the critical transition in medical AI from non‑interventional validation to a clinical decision-support application. Unlike earlier stages that emphasize standalone model performance, this phase focuses on systematically evaluating human–AI interaction effects in controlled, simulated clinical environments, with particular attention to changes in clinicians’ diagnostic direction, confidence, and decision efficiency following exposure to AI outputs26,48. To avoid the ethical and safety risks of testing human–AI interaction directly in real clinical settings, clinicians are brought into a controlled experimental environment where fatigue, external distractions, and workflow pressures are minimized. This allows precise measurement of the AI’s net cognitive benefit and clarification of its role in the decision‑making chain, whether it functions as a “parallel peer” or as a “cognitive enhancer” providing additional information. Depending on the study objective and depth of assessment, Phase 3 comprises three complementary methodological pathways that together form the core framework for evaluating cognitive contribution and human–AI collaboration. Crucially, across all three pathways, a formal study protocol and SAP must be explicitly prespecified prior to any experimental testing. To standardize the evidence-generation process for clinical decision support and human factors, studies in this phase should be guided primarily by the DECIDE-AI guidelines16. Additionally, if the primary objective includes quantifying the final collaborative diagnostic accuracy of the unified human–AI team, the reporting framework should be complementarily structured around the STARD-AI standard18.

Results › Method 3. Standalone benchmarking › Rationale and positioning

Standalone benchmarking aims to determine the “performance position” of a medical AI system by conducting blinded, parallel comparisons between the model and clinicians of different seniority levels (junior, mid-career, or senior) on the same standardized case set65,66. This method addresses a core scientific question: To which level of clinical expertise is the AI’s independent diagnostic ability equivalent? This positioning is essential for evaluating the feasibility of using AI as a second reader, a task‑shifting tool in resource‑limited settings, or an expert‑support tool65,67. By enabling a pure comparison of cognitive diagnostic performance, it provides the baseline for subsequent human–AI collaboration studies and serves as the foundational prerequisite for interaction evaluation.

Results › Method 3. Standalone benchmarking › Key design elements

The design of standalone benchmarking centers on constructing a high‑quality, reproducible, and clinically representative standardized dataset, and ensuring a blinded, parallel evaluation between AI and clinicians. The datasets should capture natural heterogeneity in disease spectrum, pathological severity, input‑quality variation, data‑source heterogeneity, and patient characteristics68,69, with reference standards established by an independent expert panel or pathology rather than initial clinical reads28,29. Clinicians of varying seniority should be recruited to create a graded reference framework that pinpoints the AI within the clinical decision pathway28,29. The evaluation adheres to the principles of blinding, randomization, and parallel assessment: blinding prevents information contamination; randomization mitigates order and learning effects; and parallel evaluation ensures that AI and clinicians diagnose identical information independently, yielding highly comparable data. Sample size is primarily driven by the number of cases and is typically determined through independent (non‑paired) comparisons of AUC70,71 or differences in sensitivity and specificity72,73 between AI and clinicians. Although there is no strict minimum number of participating readers, it is generally recommended to include at least three clinicians per seniority tier to mitigate individual variability and ensure robustness of the findings29,74 (Supplementary Material 3).

Results › Method 3. Standalone benchmarking › Evaluation components

The evaluation framework must capture both diagnostic performance and inter‑reader variability. Core metrics include AUROC, sensitivity, specificity, and PPV/NPV at clinically relevant thresholds28,29. For classification tasks, agreement between AI and expert readers can be quantified using Cohen’s kappa or weighted kappa63,75; for continuous scoring tasks, ICC or Spearman correlation may be used. For probabilistic prediction or triage-oriented systems, additional assessment of calibration and uncertainty estimation performance may be informative, using approaches such as reliability plots, Brier score, ECE, and appropriate empirical validation of uncertainty estimates.

Results › Method 3. Standalone benchmarking › Strengths and limitations

The strength of standalone benchmarking lies in its ability to generate a pure comparison of cognitive diagnostic performance between AI and clinicians of different seniority, thereby defining the AI’s performance position and providing direct evidence for its potential role within the clinical pathway29,65. This approach can reveal the model’s strengths and limitations across specific lesion types, image qualities, and case difficulties, and establishes the baseline for subsequent human–AI collaboration studies. However, its limitations stem from the inability to assess automation bias, workflow effects, or the influence of AI on clinicians’ behavior. In addition, the generalizability of findings depends heavily on the quality of the datasets, and inappropriate case selection may introduce spectrum bias or lead to over‑ or underestimation of the model’s true capability69. Therefore, it should be regarded as a cognitive‑level positioning tool that characterizes the AI’s independent diagnostic ability, rather than a basis for inferring its ultimate clinical impact. Clinician-seniority benchmarking is most appropriate for interpretive specialties where diagnostic performance exhibits clear seniority gradients. For generalist or decision-support tasks, and for generative or agentic AI systems, alternative benchmarking strategies are required, as seniority-based comparisons may not meaningfully reflect task-specific competence. It marks the starting point of Phase 3, not its endpoint.

Results › Method 4. Exploratory paired design › Rationale and positioning

The exploratory paired design aims to evaluate the direct impact of AI on clinicians’ diagnostic behavior in a controlled environment, focusing on how the same clinician’s diagnostic direction, confidence, or efficiency changes with versus without AI assistance. Using a self‑controlled framework, each clinician interprets the same case set under both “AI‑assisted” and “solo” conditions, enabling the detection of directional effects on cognitive decision‑making with minimal sample size76,77. To accommodate different study objectives and memory‑effect constraints, several structured variants of the paired design have been developed, including case‑by‑case sequential, sequential sessions, and crossover designs76, each with distinct features summarized in Box 1.

Results › Method 4. Exploratory paired design › Key design elements

The exploratory paired design relies on a rigorous self‑controlled structure and minimizes memory and order effects76,77. A representative dataset must first be assembled, with reference standards established by an independent expert panel or pathology. To mitigate memory and order effects, a washout period is typically implemented, supplemented by the random reshuffling of cases in the second session76,77. Besides, the reading interface should remain identical across sessions, with AI outputs (e.g., probability scores, heatmaps, or classification labels) introduced only in the second session. For each reading, clinicians’ final decisions, confidence ratings, reading time, and degree of reliance on AI should be recorded. To rigorously isolate true automation bias from confounding interaction variables, these studies must employ strict experimental controls. While the inclusion of trap cases (intentionally incorrect AI predictions) is helpful for probing over-reliance78, it is not sufficient on its own. Protocols must explicitly standardize or systematically vary interface elements, such as alert saliency, heatmap quality, and the presentation of predictive confidence, to ensure that UI-driven coercion is not conflated with genuine cognitive bias79. Furthermore, in addition to random reshuffling to mitigate case-order and fatigue effects, studies should mandate a dedicated familiarization phase (a warm-up set of cases) prior to actual testing80. This effectively separates the clinician’s initial learning curve from their steady-state cognitive coupling with the AI. The sample sizes for the three paired designs are estimated based on the specific metric types, such as mean differences, paired proportions, or the covariance structure of correlated AUCs. Accordingly, the main distinction among the designs lies not in the formula itself but in the estimation of the correlation coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\rho}$$\end{document}ρ)81: crossover designs, assisted with washout periods, better control memory effects and therefore yield correlation estimates closer to real‑world diagnostic agreement. In contrast, case‑by‑case sequential designs typically exhibit the highest correlation due to memory effects, resulting in the smallest theoretical sample size but also the greatest risk of bias76,77,82. Although statistical power is driven largely by case volume, at least three clinicians of differing seniority should be included as repeated‑measurement sources to account for reader variability (Supplementary Material 4).

Results › Method 4. Exploratory paired design › Evaluation components

The evaluation framework centers on changes in diagnostic performance (Δ performance), reading efficiency (Δ reading time)67,83, and may incorporate exploratory clinical utility via DCA when probability-based outputs are available41. It also evaluates how clinicians interpret and respond to AI uncertainty displays (e.g., confidence intervals or warning prompts), including error-correction rates and the proportions of false-to-true or true-to-false revisions, to assess their effects on reliance behavior, automation bias, diagnostic confidence, and potential risks of over- or mis-diagnosis84,85. In addition, changes in diagnostic confidence (Δ confidence), human–AI confidence calibration, and intra-reader agreement provide deeper insight into clinicians’ cognitive and decision stability during AI-assisted interpretation67,83.

Results › Method 4. Exploratory paired design › Strengths and limitations

The design offers high sensitivity and efficiency76,77, enabling the detection of subtle AI‑induced cognitive effects, including error correction, changes in confidence, shifts in reading time, and automation bias, through its self‑controlled structure77. Conducted in a controlled, risk‑free environment, it provides a low‑cost signal‑detection step, generating directionally informative evidence for interface optimization and subsequent design of higher‑level validation studies. Its limitations arise from the fact that findings primarily reflect short‑term cognitive changes under controlled conditions rather than effects on real clinical workflows or patient outcomes, limiting generalizability. And it cannot eliminate memory effects, nor can it assess the engineering stability of AI under authentic data streams.

MRMC studies are among the most confirmatory designs for evaluating interpretive diagnostic AI systems. They aim to assess, under controlled conditions, the impact of AI assistance on clinical diagnostic performance while quantifying the dual sources of variability arising from readers and cases. Unlike exploratory paired designs, MRMC provides statistically powered confirmatory evidence to address two core questions: (1) Does AI significantly improve clinicians’ diagnostic performance? and (2) Is this improvement consistent across different readers and cases? MRMC has been explicitly endorsed by the FDA and EMA for evaluating AI‑assisted diagnostic devices and serves as a key evidentiary framework for demonstrating human–AI performance gains in regulatory submissions for high‑risk medical software86,87.

Results › Method 5. Multi‑reader multi‑case (MRMC) › Relation to crossover designs

Although MRMC studies often employ crossover procedures, the two are conceptually distinct and operate at different inferential levels. The purpose of the crossover design is to control learning and order effects, ensuring comparability of a clinician’s performance across reading modes. Its inference is confined to the “reader-case” self‑controlled unit, addressing whether AI changes an individual clinician’s diagnosis of a specific case76,92. In contrast, MRMC focuses on population‑level inference. By incorporating multiple readers and a large case set, it constructs a three‑dimensional “reader × case × mode” data structure that enables estimation of the average AI effect across reader and case populations, as well as the stability of that effect88. Although both approaches involve multi‑session reading, randomized sequence, and washout periods, MRMC operates at a higher inferential tier and provides stronger external validity. Detailed comparison is shown in Supplementary Table 1. In high‑quality study practice, crossover designs and MRMC are complementary. Crossover procedures provide a bias‑controlled operational framework, whereas fully crossed MRMC supplies the data structure required for confirmatory inference. The former addresses “how to arrange reading sequences”, while the latter answers “whether the AI effect generalizes across readers and cases”. Accordingly, MRMC is typically implemented using paired designs with sequence randomization to ensure identifiability of mode effects and to avoid temporal confounding (Fig. 3B, C, H, I)89. If a fixed reading order is used (Fig. 3A, D–G), mode effects may become confounded with time effects, preventing accurate attribution of the AI’s true contribution.

Results › Method 5. Multi‑reader multi‑case (MRMC) › Key design elements

The design elements of MRMC studies largely mirror those of paired designs: a representative dataset, an independently established reference standard, and readers with varying levels of seniority89. Reading procedures must adhere strictly to blinding and randomization principles, with adequate washout periods between modes and reshuffling89. The reading interface should remain identical across modes, with AI outputs added only in the assisted condition. When appropriate, balanced or Latin‑square arrangements may be used to further mitigate learning effects. MRMC analysis relies on specialized dual‑random‑effects frameworks rather than conventional difference testing. The Obuchowski-Rockette (OR) and Dorfman–Berbaum–Metz (DBM) models explicitly account for the covariance structure by decomposing reader effects, case effects, and reader‑by‑case interactions, thereby avoiding underestimation of standard errors and yielding robust effect‑size estimates88. This modeling framework is a defining methodological feature that distinguishes MRMC from external evaluation, shadow‑mode, and paired designs, and is a key reason for its broad acceptance by regulatory agencies. Current mainstream MRMC analysis tools include the OR‑DBM MRMC software93–95 suite and the R package MRMCaov96. In parallel, iMRMC, developed and maintained by the US FDA, has become a standard tool in this field87. iMRMC offers greater flexibility for handling unbalanced designs, where not all readers evaluate all cases, and can automatically accommodate missing data. For studies intended to support FDA submissions, for complex sample‑size simulations, or for datasets with substantial missingness, iMRMC is generally the preferred option. Sample‑size determination in MRMC requires simultaneous modeling of reader‑ and case‑level random effects and their cross‑correlation structure. As a result, traditional analytic formulas for diagnostic studies are not applicable. Instead, the required numbers of cases and readers must be derived from effect‑size assumptions and variance components under the OR/DBM or U‑statistic frameworks. When pilot data are available, the OR‑DBM MRMC software can compute sample size using empirically estimated variance components91,96–98. In the absence of pilot data, MRMC sample size88,99 or iMRMC87 can be used to obtain approximate case‑reader combinations through assumed correlation structures or simulation (Supplementary Material 5).

Results › Method 5. Multi‑reader multi‑case (MRMC) › Evaluation components

The MRMC evaluation framework typically spans five components: primary effect metrics (e.g., ΔAUC), variance decomposition for reader and case variability, statistical inference using DBM/OR F‑tests, reader‑ and case‑level analyzes, and assessments of agreement and bias87,100,101. In applied medical studies, the most commonly reported metrics include the reader‑averaged AUC, the AUC difference (ΔAUC), sensitivity and specificity, and significance testing based on DBM/OR models28,29,102. Many studies additionally report stratified reader analyzes to examine whether AI provides differential benefit across experience levels29.

Results › Method 5. Multi‑reader multi‑case (MRMC) › Strengths and limitations

MRMC offers strong confirmatory value, statistical rigor, and generalizability94,103. It enables simultaneous estimation of the average AI’s performance gain, inter‑reader variability, and case‑difficulty variability, providing more robust evidence than single‑reader or paired designs88,89,104. In addition, MRMC also supports stratified analyzes to characterize differential AI benefit across reader experience levels or case‑complexity strata. However, MRMC studies are resource‑intensive, requiring substantial reader recruitment, and imposing a considerable reading burden, and they cannot fully replicate real clinical workflows. Accordingly, MRMC should be viewed as a key confirmatory step bridging cognitive validation and clinical validation, providing high‑quality performance evidence and behavioral baselines to inform subsequent prospective clinical trials.

Results › Rationale and positioning

The clinical evidence phase marks a fundamental shift in evaluation logic, from assessing “model performance” to determining “interventional benefit”. Its core mission is to evaluate whether a medical‑AI system, once embedded into real clinical workflows, can optimize clinicians’ decision pathways and ultimately translate into measurable clinical gains, such as improved diagnostic efficiency, reduced unnecessary interventions, or better long‑term patient outcomes105–107. This phase represents the critical juncture at which medical AI must overcome the “performance wall” and generate evidence suitable for clinical adoption; the resulting data directly influence regulatory approval and guideline endorsement108,109. This phase typically employs a multidimensional framework based on prospective randomized controlled trials (Method 6), which use controlled comparisons to eliminate confounding and ensure that any observed benefit can be causally attributed to AI110,111. Depending on the validation objective, three major trial designs are commonly used110 (Box 2): diagnostic randomized controlled trials (RCT), clinical‑effectiveness RCT, and stepped‑wedge cluster randomized trials (SW‑CRT), a specific form of cluster randomized controlled trial. Together, these designs form the core methodological system of the clinical evidence phase, addressing AI’s value from complementary perspectives of diagnostic behavior, patient‑level benefit, and system‑level deployment. A detailed comparison is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Furthermore, to ensure absolute accountability and high-fidelity evidence generation, prospective interventional trials in Phase 4 must strictly utilize the SPIRIT-AI extension112 during the clinical trial protocol design stage and adhere to the CONSORT-AI extension113 when reporting final trial outcomes.

Results › Rationale and positioning › Key design elements

Crucially, prior to trial initiation, a formal study protocol and SAP must be explicitly prespecified. In this phase, prospective RCTs provide the most compelling evidence for establishing a causal association between AI intervention and clinical benefit108,114. These trials adhere to the principles of “randomization, control, intervention, and prospective”, thereby minimizing confounding and ensuring baseline comparability between study arms110. At the design stage, investigators must specify the AI system’s functional role within the clinical pathway, such as primary screening, second‑opinion support, prioritization, or workflow triage67,115, and select an appropriate trial design accordingly (Box 2). Regardless of the specific design, several requirements are essential for this phase: the AI interface, thresholds, and alerting logic must remain fixed throughout the trial; participants should be enrolled consecutively or through natural exposure to avoid selective inclusion of atypical cases; AI outputs, clinician decision pathways, reading time, coverage, and failure rates should be systematically recorded; and safety‑monitoring mechanisms (e.g., predefined stopping rules) should be established for high‑risk scenarios. In multicenter studies, differences in workflow, equipment, and staffing must be documented and controlled to enhance generalizability. While each of the three designs has its own specific evaluation metrics (as detailed in Box 2), Phase 4 trials evaluating probabilistic risk scores or clinical decision-support systems should explicitly incorporate DCA to quantify clinical net benefit41, alongside rigorous assessments of interventional calibration and uncertainty to ensure safe human–AI decision-making. Furthermore, to align with the real-world mandates of health technology assessment bodies, these trial designs can be methodologically extended to incorporate health-economic endpoints116,117. By concurrently collecting cost and health-utility data (e.g., QALYs) alongside primary clinical efficacy outcomes, this phase provides a rigorous foundation for trial-based cost-effectiveness analysis, generating the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios necessary for reimbursement. To address structural sources of site heterogeneity, this phase must incorporate design and analytic strategies that go beyond simple sample-size inflation. First, AI-by-site interaction testing is required to determine whether the interventional effect of AI differs systematically across centers with distinct case-mix profiles, acquisition‑process characteristics, or clinical workflows. Second, for probability-output models, guidance for local threshold recalibration should be prespecified to account for center-level prevalence shifts and case-mix variation118,119, ensuring that decision thresholds remain clinically appropriate across heterogeneous settings. Third, statistical analysis strategies can rigorously distinguish the direct effect of the AI from site-specific practice effects by employing hierarchical or mixed-effects models, site-stratified analyses, or cluster-robust inference120,121. These measures align with CONSORT-Cluster122 and TRIPOD-Cluster119 guidelines, ensuring that this phase evaluates genuine causal clinical benefit rather than artifacts driven by center-specific practice patterns.

Results › Rationale and positioning › Sample size calculation

Sample‑size determination in RCTs depends primarily on the hypothesis‑testing framework123,124. In clinical evidence studies of medical AI, superiority and non‑inferiority designs are most commonly used, whereas equivalence designs are rarely adopted28,105,125,126. This pattern reflects the functional role of AI systems, their intended clinical value, and regulatory expectations. Superiority trials assess whether AI can meaningfully outperform standard care on key metrics such as sensitivity, missed‑diagnosis rate, or workflow efficiency, making them suitable for systems aimed at performance enhancement124. Non‑inferiority trials emphasize safety and substitutability, evaluating whether AI can deliver advantages in speed, cost, or accessibility without materially compromising accuracy127,128, an approach particularly relevant to triage, screening, and resource‑limited settings. Equivalence designs are uncommon in medical AI because they require two‑sided margins and substantially larger sample sizes than superiority or non‑inferiority trials72,124,125. Moreover, AI systems are typically intended to improve, or at least not fall below, clinician performance rather than achieve strict equivalence within a predefined interval; behavioral differences between clinicians and AI further challenge the plausibility of equivalence assumptions. Regulatory agencies also focus on demonstrating effectiveness and safety rather than statistical equivalence to human diagnosis129. Consequently, superiority and non‑inferiority frameworks constitute the primary inferential basis for medical‑AI clinical trials and underpin subsequent decisions on sample‑size estimation, effect‑size specification, and interpretation of results. The three trial types require distinct sample‑size calculation frameworks, respectively, see Supplementary Material 6 for details.

Results › Rationale and positioning › Strengths and limitations

The phase offers the advantage of evaluating the real‑world effectiveness of AI within real clinical workflows, providing evidence that most closely reflects routine practice26,108,110. However, these trials are resource‑intensive, lengthy, and susceptible to variability in workflow, clinician behavior, learning effects, and inter‑center heterogeneity130,131. Blinding is also difficult to maintain in real clinical environments, introducing potential behavioral bias. Despite these challenges, this phase remains essential for moving from performance validation to clinical value validation and provides the definitive evidence required to support clinical deployment of AI systems.

Results › Phase 5. Real‑world integration validation: post‑market performance and system‑level impact › Rationale and positioning

The real-world integration phase focuses on the long‑term performance of medical‑AI systems within real healthcare ecosystems. Its core question shifts from isolated “interventional efficacy” to the robustness, fairness, and organizational assimilation of AI across institutions, populations, and periods. By examining the dynamic interactions between AI and hospital structures, clinical workflows, clinician skill evolution, and regulatory systems, this phase aims to characterize the long‑term effects and systemic adaptability of AI within complex health‑care ecosystems. Phase 5 focuses on real-world clinical deployment, system-level integration, and post-market performance governance. Unlike Phase 2, which functions as a non-interventional silent trial to evaluate engineering stability under real-world data streams without influencing clinical decisions, Phase 5 assesses the AI system’s sustained clinical impact, equity, safety, adoption, and long-term performance once it is actively integrated into live, routine care. Compared with phase 4, the core advantage of the phase lies in its ability to evaluate the system‑level resilience of AI under real‑world noise and operational complexity132. To safeguard internal validity, controlled studies such as MRMC and RCTs often exclude “noisy data”, including poor-quality images, missing information, or workflow anomalies28,29. However, such data are ubiquitous in routine clinical practice. Therefore, by incorporating real‑world data noise, workflow perturbations, and inter‑institutional heterogeneity, this phase assesses both the technical robustness of AI under non‑ideal conditions and its real-world adaptability through implementation‑science metrics, such as adoption, fidelity, and fairness, thereby addressing the limitations in external validity inherent to clinical trials. Importantly, the monitoring function of the real-world integration phase does not supersede RCTs, MRMC studies, or other controlled validation designs133,134. The objective of this phase is intended to extend rather than replace clinical-trial evidence: its purpose is to ensure that AI remains effective in routine practice, not to establish its initial “efficacy”. Accordingly, the real-world integration and clinical evidence phases maintain a complementary and synergistic relationship within the medical AI evaluation hierarchy, jointly supporting safety and effectiveness across the full lifecycle of AI systems. The core methodological approach in this phase is prospective real‑world implementation and integrated evaluation (Method 7), which encompasses four complementary study types: single‑arm prospective pre-post study, real‑world effectiveness study (RWS), implementation research (IR), and post‑market active surveillance (See Box 3 for details). Together, these approaches form the post‑market evidence chain for medical AI and are essential for ensuring that AI systems are “usable, useful, and sustainably effective” in real‑world settings.

Crucially, prior to real-world implementation, a formal monitoring protocol or a versioned analysis charter must be explicitly prespecified. These approaches typically use observational or mixed‑methods designs and follow a pragmatic‑research paradigm: they prospectively track consecutively enrolled real‑world populations without altering clinical decision‑making, thereby assessing the actual utility and safety of AI in routine practice. Appropriate methodological choices should be made according to study objectives (See Box 3 for details). Regardless of the specific approach, the phase must ensure that AI versions, thresholds, and interfaces remain stable and traceable throughout the study; that failure modes and anomalous events arising in real workflows are systematically documented; that performance consistency is monitored across institutions, devices, and patient groups; and that comprehensive records of data governance, privacy protection, and model‑update processes are maintained. Each of the four designs has its own evaluation metrics (Box 3). Furthermore, to facilitate institutional scaling and long-term sustainability, these real-world deployment designs can be methodologically augmented with health-economic decision analytics. This includes budget impact analysis to project the net financial footprint of AI adoption on local healthcare budgets over a multi-year horizon135, and value-of-information analysis136 to mathematically assess whether the reduction of evidence uncertainty justifies the economic costs of delaying broader clinical integration. Additionally, to support the global mandate for green healthcare, longitudinal monitoring in Phase 5 must track the cumulative compute costs and the long-term operational carbon footprint associated with continuous algorithmic maintenance and periodic retraining137.

Results › Phase 5. Real‑world integration validation: post‑market performance and system‑level impact › Sample size calculation

In terms of sample size, this phase moves away from traditional hypothesis‑testing frameworks and instead emphasizes precision‑driven and risk‑coverage principles. First, real‑world studies typically do not specify a strict H0 but aim for adequate “coverage”, ensuring that estimates achieve a prespecified level of precision. Second, when monitoring rare adverse events (e.g., severe misdiagnosis), required sample sizes are derived using the “rule of three” or binomial‑based back‑calculation55–57. Finally, for common performance indicators such as misdiagnosis, confidence‑interval width methods are commonly applied52–54.

Results › Phase 5. Real‑world integration validation: post‑market performance and system‑level impact › Strengths and limitations

The core value of the phase lies in its high real‑world representativeness. Compared with tightly controlled RCTs, studies in this phase typically involve larger sample sizes and broader population representativeness, enabling the detection of rare risks that may be missed due to limited sample size or follow‑up duration in trials, and providing evidence with strong external validity138,139. Its major limitation is the absence of randomization, which increases susceptibility to confounding and weakens causal inference relative to RCTs133. In addition, real-world data vary in quality and often contain missing information and multiple sources of bias138,140. Pre-post designs are vulnerable to time‑trend bias and concurrent policy changes; RWS may not fully correct for selection bias arising from non‑random exposure; IR relies on qualitative interpretations; and active surveillance requires robust data infrastructure and sustained resource investment. Moreover, the effects of AI may fluctuate across time, institutions, and patient groups in complex real‑world environments.

Results › Core driver. Dynamic evaluation architecture: a meta‑methodology for evolving AI

Rationale and positioning. The defining distinction between medical AI and traditional medical devices lies in their rapid iterability. Medical AI functions as an “evolving system”, with performance that fluctuates in response to shifts in data distribution, device characteristics, and clinical workflows, and with model versions updated far more frequently than the timelines of conventional clinical trials141. The traditional linear paradigm of “development-validation-deployment” is therefore insufficient, creating structural challenges of validation lag and evidence obsolescence142,143. This necessitates a meta‑methodological framework capable of spanning the entire lifecycle and adapting dynamically as models evolve. The dynamic evaluation architecture provides such a lifecycle‑wide meta‑logic and is theoretically applicable across all stages of medical‑AI evaluation, particularly during the phases 4 and 5. However, current literature and regulatory practice indicate that adaptive designs remain largely conceptual and methodological, with limited mature application in medical AI‑assisted systems141. Implementing adaptive designs in early phases may compromise reproducibility and stability of performance assessment, whereas their use in clinical trials faces ethical, regulatory, and infrastructural constraints144. The US FDA’s Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs) provide a practical regulatory pathway to address this challenge145. PCCPs require manufacturers to pre‑specify the scope of allowable modifications, the associated validation procedures, and benefit-risk mitigation strategies at the time of initial submission, with prospective authorization granted upon approval. This mechanism enables rapid algorithmic iteration within a regulator‑approved boundary, without the need for repeated marketing submissions. As the most explicit regulatory mechanism to date, PCCPs transition dynamic evaluation from theoretical frameworks into institutionalized practice, establishing a robust cornerstone for the continuous learning and iterative optimization of medical AI. Crucially, this architecture resolves the paradox between the strict frozen-model requirement of early phases and the reality of continually learning models. Once a safety baseline is established (Phases 1–3), models that continuously learn from real-world data streams can utilize mechanisms like PCCPs to dynamically update within pre-approved boundaries, circumventing the need to restart the entire evaluation pipeline from Phase 1. Adaptive design (Method 8) is a key methodological tool enabling medical‑AI diagnostic systems to achieve continuous evaluation, updating, and safety. Among the various adaptive approaches, three designs currently offer the greatest feasibility and methodological value: adaptive sample size re‑estimation (SSR), adaptive platform trials (APT), and versioned evaluation (VE). Currently, sample size planning for adaptive designs in medical AI has shifted from traditional fixed‑hypothesis testing to rule‑based adaptive strategies. Rather than a static figure, the sample size is driven by the “informational threshold” required for robust inference (e.g., Bayesian posterior probability) and the “monitoring breadth” necessary to capture heterogeneous risks146. Because interim adaptations are permitted, sample‑size determination typically relies on statistical simulation rather than a single closed‑form formula146–148. Consequently, Regulatory review focuses on the scientific validity and transparency of the adaptive mechanisms, including pre‑specified triggering conditions, rigorous control of type I error, and operational integrity to ensure unbiased implementation146,148. Furthermore, this dynamic meta-logic allows for the selective implementation of the framework based on the clinical task. For example, non-diagnostic AI applications, such as operational algorithms for bed allocation or administrative triage, typically do not require the rigorous cognitive human–AI interaction assessments of Phase 3. Instead, they can selectively abbreviate the pathway, leveraging APT within Phase 5 to continuously evaluate system-level efficiency and workflow impact.

The evaluation metrics for adaptive designs are fundamentally determined by the validation phase in which they are applied (e.g., RCTs or real‑world integration phase), with additional emphasis on temporal stability and model evolution27,145. Key incremental metrics include the degradation rate, quantifying the decline in AI performance over time; calibration drift, capturing the divergence between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes; and version‑transition shock, assessing the transient disruption to clinical workflows or diagnostic outputs following algorithm updates. This synthesis of phase-specific and evolutionary metrics ensures precise oversight of AI performance dynamics throughout its lifecycle.

The strength of the phase lies in its ability to accommodate the evolving nature of medical AI systems, shifting evaluation from static validation to dynamic monitoring141. Through cross‑phase integration, cross‑version tracking, and cross‑context adaptation, it can detect risks and performance changes arising from algorithm updates, data shifts, or deployment‑environment variation, thereby ensuring sustained effectiveness, safety, and fairness in real‑world settings149. The framework also offers regulators an operational pathway for lifecycle management, enhancing transparency and control over AI approval, updating, and post‑market surveillance27. However, its implementation depends on high‑quality data infrastructure, continuous monitoring resources, and cross‑departmental coordination, which may increase organizational and operational burden151. The evaluation process must balance sensitivity with feasibility to avoid both over‑ and under‑monitoring, and further work is needed to establish its scalability and cross‑institutional applicability across diverse healthcare systems.

The framework also distinguishes between local and systemic fall-back triggers. Local failures, such as transient throughput collapse, isolated subgroup instability, or interface-induced variability, require re-evaluation within the adjacent phase. In contrast, systemic failures (e.g., safety-signal emergence, severe drift, or fairness degradation) necessitate returning to earlier phases for renewed data auditing, recalibration, uncertainty re-estimation, or repeat MRMC or interaction-level evaluation. This dynamic re-entry mechanism ensures that continuously evolving AI systems remain safe, reliable, and clinically effective throughout their lifecycle. Importantly, although Table 3 provides illustrative gating considerations, these are intended to guide rather than prescribe phase progression. Because this framework is designed to accommodate diverse AI applications, it deliberately does not specify universal numerical thresholds. Instead, phase-gating criteria should be prospectively defined according to the intended clinical role of the AI system, its associated patient safety risk, and the objectives of each evaluation phase152. In practice, progression decisions should integrate multiple complementary domains, including: (1) the intended clinical role (e.g., screening, triage, diagnosis, prognosis, or workflow support); (2) the potential consequences of incorrect predictions and the corresponding clinical risk level; (3) predefined minimum acceptable performance criteria established during study design, including prespecified superiority or non-inferiority hypotheses where appropriate18,113; (4) robustness across clinically relevant patient subgroups, healthcare sites, and acquisition conditions; (5) engineering performance requirements, such as latency, reliability, system availability, and interoperability109; and (6) applicable regulatory guidance, specialty-specific recommendations, or expert consensus. Accordingly, the gating considerations presented in Table 3 should be interpreted as illustrative operational guidance rather than mandatory thresholds, and quantitative decision criteria should be prespecified whenever possible and justified according to the specific clinical context. We specifically highlight two critical dimensions that are frequently overlooked in conventional evaluations: uncertainty quantification and calibration. Integrated as core evaluation axes throughout the entire framework, they directly address the stringent safety and risk-stratification requirements of clinical decision-support systems. Calibration is conceptualized in this framework as a task-dependent core evaluation dimension. Its primary function is to assess the concordance between model-output probabilities and actual event frequencies, making it indispensable for probabilistic prediction models, risk-stratification systems, and triage tools. In Phase 1, calibration evaluates the reliability and cross-subgroup stability of output probabilities, quantified by standard metrics such as reliability plots, Brier scores, and ECE64,153. In Phase 2, the focus shifts to calibration stability and drift within real-world clinical data streams. By Phase 3, calibration extends beyond a mere statistical property of the model; it directly dictates the recalibration of clinician trust, human–AI confidence alignment, and the ability to mitigate automation bias. Entering Phase 4, calibration evaluation formally transitions toward clinical utility. Here, DCA is explicitly incorporated to quantitatively assess whether a calibrated model provides genuine net clinical benefit across varying decision thresholds41. In Phase 5, calibration drift serves as a primary early-warning signal for active post-market surveillance and triggers PCCPs145. Notably, calibration is not a mandatory primary endpoint for pure detection, segmentation, or retrieval tasks, as these systems can still demonstrate clinical value by improving diagnostic efficiency or optimizing workflows despite limited probability calibration. Uncertainty characterization may serve as a potential safety mechanism; however, its ability to indicate prediction unreliability or detect out-of-distribution cases is not guaranteed and must itself be empirically validated in the intended clinical context. In Phase 1, uncertainty evaluation should assess whether uncertainty estimates appropriately identify prediction unreliability, including heterogeneous data, edge cases, and out‑of‑distribution scenarios, and should explicitly validate the performance of uncertainty estimation rather than assuming its reliability. Phase 2 further evaluates the stability and reliability of uncertainty estimates under noisy real-world data streams, including their ability to detect distribution shifts without generating excessive false alarms154,155. In Phase 3, uncertainty display (e.g., confidence intervals or warning prompts) should be evaluated as a human-factor intervention rather than assumed to improve decision-making. Evaluation should examine how clinicians interpret and respond to uncertainty information, including its effects on confidence calibration, reliance behavior, automation bias, and diagnostic performance156,157. In Phase 4, appropriately validated uncertainty estimates may support escalation strategies and conservative decision-making for high-risk cases155,156. Finally, in Phase 5, anomalous fluctuations in predictive uncertainty may provide early signals of distribution shifts or fairness degradation and should be monitored alongside overall model performance to inform lifecycle re-evaluation and dynamic recalibration145,154. Discussion

Recent guidelines, including FUTURE-AI, SALIENT, CANAIRI, and the FDA GMLP, have laid a vital foundation for medical AI, though they primarily operate at conceptual, organizational, or regulatory levels. For example, FUTURE-AI offers 30 best practices built on six core principles for trustworthy AI32; SALIENT maps out end-to-end implementation tasks linked to reporting standards60; CANAIRI expands traditional silent trials into broader sociotechnical translational trials48; and the FDA GMLP outlines ten high-level principles for machine learning development109. However, these works generally stop short of providing prescriptive guidance on selecting specific study designs, estimating sample sizes, and methodologically handling continuous algorithmic iteration. As detailed in Supplementary Table 3, our proposed framework bridges the gap between these foundational principles and actual clinical execution by serving as a methodological blueprint. It advances the field by systematically mapping 18 actionable study designs across a five-phase clinical evaluation gradient. Furthermore, rather than treating evaluation as a static, pre-deployment hurdle, our approach incorporates a dynamic evaluation architecture. This allows researchers to methodologically accommodate the continuous iteration of AI models, directly aligning evidence generation with emerging regulatory mechanisms like PCCPs. Beyond medical-AI-specific guidelines, several broader machine learning and maturity-model frameworks also inform the development and deployment of AI systems. CRISP-ML(Q) provides a structured process model for ML quality management158, while OPTICA focuses on operational readiness and organizational workflows for AI deployment19. Maturity‑level frameworks such as TRL, MDRL, and ML‑TRL assess system readiness from engineering or regulatory perspectives20,159, but they do not define validation pathways specific to adaptive, data‑driven clinical AI systems. Moreover, these frameworks do not specify how medical AI should progress through clinically grounded, evidence‑based validation phases, nor do they offer prescriptive guidance on study‑design selection, sample‑size estimation, multi‑center heterogeneity, or dynamic model iteration. Importantly, none of these frameworks articulate concrete fall-back pathways or re-evaluation mechanisms when predefined criteria are not met. In contrast, our framework introduces an explicit phase-gating structure with clearly defined success criteria and both local and systemic fall-back triggers (Table 3). This structured re-entry mechanism, absent from existing frameworks, provides an auditable pathway for returning to earlier phases when performance degradation, safety signals, or fairness drift emerge, thereby supporting lifecycle-long safety assurance for continuously evolving medical AI systems. Clinical evaluation of medical AI is undergoing a profound paradigm shift, from a narrow “performance‑metric competition” to a system‑level model of lifecycle governance. Building on the five‑phase evaluation framework and its overarching dynamic evaluation architecture developed in this study, we argue that future evaluations should prioritize six core domains. Evaluating medical AI requires distinguishing between a constant diagnostic ground truth and evolving phase-specific evaluation endpoints (Supplementary Table 5)16,112,113. To validly track core algorithm performance (e.g., discrimination, calibration) across phases, the reference standard must consistently rely on biopsy-proven pathology or rigorously re-adjudicated senior-expert consensus, expressly avoiding unverified historical clinical labels160. This constant diagnostic ground truth underpins Phase 1, Phase 3, and the diagnostic performance evaluations within Phases 2 and 4. However, the overarching evaluation targets expand across the lifecycle. While Phases 1 and 3 strictly assess diagnostic accuracy against this constant truth, Phase 2 introduces additional operational metrics by comparing AI outputs to real-time clinical judgments. Furthermore, Phase 4 expands the evaluation focus to tangible patient outcomes in clinical-effectiveness RCTs161, and Phase 5 shifts to evaluating post‑market performance and system‑level effectiveness. Consequently, valid cross-phase comparisons demand strict construct alignment. For instance, tracking calibration drift from Phase 1 to Phase 2 is valid only when calculated against the identical pathology/expert ground truth. Conversely, it is methodologically invalid to equate high operational agreement with clinicians in Phase 2 with actual patient benefit in Phase 422. The focus must shift from validating performance ceilings to identifying failure boundaries. Medical AI introduces complex forms of bias, such as spectrum bias, data drift, and automation bias22,162, that extend beyond the scope of traditional diagnostic‑test evaluation. These biases evolve across validation stages, manifesting as selective data bias in early phases and transforming into systemic bias after real‑world deployment163. Stress testing and pitfall‑case evaluation can proactively reveal performance degradation under extreme or atypical conditions, enabling a pharmacovigilance‑like monitoring loop that safeguards safety across the AI lifecycle. Evaluation must move from measuring AI performance to assessing the performance of the human–AI system. AI now functions as a multi‑role partner within clinical workflows, a parallel assistant, second reader, triage tool, or quasi‑autonomous decision system67,115,164. Traditional metrics such as AUC cannot capture dimensions such as workflow efficiency, missed‑case recovery, or intervention delay. A core challenge is cognitive coupling: clinicians’ decision behavior, trust calibration, and vigilance may be reshaped by AI over time67,165,166. Future evaluation should dissect these interaction dynamics, address risks of long‑term de‑skilling, and incorporate clinician satisfaction and cognitive load as core outcomes to support a transparent and adaptive human–AI ecosystem. Evaluation must transition from snapshot validation to traceable, dynamically updated evidence. The fixed‑intervention logic is structurally misaligned with the continuous iteration of AI systems141. Dynamic evaluation architecture is therefore critical to bridging the gap between evidence‑generation cycles and algorithm‑evolution speed. Regulatory science is also shifting: frameworks such as the PCCPs signal a move from fixed‑version confirmation to closed‑loop dynamic governance145. Meanwhile, to address the “black‑box” nature of AI, evaluation must incorporate quantitative assessment of explainability to ensure alignment with clinical reasoning, and enforce fairness auditing as a baseline requirement for safeguarding equity167. Consistent with this dynamic perspective, evidence generation itself is not confined to a linear sequence of phases. Implementation research and sociotechnical context scoping may begin well before Phase 4 randomized trials, while real‑world system‑level insights from Phase 5 can iteratively refine earlier-phase simulation parameters, workflow assumptions, and trial designs168. This legitimate parallelism and bidirectional feedback ensure that evidence generation remains adaptive, context-aware, and aligned with real-world deployment conditions. The proposed five-phase evaluation framework operationalizes key dimensions of ethical AI governance and provides a practical pathway for meeting global regulatory expectations. Its phase-gating criteria and fall-back triggers directly implement core principles emphasized in international standards such as the OECD AI Principles24 and the EU AI Act25, including robustness, fairness, transparency, human oversight, and lifecycle risk management. Calibration stability, uncertainty characterization, subgroup robustness, and safety-signal detection are embedded as mandatory evidence domains across phases, ensuring that ethical and safety considerations are evaluated continuously rather than treated as static pre-deployment requirements109. The dynamic re-entry mechanism further aligns with emerging legal requirements for continuous monitoring, post-market surveillance, and controlled model updates for adaptive AI systems145. By integrating these ethical and regulatory expectations into a structured, auditable evaluation gradient, the framework enables developers and regulators to generate the evidence needed to demonstrate compliance throughout the AI lifecycle. Evaluation must extend beyond demonstrating clinical efficacy to establishing economic viability. Regulatory clearance and clinical effectiveness alone are no longer sufficient for large‑scale adoption of medical AI; most HTA bodies now require evidence on cost‑effectiveness, budget impact169, and value‑of‑information170 before approving widespread deployment or public reimbursement171. In our framework, these health‑economic components are incorporated within the later phases (Phases 4 and 5) as essential elements of real‑world implementation and real‑world integration. However, full economic modeling depends on highly specific AI applications, local cost structures, and regional clinical pathways172. Accordingly, the present manuscript focuses on establishing the overarching conceptual and methodological architecture rather than performing application‑specific economic simulations. Future applications of this framework to concrete AI systems will need to integrate these HTA elements to bridge the gap between clinical evidence generation and institutional procurement171. Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, as a conceptual framework, its generalizability and operational feasibility require empirical validation across diverse clinical domains, data ecosystems, and healthcare systems. Second, the phase-gating thresholds, fall-back triggers, and dynamic re-entry mechanisms are grounded in theoretical reasoning and emerging regulatory trends, but lack quantitative calibration across institutions and real-world settings. Third, the framework is primarily oriented toward diagnostic and prognostic SaMD-type systems; further adaptation is needed for generative models, continuously learning algorithms, and non-diagnostic AI applications such as workflow optimization tools. Fourth, the reproducibility and standardization of human–AI interaction assessments (Phase 3) across heterogeneous clinical environments remain to be established through both experimental and real-world studies. Finally, while the framework integrates major ethical and regulatory governance directives, it does not fully encompass the broader macro-economic, organizational, and fine-grained sociotechnical factors that shape practical AI deployment, which future research should integrate more comprehensively. Clinical evaluation of medical AI is shifting from fragmented technical validation to a system‑level model of lifecycle governance. Using a five-phase evaluation framework and dynamic evaluation architecture encompassing eight core methods and 18 study designs, this study maps the evidence‑generation pathway from algorithmic cold start to deep clinical integration, emphasizing that evaluation is not a single entry‑point test but a staged, co‑evolving evidence system shaped by context, actors, and technology (Table 4). Beyond proposing a structured gradient framework, this study articulates the ‘dilution mechanism’ of algorithmic efficacy in real-world settings through a tripartite framework of “physical, consensus, and functional truth”. Furthermore, by incorporating adaptive design, the framework provides a potential pathway to reconcile evidence generation with continuous model evolution. Finally, the value of medical AI lies not in constructing a flawless “digital oracle”, but in building a safe, fair, trustworthy, and continuously evolving human–AI system. Methods

This study developed the five-phase evaluation framework and dynamic lifecycle architecture through an iterative conceptual-modeling process. The framework was refined by integrating insights from methodological literature, existing AI evaluation guidelines, regulatory documents, and established principles from diagnostic test research, clinical-trial methodology, software-engineering robustness testing, and human–AI interaction studies. Concepts identified from these sources were organized into coherent evaluation structure, which informed the definition of phase objectives, appropriate study designs, and evidence requirements. Illustrative phase-gating criteria and fall-back triggers were developed by synthesizing principles from lifecycle risk management and safety-critical system monitoring. The overall structure and terminology were iteratively reviewed to ensure methodological clarity, operational feasibility, and alignment with emerging regulatory expectations for adaptive medical AI systems.
